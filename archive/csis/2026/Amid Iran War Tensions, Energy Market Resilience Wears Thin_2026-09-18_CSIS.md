# 伊朗战争紧张局势下能源市场韧性承压

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

CSIS把中东能源基础设施遭袭与霍尔木兹海峡安全安排、能源价格和国内政治压力联系起来。文章判断，冲突若持续，地区国家可能寻求一种不再完全依赖美国的安全协调机制。其论据集中在能源设施受损、运输风险和高成本战争对选民可负担性的叠加影响。作者的态度偏向风险预警，认为能源韧性既取决于军事保护，也取决于供应多元化和地区制度安排。材料没有证明新的安全安排必然形成，因而应将其视为情景判断而非对政策结果的确定预测。

### 建议

政策含义：将能源设施防护、供应多元化和地区安全协调作为相互关联的韧性议程。

Amid Iran War Tensions, Energy Market Resilience Wears Thin Photo: Anton Petrus/Getty Images Commentary by Masha Kotkin Published September 18, 2026 Energy security continues to erode as a result of the Iran war—now in its seventh month—with fresh attacks causing more damage to infrastructure and assets in the region. In the past week, new attacks on the East-West Pipeline in Saudi Arabia and Houthi advances near the Bab al-Mandab Strait signal an escalation that could further strain oil markets and challenge the global economy. In the past week, the price of crude oil climbed to $109 per barrel following additional strikes in the region. Iran attacked 10 tankers near the strait; the United States destroyed five Iranian tankers in retaliation; and Iran responded by targeting U.S. military assets in Jordan and Kuwait. Meanwhile, the Houthis, an Iranian-backed militia, began advancing on the Red Sea coast, claiming control over Perim Island and the Hanish Islands and tightening their grip on the Bab al-Mandab Strait. The waterway provides a narrow path between the Red Sea and the Gulf of Aden that has thus far allowed Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates to continue exporting crude oil by sea during the conflict. Earlier last week, the Houthis also attacked the southern Saudi Arabian cities of Abha, Jazan, Najran, and Khamis Mushait. These attacks injured 73 people and further damaged a 400,000-barrel-per-day refining facility , which was also a target of two Houthi attacks in July and August . Furthermore, satellite images show projectile damage from an Iranian attack last week to pumping stations next to the Yanbu (East-West) Pipeline, a key asset used by Saudi Arabia to redirect its crude exports during the last six months of the conflict. Repairs to the damaged pumping stations could take up to six weeks, threatening to disrupt crude oil supply to global markets. At the time of the attack, crude inventories at Yanbu Port, on the western end of the pipeline, stood at around 9 million barrels , or two to three days of supply. Reserves of Saudi crude stored in Egypt may also supply customers for another week. However, lengthy repairs to damaged Saudi energy infrastructure may result in a further production slump, from an already historic low of 6.2 million barrels per day of crude production reported by the country in August. News of damage to multiple pumping stations on the Yanbu pipeline pushed the price of crude to $109 per barrel . Exports of oil and gas from Gulf producers have declined due to repeated war-related damage to infrastructure and shipping constraints: Saudi Arabia reported to the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) that its production of crude oil slumped to levels not seen since the 1990s, and Qatar succeeded in shipping just 18 liquefied natural gas (LNG) cargoes during the first six months of the conflict, compared to 509 cargoes during the same period in 2025. To supply customers, Saudi Arabia and Qatar have respectively resorted to time-consuming and expensive ship-to-ship transfers of oil and LNG. The challenges of delivering oil and gas to markets began to manifest in financial markets in the region and beyond. Conflict-related budgetary shortfalls in Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries also drove Saudi Arabia to borrow externally , Qatar to reduce operating expenses by 30 percent , and Kuwait to borrow from its sovereign wealth fund . The European Central Bank , the U.S Federal Reserve , and the Bank of Japan have all raised interest rates by 25 basis points, citing inflationary pressures as a result of the conflict with Iran. Borrowing for businesses and consumers will therefore become more expensive, dampening business expansion plans and purchases fueling economic growth. Economic pressures on regional actors continue to grow, driving them to take the lead at the negotiating table with Iran: The deal on the Strait of Hormuz—which Iran and GCC countries planned to discuss on Monday, September 14 before talks were delayed due to the Yanbu Pipeline attack—potentially represents the first attempt of lateral states to build a post-U.S. security arrangement for the strait. Despite the delay in negotiations, the current escalation could incentivize the region to embrace a settlement with Iran that might not include U.S. security and economic priorities. The conflict started as a preemptive strike by Israel and the United States on Iran to prevent it from obtaining nuclear capabilities, but it has turned into a crisis management exercise to stabilize global energy markets. The last two weeks show that the longer the conflict lasts, the worse the potential damage may be to infrastructure, the global energy trade, and affordable energy. U.S. allies in the region will likely seek to cut a deal with Iran to ensure their own economic security. Interest rate hikes and protests over energy prices also exhibit broader economic strain outside of the immediate conflict. When Americans vote in November, affordability and the state of the economy—and the war’s impacts—will be top of mind.
