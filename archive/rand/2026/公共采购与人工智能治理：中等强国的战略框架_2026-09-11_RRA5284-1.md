# 公共采购与人工智能治理：中等强国的战略框架

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

RAND把“AI中等强国”界定为具备经济、外交、安全或监管能力，却依赖美中前沿模型供应商的国家，并分析公共采购能否成为治理这种依赖的工具。报告识别三类由政府买方承担的突出风险：供应商锁定与断供、无法独立评估模型能力，以及数据主权和信息安全问题。作者提出在合同中争取模型评估访问、信息共享、可靠性研究投入、先进风险管理、退出与终止条款，以及支持本国和盟友能力的义务。报告同时强调采购能力有边界：政府尚非不可替代的大客户，外国供应商仍受本国国家安全法约束，买方与头部企业之间的信息不对称也难以消除；其结论因此是通过多国协调提高议价能力，而非把采购视为万能监管。

### 建议

短期应共同界定风险、选择G7、欧盟、北约或AI安全研究所网络等协调载体，并让较小国家汇聚需求；中长期应建设可替换供应商所需的本国或盟友算力、安全托管、多模型选择、政府技术人才及第三方评估能力。

## English Source Material

This report assesses public procurement as an AI governance tool, develops contractual guarantees for state buyers, and examines whether coordination among AI middle powers could strengthen bargaining leverage, evaluation capacity and resilience to AI crises. Public procurement and AI governance Developing a strategic framework for middle powers Afek Shamir Research Published Sep 11, 2026 Download PDF Share on LinkedIn Share on X Share on Facebook Email Frontier AI capabilities are concentrated among a small number of private firms headquartered in the United States and China. For states which neither develop nor host leading models, this concentration creates a dependence on foreign AI providers whose capabilities they often cannot independently evaluate and whose technology may be constrained by the laws of the provider’s home jurisdiction. Public procurement offers ‘AI middle powers’ one mechanism for governing these dependencies. These are states with significant economic, diplomatic, security or regulatory capabilities that nevertheless depend technologically on foreign frontier AI firms. The report finds that three categories of risk fall disproportionately on state buyers in AI middle powers : vendor lock-in and the threat of service withdrawal, limited visibility into frontier AI development, and data sovereignty and information-security risks. To mitigate these risks, the report develops a framework of procurement obligations that governments could seek to negotiate with AI providers. These include model access for evaluation, information sharing, funding for reliability research, state-of-the-art risk management requirements, termination and exit provisions, and measures to support domestic and allied capability. The analysis finds that public procurement is a useful but bounded mechanism for governing frontier AI. Governments are not yet major customers of frontier AI firms, and leading developers may be able to forego government contracts rather than accept costly requirements. Procurement contracts with foreign providers may also remain subordinate to the national-security laws of a vendor’s home state, and state buyers can face persistent information asymmetries when negotiating with leading technology companies. At the same time, government demand for frontier AI is growing, and public-sector contracts offer firms more than revenue alone. They provide financial predictability and a degree of prestige and regulatory validation, making governments strategically important customers even if they are not yet indispensable to firms’ business models. The central recommendation is that AI middle powers should assess whether collective coordination on procurement requirements can strengthen their bargaining position and state capacity. Key Takeaways Short-term actions (actions that could be initiated within 12 months): Firstly, governments should begin with a shared assessment of the risks that procurement requirements are intended to address. Participating states should compare the risks they associate with different government uses of frontier AI, including but not limited to dependence on individual foreign providers, loss of access, insufficient visibility into model behaviour, and requirements for data control. Common procurement provisions should follow predominantly where separate governments assess risks similarly. Secondly, governments should assess the most suitable institutional arrangement for procurement coordination. The G7, the EU, NATO, and International Network of AI Safety Institutes already provide mechanisms for cooperation on AI policy, standards and technical evaluation. A dedicated arrangement may be justified where no current institution combines sufficient purchasing relevance and technical capacity. Thirdly, smaller middle powers which lack state access to frontier AI should explore opportunities to pool demand with partners and strengthen their negotiating position. Where an individual government is unlikely to represent sufficient demand to influence a frontier AI provider, cooperation with other buyers could increase the commercial significance of their purchasing preferences and requirements. Medium- to long-term actions (actions likely to require approximately 1–5 years or longer): Firstly, governments should identify which AI capabilities need to remain available independently of any single foreign provider and invest selectively in the infrastructure required to preserve them. For most middle powers, this is unlikely to mean reproducing the full frontier AI stack nationally. A more realistic strategy could include building a meaningful share of domestic or allied compute, creating secure hosting facilities, securing access to multiple model families for optionality reasons, and bringing in technical talent to government with expertise in fine-tuning and the know-how to migrate critical workloads. These assets would also strengthen bargaining power by making the future option of switching providers more credible. Secondly, governments should strengthen their capacity to evaluate and assure frontier AI systems. This includes developing assurance and technical AI expertise within defence agencies and supporting the creation of a mature third-party evaluation and assurance sector. These capabilities are necessary to make procurement guarantees practically enforceable. Thirdly, procurement coordination could provide a basis for wider cooperation on AI incidents and resilience to AI-related failures. Governments that establish common procurement requirements would have an existing mechanism for sharing information with one another and with providers. Over time, this could support common approaches to incident reporting and exercises for responding to significant AI-related incidents.
