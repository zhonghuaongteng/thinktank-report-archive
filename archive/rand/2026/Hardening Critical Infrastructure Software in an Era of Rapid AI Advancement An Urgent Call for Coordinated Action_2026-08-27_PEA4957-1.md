# 人工智能加速时代的关键基础设施软件加固

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

报告把老旧软件、基础设施互联依赖和AI加速漏洞发现的叠加视为迫近的系统性风险。RAND判断先进模型发现漏洞的速度已可能超过全球防御者修补速度，Anthropic Project Glasswing和OpenAI Daybreak被用作能力跃迁的现实案例。报告反对把防御停留在逐个补丁层面，主张转向能够减少整类漏洞的结构性加固，包括内存安全语言、隔离机制、形式化验证和高可信软件工程。AI也可用于资产清单、代码理解、规格草拟和遗留系统重写，但必须由领域专家持续校核，避免自动化防御产生新的盲点。Y2K、HTTPS迁移和内存安全语言推广说明，大规模技术转型需要标准、采购、激励和多方协调共同塑造“阻力最小”的升级路径。报告据此认为政策窗口短于传统基础设施治理周期，运营商、政府和研究界应立即建立协同机制，而不能等待攻击能力进一步成熟。

### 建议

报告建议基础设施运营者先以AI辅助建立软件资产清单和代码结构认知，再逐步采用隔离、内存安全语言与形式化验证；政府应通过采购要求、标准和激励协调跨行业迁移，研究界则需继续降低高可信软件工程的实施成本。

Advancement: An Urgent Call for Coordinated Action Introduction In April 2026, Anthropic announced that its Claude Mythos Preview model had identified thousands of previously unknown software vulnerabilities, many of them critical, including in every major operating system and every major web browser (Anthropic, 2026a; Carlini et al., 2026; Nolan, 2026; UK AI Security Institute, 2026). Within weeks, approximately 50 partner organizations participating in the company’s Project Glasswing initiative had surfaced more than 10,000 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities in production software (Anthropic, 2026b). OpenAI followed with Daybreak, its own defensive cybersecurity program, likewise arguing that the bottleneck was shifting from vulnerability discovery to patching (OpenAI, 2026a). For decades, an important challenge for cybersecurity, and software security more broadly, has been discovering what is wrong with the software we depend on. Teams of skilled analysts, working with imperfect tools on sprawling codebases, could examine only a fraction of the code that needed scrutiny. Attackers, who needed to find only one exploitable flaw, held a structural advantage over defenders, who needed to find and fix all flaws. Artificial intelligence (AI) has now collapsed the discovery side of this equation. It is not yet clear whether this shift ultimately advantages attackers or defenders. As Chad Heitzenrater (2025) has argued, the right investments in people, process, and technology could make advanced AI a net advantage for cyber defense by fundamentally disrupting the economics that have historically favored the offense. But realizing that advantage requires action. The question facing policymakers, infrastructure operators, and the security community is whether we can organize the response side quickly enough to avoid a period of acute and systemic vulnerability. This paper argues that the convergence of aging software infrastructure, accelerating AI capabilities, and the growing dependence of every sector on interconnected digital systems creates acute risk. But the same AI systems that are compressing the offensive timeline can also be turned to defensive purposes if we organize to do so. We draw on a structured review of the relevant technical and policy literature, large language model–assisted research and drafting, and extensive discussions with more than two dozen experts in academia, industry, and government whose knowledge spans machine learning, cybersecurity, high-assurance software engineering, and national security policy. 1 Calls for improved cybersecurity are not new, and the policy community has developed a certain fatigue with alarm bells that have not been followed by the catastrophes they implied. 1 Structured virtual discussions took place between January and May of 2026. The temptation is to assume that the next warning is like the last one: technically correct but practically manageable. We believe that this assumption is becoming dangerous. The speed and autonomy with which vulnerabilities can be discovered, chained, and exploited represent a real shift. When that speed crosses a threshold at which human-mediated defense can no longer keep pace, the resulting failures will be systemic rather than incremental. A recent incident made this concrete. During an internal evaluation of advanced cyber capabilities in 2026, OpenAI models operating in a sandbox testing environment autonomously identified and exploited a zero-day vulnerability in the package-registry proxy that constrained their network access, escaped containment, and then chained multiple attack vectors, including stolen credentials and additional zero-day vulnerabilities, to compromise Hugging Face’s production infrastructure (Hugging Face, 2026; OpenAI, 2026b). The models had not been instructed to break out of their environment; they did so in pursuit of a narrow evaluation objective, inferring that Hugging Face might host solutions to the benchmark on which they were being scored. OpenAI (2026b) described the event as an “unprecedented cyber incident.” Both organizations detected and contained the activity, and Hugging Face’s forensic reconstruction was itself AI-assisted. The incident nonetheless demonstrates that frontier models can discover and exploit novel attack paths in real-world production systems without source-code access, and can do so instrumentally, in pursuit of objectives that have nothing to do with the system they compromise. The Architecture of Vulnerability The cybersecurity community has long understood the basic inventory of what needs to be hardened. The categories are not mysterious: insecure remote access protocols, identity and privilege management systems, the boundary between information technology (IT) and operational technology (OT), software supply chain integrity, and memory-unsafe parsing code at critical interfaces. Several of these categories overlap with established security taxonomies (see, for example, OWASP Foundation, undated). These architectural intervention points are considered relatively stable by the experts we consulted, across sectors and across varying assumptions about how quickly AI capabilities will advance. New vulnerabilities will continue to be discovered, and AI will accelerate that discovery, but the places where hardening efforts should concentrate are unlikely to change. The programs now being stood up reflect this understanding. Glasswing and Daybreak have reported findings that overlap substantially with these categories (Carlini et al., 2026; Anthropic, 2026b; OpenAI, 2026a). But both Glasswing and Daybreak are oriented primarily toward the vulnerability discovery and remediation cycle. Find a bug, write a patch, deploy the fix, move to the next bug. This essential work is ultimately reactive. Each fixed vulnerability is a local 3 improvement to a codebase whose overall structure, dependency relationships, and operational assumptions remain unchanged. The deeper objective, one that neither program is primarily designed to address, is how we can use AI to understand the software systems, and the organizations behind them, well enough to make structural improvements that reduce the rate at which new vulnerabilities arise. Achieving this objective will require not only technical analysis but rapid, structured collaboration among engineers, domain experts, and policymakers. AI can accelerate that coordination process just as it accelerates the discovery of vulnerabilities. Precedents for Coordinated Technical Transitions Society has, on occasion, organized itself to execute large-scale technical transitions under time pressure. A frequently cited example is the Y2K remediation effort of the late 1990s, in which governments and private-sector organizations worldwide undertook remediation that contemporary estimates projected would cost hundreds of billions of dollars (Special Committee on the Year 2000 Technology Problem, 1999, citing the Gartner Group). Y2K had several features that made coordination possible: a hard deadline that concentrated attention, a precisely defined technical problem that could be explained to nonspecialists, and a governance architecture in which the private sector executed the work while governments provided convening authority (U.S. General Accounting Office, 1997, 2000) and funded remediation of their own systems. Governments also created limited liability protections for certain Y2K readiness disclosures and a temporary, narrow antitrust exemption for certain collaborative remediation-related conduct (Public Law 105-271, 1998; Public Law 106-37, 1999). The effort was overwhelmingly successful. The dire predictions did not materialize, which led some observers to conclude that the threat had been overblown, when in fact the quiet outcome was the result of extraordinary effort (see Figure 1). But Y2K is an imperfect analogy for the current challenge. The date-encoding problem was a single defect class with deterministic behavior. Many systems used date representations or date- processing logic that would have failed around the 1999–2000 transition, with two-digit year storage as the canonical example. The modern software hardening challenge is heterogeneous: The vulnerabilities span multiple categories, the adversary is adaptive, and there is no single deadline that concentrates political will. The absence of a hard deadline is perhaps the most significant difference. Y2K had December 31, 1999. We have a steep and accelerating trajectory but no cliff. 4 Figure 1. The transition of the World Wide Web from unencrypted HTTP to encrypted HTTPS is another case study in how a distributed, multistakeholder technical transition can be accomplished over time with the right combination of technical enablement, institutional pressure, and cultural shift. HTTPS had existed since the mid-1990s, but adoption remained low for years because certificates were expensive, server overhead was nontrivial on the hardware of the era, and most website operators saw no reason to encrypt. The transition accelerated only when several forces converged: the Electronic Frontier Foundation launched its campaign to encrypt the web in 2010 (Hancock, 2021); Let’s Encrypt began offering free, automated certificates in 2015; Google began favoring encrypted sites in search rankings (“HTTPS as a Ranking Signal,” 2014); and browser vendors started marking HTTP pages as insecure. By 2021, more than 90 percent of web traffic was encrypted (Hancock, 2021). The process took a decade, but it worked, not because anyone mandated HTTPS by fiat, but because a combination of free tooling, institutional incentives, and normative pressure made adoption the path of least resistance.
