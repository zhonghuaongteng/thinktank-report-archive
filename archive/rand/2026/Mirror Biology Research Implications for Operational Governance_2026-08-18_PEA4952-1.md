# Mirror Biology Research: Implications for Operational Governance

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

RAND指出，镜像生物学已被识别为潜在高后果风险，但国际社会尚缺少可实际执行的协同治理安排。报告依据研究活动距离可自我复制镜像细胞的技术里程碑，将研究划分为六类，并把治理强度设置为社区自律、机构与资助方监督、监管和供应链控制、禁止四级。其核心原则是风险越接近完整复制能力，责任主体越应从研究共同体外移至国家监管和国际协调层级。作者强调，分类只描述技术活动与关键屏障的关系，具体治理强度仍需结合潜在收益、可替代路径和证据变化校准。报告也承认天然手性研究、检测工具和既有镜像组件存在边界问题，过宽控制可能抑制有益研究，过窄控制则会留下能力迁移通道。该框架被定位为启动操作性治理讨论的基础，后续仍需完善重分类规则、供应商义务、资助与出版条件以及国际协作机制。

## English Source Material

A dvances in synthetic biology in the past few years have raised the possibil- ity of creating mirror life: These are self-replicating cells built from molec- ular components that are mirror images of those found in natural life (Adamala, Agashe, Binder, et al., 2024; Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024). Mirror life does not yet exist, and its creation is widely assessed to be at least a decade away. But the trajectory of synthetic biology suggests that the key techni- cal barriers to its creation may erode over time, and a growing number of scientific bodies, governments, and multilateral forums have concluded that the prospect of mirror life is serious enough to warrant governance before the capability matures. Over the past 18 months, declaratory recognition of mirror biology as a gov- ernance challenge has accumulated rapidly while operational oversight through funding conditions, licensing regimes, publication norms, and treaty mechanisms has not yet begun to take shape. In our assessment, the gap between the growing recognition of mirror biology as a governance challenge and the establishment of operational oversight mechanisms is a pressing deficit. Why Prevention Must Operate Upstream The consequences of creating mirror life have been hypoth- esized to be of catastrophic concern to natural ecological systems. Expert assessment holds that mirror bacteria, if created and released, could evade many of the immune mechanisms that humans, animals, and plants rely on to recognize pathogens, and thus these mirror bacteria could evade the predators and phages that keep natural bacterial populations in check; such bacteria could therefore spread as an invasive species, cause lethal infections across many host species, and irreversibly disrupt ecosystems (Adamala, Agashe, Binder, et al., 2024; Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024). The World Health Organization (WHO) frames the hazard as extending to “the health of humans, animals, plants, or the environment” and notes that exist- ing countermeasures would be badly limited: Most exist- ing antibiotics act on a specific molecular handedness and would not bind to mirror targets, and no vaccine has been developed against an entirely novel, mirror-image pathogen (WHO, 2026). Some countermeasures could, in principle, be developed: Certain achiral or racemic antibiotics might have activity, and conjugate vaccines are conceivable, but none would protect species outside direct human care, and none have been demonstrated (Kalra et al., 2026). These consequences are the reason the subject merits attention. Because there would be no reliable countermeasure after release, governance cannot fall back on response and reme- diation in the way that environmental and biosafety regula- tion does. The point of intervention must move upstream to the creation of a mirror cell itself: i.e., prevention over miti- gation and the research that would enable it. This is a differ- ent posture from the safety regulations and the prohibition regimes that exist for biological and chemical weapons. Why Mirror Biology Is Hard to Govern Mirror biology poses governance challenges that differ in important ways from the challenges associated with con- ventional synthetic biology. Mirror life may not emerge from a single, discrete breakthrough. It would likely arise through incremental advances across synthetic biology research, such as chemical synthesis of proteins, ribosome engineering, cell-free transcription and translation, and in vitro genome assembly, many of which are benign in 3 isolation. These findings are reflected in recent research conducted by the authors of this paper. That research will be presented in a companion report that was under review at the time of this paper’s publication. A second difficulty is that the field of mirror biology is a continuum with two ends and a great deal in between. At one end are individual mirror molecules, such as mirror peptides, mirror proteins, and mirror nucleic acids, which have beneficial applications in therapeutics, diagnostics, and novel materials, some of which have already been established clinically and commercially. At the other end is mirror life itself: self-replicating mirror cells, whose cre- ation carries the risks described previously. Between these two extremes sits research aimed at constructing increas- ingly complex biological systems out of mirror biomol- ecules, some of which could eventually become the major components of a mirror cell, such as a mirror genome or a mirror ribosome. The work in the middle of this con- tinuum is not dangerous in itself, but its potential to enable the creation of mirror life is a significant concern. Dual-use research of concern (DURC) is the established category for research that has genuine beneficial applica- tions but could also be directly misused to cause harm. However, research along the pathway to mirror life does not fit this definition at any point. Although mirror life’s precursors enable the ultimate creation of a mirror organ- ism, such examples as mirror molecules, synthesis meth- ods, and individual components carry no direct risk of harm in themselves. The same is true for every milestone on the pathway to a mirror cell; a mirror genome, mirror transcription-translation system, or other components of a mirror cell are not immediately harmful by themselves. The end state—a mirror cell—could be acutely dangerous, but it fails the DURC test from the other side: Its risks far outweigh any plausible benefit (Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024; Imperiale and Casadevall, 2026), and the ques- tion is not how to manage the misuse of a capability but whether the capability should exist at all. Governance regimes for mirror life will incorporate some aspects that resemble the governance of DURC in that the beneficial value of various precursor steps will need to be weighed against these steps’ ultimate contribution to enabling the creation of mirror life. However, a standard DURC framing is unhelpful: not because the work is safe but because nei- ther the harmless precursors nor the catastrophic end state is the kind of thing DURC was built to govern. Separately, mirror biology resists the timing that con- ventional oversight relies on. Early intervention runs the risk of overreach and scientific backlash; delayed action might foreclose meaningful preventive options once the capabil- ity has advanced (Epstein, Crawford, and Nevo, 2025; Kalra et al., 2026). Regulatory approaches that wait for clear risk signals or mature capabilities are poorly suited to research that lowers the barriers to mirror life only in aggregate and only eventually, which is what makes a standing, gated framework—the kind described herein, set in advance, and applied by degree—the more workable posture. The Governance Landscape The governance landscape for mirror biology, though still nascent, is evolving quickly. Since the December 2024 Sci - ence article calling for the global community to ensure mirror life is not created (Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024), a series of statements has emerged. Philan- thropic funders, including the Alfred P. Sloan Foundation 4 and Renaissance Philanthropy, have stated that they will not fund research aimed at creating mirror organisms (Alfred P. Sloan Foundation, undated; Renaissance Phi- lanthropy, 2025). In early 2026, the United Kingdom’s UK Research and Innovation (UKRI) introduced an enhanced dual-use scrutiny requirement—extended in June 2026 into a joint policy statement covering UKRI as a whole—which includes mirror biology, among other concerns (UKRI, 2026). In July 2026, the U.S. Office of Science and Tech- nology Policy issued a policy that prohibits the federal government from funding research to create mirror organ- isms (Office of Science and Technology Policy, 2026). The United Nations (UN) Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO)’s International Bioethics Com- mittee recommended a precautionary global moratorium on the creation of mirror organisms (International Bioeth- ics Committee, 2025). In 2025, Germany’s Central Com- mission for Biological Safety (2025) warned that mirror organisms could pose serious risks and called for broad scientific and societal debate. In March 2026, the UN Secretary-General’s Scientific Advisory Board published a brief on mirror life, which warns that risk grows sharply as research approaches critical milestones on the path to mirror life; the brief also warns that the dangers posed by a replicating mirror cell would be extraordinarily high. The board also noted that a growing number of scientists and experts have called for proactive multilateral action to establish clear red lines well before mirror life becomes fea- sible (Secretary-General’s Scientific Advisory Board, 2026). The WHO has issued a fact sheet recognizing that the cre- ation of mirror life “could plausibly result in catastrophic harm to humans, animals, plants and the environment” (WHO, 2026). The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists cited mirror life as a factor that is pushing the 2026 Doomsday Clock closer than ever to midnight (Mecklin, 2026). This cluster of activity is primarily declaratory. It sig- nals a broadening consensus that mirror life should not be created and that mirror biology should be governed. What it does not yet include is consensus on what form(s) governance of mirror life should take, including an opera- tional mechanism for establishing boundaries of accept- able research, how oversight should be structured, or who should bear primary responsibility. Some observers view existing biosafety and dual-use frameworks as adaptable to mirror biology (Zhu, 2025). Others sketch a framework of decision points along the path toward mirror life; at some of those points, work would pause for a deliberate deci- sion on whether to advance (Evans et al., 2025); still others argue that mirror life poses qualitatively new risks requir- ing bespoke arrangements (Kalra et al., 2026; Paris Confer- ence on Risks from Mirror Life, 2025). A Gated Framework for Calibrating Governance We propose a deliberation framework that is underpinned by two well-established governance ideas—risk-benefit assessment and staged, capability-gated oversight—applied to the specific case of mirror biology. It has a direct ante- cedent where the 2007 study, “Synthetic Genomics: Options for Governance,” worked through how to intervene at defined points in the deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) synthe- sis value chain (owners of synthesizers, suppliers of synthe- sized DNA, and the users of both), mapping graded policy options to each (Garfinkel et al., 2007). The framework here is recognizably in that tradition, transposed from generic gene synthesis to the milestone structure of the bottom-up pathway. More broadly, this framework draws on the risk- governance tradition cataloged in work on dual-use and emerging biological and chemical technologies (Tucker, 2012), which distinguishes technologies by their governabil- ity and matches oversight tools to that profile. The framework also has a close contemporary paral- lel that is specific to mirror biology. Reviewing the state of the field, Chen et al. (2026) proposed a tiered gover- nance architecture for mirror biology in which regulatory stringency scales with the complexity of the system under construction and its proximity to autonomous replication, mapping three risk tiers onto institutional review, national licensing and reporting, and international coordination, respectively. The organizing logic is the one adopted here, arrived at independently: oversight graded to how close a line of research sits to a self-replicating mirror cell, and the responsible actor shifts outward as the tier rises. Categories of Research Activity We sort mirror biology research into six categories that are defined by their relationships to the barriers that stand between existing capabilities and the creation of a mirror cell. The language for these categories is necessarily tech- nical and can be further understood by referring to our companion technical report. The categories encompass not only the components of mirror cells but also some of the research activities that, while not generating a component, would remove barriers to doing so. The potential beneficial uses of research activities or products do not play a role in defining these categories; they are to be considered when calibrating governance to these categories. Category 1, minimally enabling mirror molecules: Research falls into this category if it involves mirror bio- molecules, such as mirror peptides, mirror proteins, mirror nucleic acids, mirror sugars, or mirror lipids, and it has no relationship to a mirror-life milestone either as a compo- nent of such a milestone or as a tool that would remove a barrier to achieving one. Examples would include a mirror enzyme that degrades plastic, therapeutic mirror peptides and mirror nucleic acids, mirror aptamers used as diagnos- tic probes, or individual lipids—including sterols—used as research probes. Category 2, individual mirror translation compo- nents: Research falls into this category if it involves the synthesis or use of any individual mirror biomolecule that would be a component of a mirror transcription-translation 6 system, be it an individual mirror ribosomal protein, mirror ribosomal ribonucleic acid (RNA), or an individual component of a mirror protein synthesis using recombinant elements (PURE). A single such component performs no function on its own and translates nothing; what places the work in this category is that the molecule has no purpose except as part of the mirror translation machinery. Category 3, assembling a mirror translation system: Research falls into this category if it involves the assembly of a mirror transcription-translation system or its most complex components, i.e., tRNAs, aminoacyl-tRNA syn- thetases, or work toward a functional mirror ribosome. A functioning ribosome requires kilobase-scale ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and roughly 50 ribosomal proteins to coas- semble in a cooperative hierarchy. This category is inclusive of methods that would add post-translational modifica- tions to mirror rRNAs and mirror proteins that facilitate their assembly or function. Category 4, advancing a complete mirror genome: Research falls into this category if it results in the syn- thesis or assembly of mirror DNA above a size threshold; we tentatively suggest ten kilobases. This category also includes research that would enable the replication of genome-scale mirror DNA or its error correction, which would be required for the construction of a mirror genome; the synthesis of enzymes that would enable Sanger-style sequencing of mirror DNA; and research that adapts high- throughput DNA sequencing platforms (e.g., Illumina, PacBio, Nanopore) to mirror DNA. Category 5, mirror components for cellular viability: Research falls into this category if it involves the synthesis of mirror versions of cellular components other than the genome and the transcription-translation system that a mirror cell would require to form a physical boundary, exchange materials with its environment, and generate the energy and metabolites needed to function. This includes mirror transmembrane transporters, mirror versions of the metabolic pathways judged necessary to sustain a mirror cell, and mirror membrane constituents produced and assembled as a functional membrane, such as a mirror liposome or mirror lipids generated through a mirror bio- synthetic pathway. The pathways and transporters that a minimal mirror cell requires are not yet settled, so the precise contents of this category could sharpen as the sci- ence does. Individual activities would move into or out of this category under the reclassification provision below. Category 6, assembling a mirror cell: This is any work to encapsulate, at a minimum, a mirror genome and a mirror transcription-translation system within a lipid membrane. Encapsulation of the genome and transcription-translation system alone falls into this cat- egory because those two subsystems together could be sufficient to boot a cell that can produce the remaining components itself. The membrane might be of any chirality because there is uncertainty as to whether the initial cell membrane of a mirror cell would need to be fully mirrored. This boundary applies to any activity that would directly produce a mirror cell regardless of whether it can grow or replicate: A constructed mirror cell is a critical step toward a propagating one. Several activities sit adjacent to the aforementioned categories without falling cleanly inside them. We flag them as open questions rather than forcing them into an individual category. Top-down pathway: These categories cover the bottom-up pathway, in which the milestones are clearest 7 and where our companion technical report concentrates. The top-down pathway needs governing too, and catego- ries for it can be added alongside these. This pathway will need separate treatment: The top-down pathway starts in natural chirality work, and many of its early steps, such as whole genome recoding, have clear beneficial uses that are unrelated to mirror biology, so the chokepoints might not fall in the same places. Whether existing synthetic-biology oversight could apply to the top-down work or whether it would need its own milestone framework is a question we leave open. Natural-chirality advances: Three capabilities in natural-chirality biology would sharply lower the barri- ers that this framework addresses: (1) a natural-chirality ribosome able to synthesize mirror peptides beyond the existing ten-amino-acid maximum achieved to date, which would make mirror proteins far easier to produce; (2) the chemical synthesis of any natural-chirality component of a transcription-translation system, because the method would transfer directly to mirror components; and (3) the bottom-up construction of a natural-chirality synthetic cell, which would provide a booting protocol that could be repeated with mirror components. In the absence of dis- cussion of restraining these activities, of which there has been little to date, they should all be expected to advance. However, the capability leaps expected from natural chi- rality work and the lack of oversight of the work, given its benign nature, strengthen the case for governing mirror biology–specific research strictly. The converse also holds. If controls on mirror-specific research were judged insuf- ficient on their own, closing the gap would mean governing parts of natural-chirality biology, which would be consid- erably harder because that work has more beneficial uses and the line between enabling behavior and benign behav- ior is difficult to draw. Detection tools: Detection work that sits outside the categories should be encouraged. The exception might be mirror DNA sequencing: Methods that depend on better mirror DNA sequencing would fall within Category 2, because the capability that reads mirror DNA for surveil- lance also reads it for construction. Chirality-sensitive biosensors, enantiomer-specific reagents, and mass spec- trometry do not. Improved chemical synthesis of proteins: Existing chemical synthesis methods can be used to create mirror proteins or natural-chirality ones. Therefore, efforts to make these methods less error-prone, more efficient, and less costly could reduce the incentive to build a mirror transcription-translation system. The trade-off is that making the synthesis of mirror proteins easier would also make it more feasible to construct a mirror cell. Discus- sions of how to effectively govern mirror biology will need to take this into consideration. Pre-existing mirror components: Some of this has already been done. Mirror DNA polymerases, mirror T7 transcription, full-length mirror ribosomal RNAs, and mirror 5S ribonucleoprotein complexes have all been constructed. Research governance, of course, cannot be applied retroactively to experiments that have already been completed, but consideration could be given to including future attempts to recapitulate this work within the cat- egorization described previously. The companion technical report sets out the full milestones on the pathway to mirror life creation, and the reader can infer what these achieve- ments mean for the barriers that remain. 8 Four Levels of Governance Intensity We set a spectrum of governance intensity against these categories, distinguishing four levels. Governance levels are cumulative: Measures at lower levels stay in force as higher- level measures are added. Level A, community self-governance: ethical guide- lines, voluntary norms, codes of conduct, due-diligence standards, and community-led transparency mechanisms. These measures rely on the research community’s own institutions and professional culture for compliance. Level A would include no formal governance at all in those areas in which there is consensus that no governance is warranted. Level B, institutional, funder, and publisher over- sight: registration requirements; institutional review and oversight committees; funder conditions, including milestone-gated funding and justification statements; enhanced peer-review protocols, disclosure delays, and redactions; and publishing moratoria. These measures introduce external accountability but operate through existing institutional infrastructure, including through publishers. Level C, regulatory and supply-chain controls: regis - tration or licensing requirements, export controls, manda- tory vendor know-your-customer and transaction-logging obligations, and access controls to materials and facilities. These measures carry state authority and enforceable civil obligations. Level D, prohibition: These activities are placed out- side the bounds of permissible research through statutory measures. Instruments include domestic legislation that establishes the prohibition in law, treaty obligations bind- ing states-parties to it, and standing agreements among the states that are capable of carrying out such work and inter- national treaties. The move from C to D is a move from tightly regulated and licensable to proscribed. The instruments listed within each level are illustrative; which of these instruments suits mirror biology and in what sequence is a design question that we do not answer in this paper.
