# Principles for Communicating the Risks of Mirror Life

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

报告讨论政府如何向公众、科研界、企业和政府机构传播镜像生命风险，背景是合成生物学正在提高构造反向手性生物系统的可能性，而社会认知和政策准备仍然有限。RAND认为镜像细菌可能逃避人类、动物和植物免疫系统，并使既有反制能力难以规模化部署，因此需要政府主导、在能力成熟前启动沟通。报告提出可理解性、透明度、谨慎使用类比、面向不同受众定制信息、预判异议与错误认知五项原则。其证据基础结合镜像生命科学事实、风险传播研究和作者专业判断，并建议把“手性”等术语转换为“左右手差异”等可理解表述。作者强调应如实说明镜像生命尚不存在、可能在10至30年内出现，同时公开风险与政策效果的不确定性、价值判断和科学共识程度。报告反对夸大、隐瞒或用单一信息劝服所有受众，认为这会削弱信任；但也承认当前不确定性可能深到无法给出稳定概率，传播者需在量化表达与避免虚假精确之间权衡。

### 建议

报告建议政府建立统一事实基线和分受众传播方案，使用清晰语言、可校验的时间范围及不失真的类比，并主动回应常见异议、误解和错误信息。传播者还应持续披露研究进展、风险证据、政策选择及其不确定性，把沟通作为后续治理讨论和公众自主判断的基础。

## English Source Material

A s advances in synthetic biology accelerate—enabling scientists to design bio- logical systems from synthetic components—many researchers have begun to question whether some developments could introduce risks that out- weigh their benefits. One such development involves the possible creation of mirror life: a conceivable alternate life form, such as a bacterium, built from proteins and nucleic acids with the opposite molecular “handedness” from those found in nature. Some scientists have warned that, if created, mirror life could pose an unprec- edented global risk, because mirror bacteria could potentially evade human, animal, and plant immune systems, leading to fatal infections, and society would be unable to produce sufficient countermeasures at the needed scale. Despite the potential severity of these concerns, awareness of mirror life remains limited beyond specialist com- munities, and governments have yet to articulate policy responses—let alone develop strategies for communicating what mirror life is, what risks it might entail, and how close current research is to making it possible. In this paper, we draw on research and our expertise in risk communication to outline principles that the U.S. and other governments should consider when devel- oping strategies for communicating about mirror life to the public, research com- munity, business community, and government agencies. Facts About Mirror Life, Its Development, and Potential Risks Communicating about mirror life will require a basic understanding of mirror life. We provide an overview of what it is, the risks it could pose, when it could become reality, and what factors are driving interest in creating it. We further establish a baseline of what activities are underway within the scientific community to address this emerging risk. What Is Mirror Life? Most molecular building blocks necessary for life—known as biomolecules—have three-dimensional, asymmetric struc- tures that are different from their mirror images, much like left hands differ from right hands. Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA), ribonucleic acid (RNA), proteins, and many other biological molecules have this property, called chirality. All life on Earth is composed of biomolecules with only one of these two possible orientations. A mirror organism created through synthetic biology techniques would have molecular components that are mirror images of those found in nature and would constitute mirror life. For example, all known life uses proteins built from left-handed amino acids, whereas nucleic acids—DNA and RNA—are constructed from right- handed sugars. A mirror organism, by contrast, would rely on right-handed proteins and left-handed nucleic acids: a mirror image of natural biology. Although mirror life could theoretically include multi - cel lular organisms, such as a mirror dog or cat, scientists believe that the first instance of mirror life is likely to be a bacterium—a single-cell organism—because of its relative biological simplicity. What Risks Could Mirror Life Present to Humanity and the Environment? In a recent publication, a group of experts and concerned scientists argued that the creation of mirror life would hold significant risks for human, animal, and plant life, as well as the broader ecosystems of Earth (Adamala, Agashe, Binder, et al., 2024). Many scientists worry that, because natural life has never encountered mirror life before, existing biologi- 3 cal defenses would struggle to fight it effectively. This is because the opposite chirality of mirror bacteria would likely disrupt or impair the immune defenses of humans, plants, and animals. Moreover, mechanisms that normally limit the growth of bacteria in the environment—viral infection and predation—also would likely be impaired by the opposite chirality of mirror bacteria. In the absence of effective immune defenses or natural predators, mirror organisms might spread widely, causing fatal infections among many or most multicellular organisms. Concerned scientists also question whether society could develop and deploy countermeasures sufficient to protect humanity if mirror bacteria were created. Although some existing antibiotics are not sensitive to chirality and would thus be expected to kill mirror bacteria, most existing antibiotics are designed to fit the biology of natu- ral organisms and would probably be ineffective against mirror bacteria. It might be possible to make new anti - bi otics targeting mirror bacteria, but scientists doubt such countermeasures could be developed, manufactured, and distributed at the speed and scale necessary to prevent irreversible harm. Moreover, even if society were able to protect humans with medical countermeasures, this effort would do little to mitigate potentially catastrophic impacts on plants and animals that underpin global ecosystems and food systems. Given that mirror life does not yet exist, scientists cannot be certain about the magnitude of these risks. The only way to directly assess them would be to create mirror organisms and test their survivability in the environment and against immune systems and pharmaceuticals. This, however, presents a dangerous paradox: If mirror life is as hazardous as theorized, the very act of testing it could create the risk such experiments are intended to assess. For this reason, many concerned scientists believe that the most effective defense against mirror life is to prevent its creation; by the time its risks are empirically demonstrated, it may be too late for humanity to avoid catastrophe. Although scientists generally view the creation of mirror life as posing potentially catastrophic risks, they tend to agree that research focused on mirror biomolecules—mirror DNA and mirror proteins—does not, on its own, carry comparable dangers. When Could Mirror Life Become Reality? Mirror life does not currently exist. However, research in mirror biology (and synthetic biology more generally) has made it conceivable to some scientists that a living mirror cell could be created within ten to 30 years. Some scientists estimate that, within a decade, new tools from synthetic biology could enable the construction of a living cell from synthetic nonliving parts, and following the same tech- niques with mirror parts could potentially produce a syn- thetic mirror cell (Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024; Adamala, Agashe, Binder, et al., 2024). Researchers have already synthesized mirror ver- sions of important biomolecules, such as mirror DNA and mirror proteins. Furthermore, scientists have made partial progress toward certain steps or processes that will likely be required to create mirror bacteria, such as chemically synthesizing a genome and recoding a bacterial genome (Hutchison et al., 2016; Fredens et al., 2019). However, pro- ducing a viable, self-reproducing mirror organism likely remains years or decades away (Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024; Adamala, Agashe, Binder, et al., 2024). Because mirror life would be a human-made life form, its emergence would depend on the scale and persistence of scientific effort, including funding, personnel, and institutional support. Publicly available evidence suggests that only a small number of research teams worldwide has pursued advanced work in mirror biology, and that, as of June 2026, there are no active research programs with the explicit goal of creating mirror life (Paris Conference on Risks from Mirror Life, 2025). Advances in synthetic biology, biotechnology, or arti- ficial intelligence could significantly accelerate progress and reduce the financial barriers to developing mirror life. One expert has estimated that a mirror cell could be devel- oped for approximately $500 million over the next decade, assuming a direct and transparent effort (Kubota, 2024). This cost could decrease because of improvements in bio- technology or new discoveries in synthetic biology. Why Is There Interest in Mirror Life? Many experts think, based on current knowledge, that the risks of mirror life outweigh the benefits. They have proposed commercial or medical uses—not for mirror life itself but for its subcellular components, such as mirror proteins or mirror DNA. These mirror biomolecules could, for example, improve diagnostics or therapeutics by reduc- ing immune system responses and increasing stability in the body because of their mirror chirality (Harrison et al., 2023; Schearer et al., 2024). By contrast, mirror life—a distinct and more advanced concept—refers to self-sustaining cells that assemble mirror versions of these components. Scientists have hypothesized few beneficial uses for such organisms (Ada- mala, Agashe, Binder, et al., 2024). Although no current projects to create mirror life are known to be underway in the United States or elsewhere in the world, the U.S. government in the past has funded fundamental synthetic biology research aimed at pursuing mirror life (National Science Foundation [NSF], 2014a; NSF, 2014b NSF, 2019). Given the limited perceived practical benefits of synthesiz- ing a mirror cell, actors who pursue it have been motivated by broader, fundamental goals. For example, synthesizing a mirror cell would constitute a major scientific break- through, and some researchers believe that the discovery may reveal insights into the chiral origins of life on Earth (Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024; Adamala, Agashe, Binder, et al., 2024). How Is the Scientific Community Addressing Potential Risks? Concerned about the potential risks of mirror life, interna- tional scientists and experts are increasingly recommend- ing that research intended to create mirror life not be per- mitted (Adamala, Agashe, Belkaid, et al., 2024). In January 2025, the UK Government Office for Science convened an expert roundtable to advise policymakers on the feasibility, potential benefits, and risks of mirror life, including self- replicating mirror cells, and it recommended taking steps to prevent such creation (UK Government Office for Sci- ence, 2025). At recent conferences at the Asilomar Confer- ence Grounds in Pacific Grove, California; the University of Manchester, United Kingdom; and the Institut Pasteur in Paris, France, hundreds of experts shared the concerns that have been raised about mirror life and endorsed the need for governance (The Spirit of Asilomar and the Future of Biotechnology Summit, 2025; Institut Pasteur, 2025; University of Manchester, 2025). The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (undated) held a workshop in September 2025 focused on risk assessment, governance options, and advancing responsible science domestically and internationally. In September 2025, an independent investigation from Germany’s Central Com- mission for Biological Safety endorsed the severity of the risk, and the UNESCO International Bioethics Committee proposed a precautionary global moratorium on creating mirror cells (Zentrale Kommission für die Biologische Sicherheit, 2025). The Carnegie Endowment for Interna- tional Peace hosted a gathering of scientific, policy, and security experts, who recommended that the scientific community clearly and accurately communicate the risks of mirror bacteria to key stakeholders, that funders refrain from supporting research aimed at creating mirror life, and that policymakers engage diverse stakeholders to establish policies preventing its creation (Cuéllar, Hinderstein, and Relman, 2025). RAND publications—Policy Options to Prevent the Creation of Mirror Organisms (Epstein, Craw- 6 ford, and Nevo, 2025) and A U.S. Strategy to Prevent the Creation of Mirror Life (Kalra et al., forthcoming)—provide recommendations for addressing the potential threat from mirror life. Together, these initiatives aim to inform policy deliberations and promote safe research while mitigating catastrophic risks of mirror life. Not all scientists agree on the timing of policy mea- sures to mitigate the risks of mirror life, and some have stated that a ban on mirror life is premature because its creation remains a distant prospect (Peplow, 2025; Ser- vice, 2024). However, given the potential for catastrophic consequences—even if unlikely in the near term—early dialogue on how governments might implement policies to prevent mirror life, including how they communicate its risks to the broader public, is warranted. Why and How Should the U.S. Government Pursue a Communications Strategy on the Risks of Mirror Life? Should governments begin implementing policies to pre- vent the creation of mirror life or mitigate its risks, they will likely require a communications strategy that effec- tively engages the public and key stakeholders. Policy success may depend on the public’s willingness to act in coordinated and specific ways, such as adopting new norms or accepting restrictions on certain activities. Build- ing public understanding and addressing misinformation could help build trust in government officials and legiti- macy for policy actions, helping with policy implementa- tion and potentially combating politicization. Likewise, policies targeting private industry and academic institu- tions to prevent mirror life may require leaders to accept constraints on research and development. Such collective action requires not only clear guidance but also broad understanding and trust. Achieving this effort will demand a communications strategy attuned to how people and organizations perceive and respond to risk, shaped by complex and interconnected analytic, emotional, and social processes. For instance, when the public perceives risks as deeply uncertain or dreaded—as in the case of mirror life, which may be seen as uncontrollable, unjust, or threatening to future generations—people may exhibit stronger, more polar- ized reactions, jeopardizing coordinated responses (Slovic, 2020; Slovic, 1987). To be effective, communications must therefore account for individual traits that influence how people assess risks and proposed responses, including their educational backgrounds, personalities, ethical views, community norms, lived experiences with risk, their trust in authorities, and their sense of personal and economic capacity to act (Fischhoff, 1985). Similarly, communica- tions aimed at organizations, such as businesses or research institutions, must consider what incentives or factors influence their decisionmaking, such as pursuing prestige, generating profits, or advancing social well-being. Under- standing how these factors influence risk perceptions and decisionmaking can help shape a communications strategy that supports the public’s informational needs for making informed, constructive decisions (Fischhoff, 1995; Slovic, 1987; Slovic, 2020; Fischhoff, Slovic, and Lichtenstein, 1982; Wachinger et al., 2013). 7 Five Principles for Communicating Risks of Mirror Life In this paper, we outline five communication principles for (1) explaining mirror life and its risks and (2) framing policy proposals to guard against those risks (see Figure 1). These principles can help guide risk communicators in speaking with the public, scientists, businesses, and gov- ernment agencies that could be tasked with implementing policies to respond to the threat of mirror life. Be Clear by Describing the Facts and Risks in an Accessible Way A core principle of risk communication is bridging the gap between what people currently understand and what they need to know to make informed decisions about risk (Morgan et al., 2001). Early statements on mirror life should quickly orient audiences to the key facts, terminol- ogy, and potential risks of mirror life. When communicat- ing the scientific facts about mirror life to nonspecialists, messengers should use plain language and avoid jargon and complex scientific descriptions (Fischhoff, Brewer, and Downs, 2011). Messengers should opt for simpler language and avoid inaccessible terms that are likely unfamiliar to most people. Be Transparent About Risks, Value Judgments, and Uncertainty Communicators of the risks of mirror life should strive for transparency with the general public and other audiences, because transparency increases the credibility of commu- nication and conveys respect for the audience (U.S. Envi- ronmental Protection Agency, 2025; Federal Emergency Management Agency, 2024; Fischhoff, Brewer, and Downs, 2011). They should be open about the current status and trajectory of mirror life development, the uncertainty of its risks and policy responses, any value judgments, the extent of scientific consensus, and key context surrounding the underlying evidence. Status and trajectory: Messengers should aim to clearly share what is known regarding progress in mirror life research and what developments might unfold in the future (Fischhoff, 1995; Fischhoff, Brewer, and Downs, 2011). In current communications, they should stress that mirror life does not yet exist but could be created in the next ten to 30 years. Communicators should be aware that different people may interpret verbal probabilistic state- ments (e.g., the term unlikely) differently. Best practices suggest incorporating quantitative estimates or ranges where possible (Budescu and Wallsten, 1995; Wallsten et al., 1993). That said, many current uncertainties may be profound, preventing numeric estimates.
