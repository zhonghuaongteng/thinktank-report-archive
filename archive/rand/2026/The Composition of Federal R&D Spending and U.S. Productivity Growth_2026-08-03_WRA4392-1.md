# The Composition of Federal R&D Spending and U.S. Productivity Growth

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

RAND研究认为，联邦研发支出的内部构成比总额变化更能解释美国长期生产率差异，当前偏重开发活动的组合可能超过生产率最大化水平。作者利用1956至2022年研发预算和生产率数据，通过局部投影估计不同研发类别冲击的十年动态效应。一标准差的基础研究占比上升与十年累计劳动生产率提高约2.1个百分点相关，约四分之三效应来自全要素生产率；开发占比的同等上升则与约2.2个百分点下降相关。应用研究和科研设施投资对应约1.7和1.4个百分点的正效应，但估计精度较低。组合优化显示，在总研发支出不变时，生产率导向的帕累托有效组合会把基础研究占比由历史约17%提高到47%，把开发占比由59%降至18%。作者支持重新平衡研发预算，但明确承认模型仅优化劳动生产率，未计入军事竞争力、公共卫生、人才和科学能力等其他合法目标，因此不能把结果直接当作完整预算最优解。

### 建议

在研发预算约束加剧时，联邦政府应提高基础研究权重、降低过度集中的开发支出，并用生产率以外的国家安全、公共卫生和科学能力目标对组合优化结果进行二次校准。

WR-A4392-1 iii About This Working Paper Recent research suggests that the composition of public research and development (R&D) investment such as for basic versus applied research or for plant R&D—not just the overall amount spent on R&D--matters for productivity outcomes. We present new, corroborative evidence using a local projections method to estimate dynamic responses of macroeconomic productivity outcomes to unanticipated changes in R&D composition. We find that if R&D spending shifted toward basic research (i.e., research to expand the knowledge base without specific applications) are associated with the largest cumulative productivity gains over a ten- year horizon, operating primarily through total factor productivity. By contrast, spending shifts toward development activities (i.e., using research to develop or improve products and systems) are associated with cumulative productivity declines of comparable magnitude. This suggests that the current development share exceeds its productivity-maximizing level. We then use an illustrative portfolio analysis to benchmark the historical federal R&D portfolio against alternative allocations, finding that Pareto-optimal portfolios place roughly three times the historical share on basic research and substantially lower shares on development. These results have practical significance for science and technology policy, particularly in the era of increasing budget tradeoffs against R&D spending. This report is written primarily for economists working on innovation, productivity, and science policy. The report may also interest policy analysts engaged with federal R&D spending, though the analysis presumes familiarity with applied macroeconometric methods. Placebo Test ................................................................................................................. 41 1 The Composition of Federal R&D Spending and U.S. Productivity Growth The United States federal government has invested in research and development for decades, with annual expenditures recently estimated to be roughly $200 billion (NCES, Survey of Federal Funds for Research and Development, July 2025).1 This investment spans a diverse portfolio of activities, from fundamental scientific research with no immediate commercial application to the development of specific technologies for defense, health, and other missions. A substantial literature documents that aggregate R&D investment generates positive returns to productivity growth (Gullo, et. al. 2025). Yet relatively little attention has been paid to whether the composition of this investment matters for productivity outcomes, a question of considerable policy relevance. This compositional question has practical significance for science and technology policy, particularly in an era of constrained federal spending on R&D. If different types of federal R&D are associated with substantially different productivity effects, then reallocating resources toward higher-productivity federal R&D types could achieve more productivity growth from the same total spending. Because productivity growth is one of several legitimate objectives of federal R&D spending, we do not claim that this reallocation is strictly optimal. Our analysis focuses on productivity outcomes and does not address how the composition might be evaluated against other objectives such as national security, public health, human capital development, or basic scientific capacity. This work considers four types of R&D spending: basic research, applied research, development, and research infrastructure (“plant”) investment. Distinctions among these types reflect genuine differences in the nature of scientific activity, its time horizons, and its channels of influence on economic outcomes. • Basic research aims to expand fundamental knowledge without specific applications in mind, potentially generating broad spillovers across industries and over extended time periods. This result indicates that, compared to the current development-heavy R&D spending mix , shifting dollars away from development and toward other categories of R&D would result in stronger productivity growth, all else equal. This reallocation should be read as a labor productivity result, not a complete accounting for all policy tradeoffs, such as defense capabilities, public health technologies, or scientific infrastructure. Second, we find that these compositional associations substantially exceed the associations we can detect from positive shocks to overall R&D spending. When we examine shocks to total federal R&D without distinguishing composition, the estimated cumulative productivity response is positive but imprecisely estimated, with confidence intervals that include zero. Third, we develop a portfolio optimization framework that treats different R&D types as assets with heterogeneous expected returns and uncertainties. Our analysis suggests that the historical composition of federal R&D has been substantially over-invested in development and thus undershooting the potential maximum productivity gains. Optimal portfolios in our framework, which seek to maximize long-run productivity gains, nearly triple the investment in basic research and cut investment in development by roughly 40 percentage points. While such calculations require strong assumptions about the stability of estimated effects and the feasibility of reallocation, they provide a useful benchmark for evaluating the productivity implications of compositional choices. Our analysis characterizes associations with labor productivity in the U.S. private business sector. Thus, implications for reallocating funding away from development are therefore conditional on labor productivity being the relevant policy objective. This analysis combines detailed data on federal R&D budget authority by function with the productivity accounts maintained by John Fernald at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco (Fernald, 2014), constructing an annual panel spanning 1956 through 2022. This period encompasses substantial variation in both the level and composition of federal R&D, driven by the Cold War buildup, the Space Race, shifting defense priorities, the rise of health research funding, and periodic changes in science policy emphasis. Figures 1 and 2 in the next section present the evolution of R&D composition and productivity over this period. Our empirical strategy employs Local Projections (LP) as introduced by Jordà (2005) to estimate dynamic responses for macroeconomic productivity outcomes to unanticipated shifts in R&D composition (referred to throughout this paper as “shocks”). The LP method accommodates straightforward computation of cumulative effects and permits flexible treatment of the relationship between short-run and long-run responses. We construct R&D composition shocks as residuals from forecasting equations that control for the predictable component of each series, aggregate R&D dynamics, and macroeconomic conditions.
