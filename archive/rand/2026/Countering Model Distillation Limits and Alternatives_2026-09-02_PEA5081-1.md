# 技术性反蒸馏措施难以单独改变竞争激励

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

报告把模型蒸馏界定为利用更强模型输出训练新模型，从而以远低于前沿研发成本获得大部分能力。其关键判断并非技术措施能否增加一些摩擦，而是这些摩擦能否让理性行为者改为自主建设模型。作者在有利于反蒸馏措施的假设下建模，仍认为只要使用者接受略低于前沿的性能，蒸馏通常具有更高价格—性能比。出口管制提高自主训练所需算力和设备成本，可能进一步强化“蒸馏优于自主研发”的经济激励。降低蒸馏模型质量或提高规避成本需要达到很大幅度，现实中的技术防线多半只能产生边际作用。报告因此主张将技术控制同法律、经济和战略工具结合；同时其曲线和成本参数属于风格化模型，结论应作为政策压力测试而非精确预测。

### 建议

报告建议评估反蒸馏措施时直接比较蒸馏与自主训练的成本—性能关系，并使用能够给蒸馏增加外部代价、降低其市场价值的法律与经济工具。技术措施可继续部署，但不应被当作足以独立形成威慑的方案，出口管制也需评估其对替代路径激励的反作用。

### 中国/上海参考

报告以中国企业获取美国前沿模型能力为主要竞争情景，并指出对华算力出口管制可能抬高自主训练成本、相对增强蒸馏吸引力。这为评估技术封锁与能力替代之间的政策反馈提供了直接证据；原文没有上海层面的讨论。

MICHAEL J. D. VERMEER, BRIAN A. JACKSON Countering Model Distillation Limits and Alternatives Expert Insights PERSPECTIVE ON A TIMELY POLICY ISSUE September 2026 A rtificial intelligence (AI) model distillation, or the process of training a new model on the outputs of a more capable one, presents a real challenge to U.S. advantages in AI because this process allows firms to reproduce much of the value of frontier systems without bearing the full cost of developing them. Recent communications from the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy and leading U.S. AI labs document how Chinese AI firms have been using this method to extract capabilities from U.S. AI models. 1 Theoreti- cally, technical counter-distillation measures could help protect frontier advantage by raising the cost of distillation (for example, by forcing firms to spend more effort on circumventing distillation countermeasures) or degrading the quality of the resulting models. In practice, however, the strategic question is not whether distil- lation can be made somewhat harder but whether it can be made sufficiently unat- tractive that a would-be distiller would rationally choose to build a model indig- enously instead. We argue that the answer is often that technical countermeasures are insuf- ficient to meaningfully alter incentives toward distillation, especially in the geo- political context of 2026. For actors that do not require absolute frontier perfor- mance, distillation can remain the most attractive option because it offers a large 2 share of frontier capability at much lower cost. Export controls reinforce this incentive by increasing the cost of indigenous development relative to copying or approximat- ing existing frontier systems. As a result, even meaningful technical countermeasures might shift the economics of distillation only modestly, leaving distillation the preferred strategy across a variety of plausible circumstances. Our examination proceeds in three steps. First, we use stylized models of AI acquisition, built on assump - tions deliberately favorable to counter-distillation efforts, to show why distillation is likely to dominate indigenous development under those assumptions for any actor driven by economic rationality and willing to accept a model below the true frontier. Second, we assess whether technical countermeasures could alter that result either by imposing enough additional cost or by reducing performance enough to worsen the distilled model’s price-to-performance ratio; we conclude that doing so would likely require changes far larger than are realistically achievable, even if the means to do so were available. Third, we consider what kinds of nontechnical interventions could matter more—particularly measures that impose meaningful external costs on distillation or reduce the market value of distilled models. Our central conclusion is that technical counter-distillation measures might be useful at the margin, but they are unlikely to deter distillation on their own. If policymakers seek real deterrence, they will likely need broader policy, legal, economic, and strategic measures. The Economics of Distillation The fundamental problem with distillation and counter- distillation efforts is that technical distillation counter- measures might not impose costs on would-be model distillers that are significant enough to deter them from distilling. 2 Under those circumstances, as long as Chinese actors are satisfied with acquiring a model that is not at the true frontier of model capability, it is rational to distill the model rather than build it indigenously. Put another way, it is always rational to steal as long as there are no conse- quences for theft. This is especially true under the imposi- tion of export controls on China that make it even harder to build a model indigenously. We illustrate this idea with a series of diagrams. One possible approach would be cost imposition: making distillation so expensive that it either deters the practice or at least forces Chinese firms to pay more than a tiny fraction of the cost of training a frontier model. The relevant issue is not whether distillation has a nontrivial cost—we will show that it plainly does—but whether increasing that cost could meaningfully alter the incentives for distillation. Table 1 shows the estimated costs of compute and distillation for the three Chinese AI companies that were named in Anthropic’s February 2026 report on distillation attacks. 6 DeepSeek is an exception, but the costs for the other two Chinese companies that engaged in aggressive distillation show that distillation costs can be a significant portion of training costs: Distillation costs are on the same order of magnitude as compute costs for the final training run, and analysis shows that distillation costs one-fifth to one-half of the total costs for training compute. At first blush, this might imply that increasing the cost of distil- lation could have a meaningful impact on the total cost to train these models, and thus have an effect on company decisionmaking on whether to engage in distillation. The more-relevant analysis, however, might be to compare distillation costs with overall training research and development (R&D) costs for each of these models, or total cost of ownership. Unfortunately, there are not good public estimates of total R&D costs for each of these models. That said, we might provide other estimates as points of comparison. For example, it was estimated in January 2025 that the total capital expenditure for DeepSeek servers was $1.6 billion and that an additional $944 million was needed to operate them. 7 Against that scale of total expenditure, it is unlikely that the cost of distillation could be increased enough to make it prohibitive to do it. Costs of distillation could increase by an order of magnitude over what Minimax apparently spent on it, and it would still be less than 10 per- cent of the total server operational expenditure of DeepSeek and approximately 3 percent of the estimated total cost of ownership. Those distillation costs would need to be increased by a factor of more than 300 to quadruple the price of the models. Impact of Model Performance Performance degradation is the other obvious lever. If counter-distillation measures cannot materially raise cost, they might still matter if they can substantially reduce the quality of the resulting Chinese models. Here, however, the relevant standard is also demanding. Counter-distillation would have strategic value only if it degraded distilled models enough to materially worsen their price-to- performance ratio or pushed them below capability thresh- olds that matter for adoption in key use cases. A key question to consider, then, is how much would counter-distillation techniques need to degrade distilled model performance to achieve the goals of frontier model developers? 12 Two elements are relevant here: capability thresholds (the distilled model still has to be good enough to accomplish its use case) and price-to-performance ratios. Counter-distillation techniques could succeed if they could successfully lower the capability of the result- ing Chinese models below a key capability threshold (or, at least, convince model developers that they might do so through some kind of sabotage of model outputs used for distillation). 13 However, this threshold will likely differ from one customer to another, and there is likely a capabil- ity floor that arises from the ability to distill capable open- weight models. Such Chinese models as DeepSeek V3 can be served at one-third the price of same-generation Western models for input tokens and one-ninth the price for output tokens. 14 Again, for simplicity, we will combine these ratios and assume that Chinese models are served at one-fifth the price of same-generation Western models overall. Litera- ture suggests that the performance of distilled models can exceed 90 percent of the performance of models they were distilled from. 15 At these values, the price-to-performance ratio of Chinese models, compared with Western frontier models, is 0.2 to 0.9, or 22.2 percent (for which a lower price-to-performance ratio would be more desirable to potential customers). At this value, counter-distillation techniques would need to somehow increase the price of the distilled models by four and a half times or degrade the performance by the same ratio to achieve an equivalent price-to-performance ratio to frontier models. We showed earlier that it was unlikely that counter-distillation efforts 9 could be sufficient to meaningfully raise the price at which a model is served to customers, so we are left to consider performance degradation. Performance would need to degrade to 20 percent of that of a frontier model to bring the price-to-performance ratio close to parity with frontier models. However, even if such a degradation were possible using counter-distillation techniques (and that is a dubi- ous assertion), Chinese AI companies have alternatives. They could simply distill or post-train other existing open- weight models, in the Chinese or Western AI ecosystems, and reach a performance benchmark that would still put them at an advantageous price-to-performance ratio. That said, forcing Chinese companies to pursue that course of action might be considered a win from the perspective of frontier model developers—they would have prevented distillation of their premier models and protected a perfor- mance advantage. Ultimately, this means that there is a performance floor (resulting from capable open-weight models) that Chinese models will never fall beneath, no matter how effective counter-distillation efforts are at degrading distilled model performance. Thus, even if technical counter-distillation techniques employed by frontier model developers succeeded at deterring Chinese compa - nies from distilling frontier models, it would not actually solve a key problem caused by distillation: Chinese com - panies could still serve “good enough” models (derived from open-weight models) for much lower prices than frontier U.S. models. Ultimately, this might mean that both technical countermeasures and strategic deterrence efforts aimed at stopping distillation would be ineffective at solving the problem if price and performance are the only factors that matter. The question then becomes what other options might exist to change those incentives.
