# 开放权重人工智能模型可能增加生物误用风险

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

RAND测试开放权重大模型被用户修改后对生物安全的影响，区分“移除拒答保护”和“增强生物学能力”两条路径。研究发现，移除安全训练在技术上可行，可能放大现实的生物误用路径；直接提升模型的生物能力则更困难。作者据此认为开放权重带来创新和可访问性收益，但安全对齐不能被视为一次性防线。主要证据来自模型实验和威胁路径评估，不能直接推断现实攻击概率。报告建议将模型访问控制、危险能力测试和生物实验环节的筛查结合，并承认过度限制会压缩正常研究与开源创新。

RR-A5112-1 iii About This Report Open-weight large language models (LLMs) are a central part of the AI ecosystem. They enable researchers outside of frontier AI labs to study important topics such as mechanistic interpretability, support open research and reproducibility, and allow users to deploy their own capable models without relying on a third-party provider. The same accessibility that supports scientific use, safety research, and equitable deployment also creates a distinct governance problem. Once a model’s weights are released, its developer can no longer enforce the safety controls that a closed-provider API applies at inference time; anyone can host, modify, and redistribute the weights outside of those controls. A moderately skilled actor can therefore tamper with an open-weight model to strip its safeguards and adapt it for harmful tasks. Biology is a particularly sensitive domain because some upstream knowledge and tool-use capabilities that enable beneficial outcomes also have plausible misuse pathways enabling the development of biological weapons. This report presents an evaluation of biological misuse risks from open-weight LLMs after three classes of post-release modification and tampering: anti-refusal supervised fine-tuning (SFT), reinforcement-learning–based capability enhancement (referred to throughout as capability-enhanced models), and publicly available abliterated or uncensored variants distributed through Hugging Face, an open platform for hosting AI model weights. We compare these tampered variants against untampered open-weight baselines and frontier closed-weight baselines on a benchmark suite that includes a broad set of biosecurity-relevant knowledge-based questions and agentic tasks, adversarial/refusal benchmarks, and a custom influenza pathway evaluation. The report is intended for open-weight model developers, third-party inference providers, AI evaluation researchers, and biosecurity policymakers. It avoids operational fine- tuning recipes, sample-level content from private benchmarks, and procedural detail that could enable threat actors. We document the methodology of our fine- tuning pipelines in enough detail to allow auditing by qualified researchers but withhold specific recipes, datasets, and prompt templates that would meaningfully reduce the effort of replicating an unsafe model. v Summary This report evaluates how feasible it would be for a threat actor to use or modify an open- weight large language model (LLM) for biological misuse1. Biology is widely regarded as one of the highest-priority domains for AI misuse risk because advanced LLMs can provide information and technical assistance that may lower barriers to harmful activities. A sufficiently capable LLM without effective safeguards could help a malicious actor understand biological concepts, develop and troubleshoot computational methods and laboratory protocols, and/or plan aspects of a biological attack—activities that would otherwise require specialized expertise. 2 While current LLMs cannot replace hands-on laboratory skills or directly access physical resources, concerns persist that increasingly capable systems could reduce knowledge bottlenecks and make certain forms of harmful activity more accessible. In this context, the distinction between closed-weight and open-weight LLMs is important. Closed-weight LLMs are retained as proprietary models by their developers and can be deployed with safeguards to detect and reduce misuse. Open-weight LLMs, on the other hand, are publicly available for downloading, self-hosting, modification, and redistribution, with no mechanism to enforce safeguards. Open-weight LLMs provide significant benefits for research, transparency, reproducibility, innovation, customization, and broader access to advanced AI capabilities. However, because the model weights of open-weight LLMs are publicly released, users can alter the model’s behavior, remove safety mechanisms, and share altered versions with others. Moderately skilled actors can apply anti-refusal fine-tuning or weight-level interventions, such as refusal-vector ablation (known as abliteration) 3, to weaken or remove safeguards. Less skilled actors can turn to public repositories that distribute such tampered variants. While recent research shows that many of these tampering methods can reliably reduce or eliminate refusal behavior across harmful queries, it remains unclear whether safeguard removal produces meaningful increases in practical dual-use biological capability. Understanding this distinction is critical for assessing real-world risk. Open-weight LLMs therefore present a complex policy challenge: Their openness generates substantial social, scientific, and economic benefits, but it also creates pathways for safeguards to be weakened, removed, or circumvented outside the control of the original developer. The 1 We define biological misuse of an LLM as any use of an LLM to aid in the creation of a hazardous biological material that is not for legitimate scientific purposes. 2 Soice et al., “Can Large Language Models Democratize Access to Dual-Use Biotechnology?”; Sandbrink, “Artificial Intelligence and Biological Misuse.” 3 Arditi et al., “Refusal in Language Models Is Mediated by a Single Direction.” vi central question is how to preserve the benefits of openness while limiting the risks associated with tampering, unrestricted redistribution, and misuse in sensitive domains, such as biology. Approach We evaluated three classes of tampered open-weight LLM variants against untampered open- weight and closed-weight baselines on a biosecurity-focused benchmark suite to quantify how much post-release tampering raises an open-weight model’s usefulness for biological misuse, as well as to separate gains that come from removing refusals from gains in underlying dual-use capability. We tested four open-weight LLM variants: 1. Baseline: We evaluated untampered open-weight LLMs and compared them against two closed-weight baselines. 2. Anti-refusal SFT: We performed supervised fine-tuning (SFT) to lower refusal rates while preserving capability and evaluated the resulting models. 3. Capability-enhanced: We developed an automated fine-tuning loop that iteratively trains the anti-refusal SFT checkpoint to maximize biological capabilities, and we evaluated the effect of this training. 4. Abliterated: We evaluated LLMs that have been abliterated to remove their refusal behavior, including publicly available models and models abliterated in-house. We evaluated these variants using a benchmark suite that spanned five categories: biology knowledge (general and graduate-level biology); biosecurity-relevant specialized knowledge (virology, human pathogens, and molecular biology); agentic and biological tool-use tasks (protein design, screening evasion, liquid-handling-robot operation, and benchtop DNA synthesis); refusal stress tests; and a custom end-to-end influenza pathway evaluation. The specific benchmarks are listed in the benchmark suite summary (Table 2.1) in Chapter 2. Key Findings Taken together, our findings indicate that current biological misuse risk of open-weight models is governed by how easily safeguards can be removed and how widely refusal-free variants circulate. The most capable open-weight models are already useful for dual-use biology before any tampering, and removing their refusals is cheap and reliable. The current risk comes primarily from making existing capabilities freely accessible without refusals rather than from amplifying underlying capabilities. Here, denial refers to cases where no answer was provided by the model; this can occur due to model refusal or, for closed-weight LLMs, blockage due to a content filter. • Baseline agentic capabilities are already near the frontier. Untampered open-weight frontier models are becoming useful for dual-use computational biology, with capability approaching that of a closed-weight frontier, Gemini 3.1 Pro, and most models surpassing a small panel of human experts on several agentic tasks. Gemini 3.1 Pro leads our suite in part because it refuses less often, whereas GPT-5.4’s performance on dual-use biology is reduced by its high refusal rate. Note, however, that this difference in refusal rates may change with future model releases or safeguards. Because open-weight models can also be self-hosted without refusal classifiers or know-your-customer (KYC) controls, an unmodified open-weight model may ultimately be more useful to a threat actor than a frontier closed model. • Open-weight models can be more useful for end-to-end biological attack planning. On our expert-developed and -graded influenza pathway evaluation, the untampered open-weight Kimi K2.5 outperformed the closed-weight Gemini 3.1 Pro, even though viii Gemini leads on every other isolated benchmark in our suite. The tampered Qwen3.5- 397B model achieves an even higher score than Kimi K2.5 because it rarely refused (Figure S.1). This evaluation covers one end-to-end pathway, and future work covering additional pathways would strengthen this evidence. • Anti-refusal SFT often reduces both refusals and capabilities. This fine-tuning approach reliably removes refusals across every open-weight model we evaluated, but constructing an SFT recipe that simultaneously removes refusals, preserves broad capability, and preserves tool calling requires nontrivial experimentation and expertise. • Capability enhancement fine-tuning did not significantly improve performance. Iterative capability enhancement produced minor improvement in our 12-hour experimental window on a narrow set of public biology benchmarks, but did not generalize to the broader benchmark suite. The final Qwen3.5 checkpoint improved on seven agentic tasks while regressing on six. Further fine-tuning with expanded datasets may yield broader uplift. • Model sharing platforms can make capable, refusal-free models widely accessible. Publicly available abliterated and uncensored models lower the barrier to refusal-free models. Some available uncensored models retain most useful capabilities relative to their unmodified baseline, though many have reduced capabilities. Smaller (<120B parameters), less capable models are more abundantly available, while larger ones are rarer and more expensive to host, owing to greater computational requirements, though hosted inference and fine-tuning services increasingly make even large models accessible. • The cost of tampering to remove refusals and attempt capability enhancement is low. Producing the final anti-refusal checkpoints costs only the low tens of dollars in fine-tuning, and the best capability-enhanced checkpoint costs under $300. These figures reflect only the final training runs and exclude the costs of preliminary experiments and data generation. The total compute cost for all fine-tuning experiments conducted for this project, including preliminary experiments, was roughly $12,000 USD. Recommendations • Open-weight model developers should (i) run pre-release evaluations that include end- to-end threat-pathway evaluations with agentic tasks; (ii) evaluate at least one anti-refusal variant alongside each release; and (iii) consider a staged release strategy for models that approach frontier biological capability. • Researchers and developers should continue research in pre-training data filtering and tamper-resistant safeguards with robustness against anti-refusal SFT and weight-level interventions. Developing such methods is an active research area, but additional work is needed. • Evaluation researchers should design evaluations around end-to-end threat pathways, not individual isolated capabilities, and separate refusal rates from capability uplift when reporting results. If available, they should also benchmark a helpful-only variant to establish an upper bound on a model's latent capability. • Open-weight model platforms should implement safeguards. Third-party inference providers (such as Together AI) could implement content filters against harmful prompts, and fine-tuning services (such as Tinker) could implement harmful data classifiers. ix Platforms that host open-weight model weights (such as Hugging Face) could investigate the feasibility of implementing basic safety evaluations on models whose size is above some threshold. Because such platform-side checks could be evaded, they mitigate rather than fully eliminate the risk.4 • Government decisionmakers should treat open-weight governance as a problem of tampering and distribution, not of static model capability. Funding agencies should support third-party evaluations that can safely use private, high-fidelity biosecurity benchmarks and that report on entire threat pathways rather than individual capabilities while avoiding saturated benchmarks as a risk signal. Policymakers and other governing bodies should encourage disclosure standards that include refusal behavior, post- tampering evaluation, and subject-matter expert review.
