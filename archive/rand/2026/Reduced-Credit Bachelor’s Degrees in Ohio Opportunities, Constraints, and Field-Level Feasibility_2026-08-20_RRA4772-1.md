## English Source Material

Ohio’s education policymakers are considering whether some bachelor’s degree programs can require fewer than the traditional 120 semester credits while preserving educational quality, workforce value, and student opportunity. The authors examine the feasibility of reduced-credit bachelor’s degree programs in Ohio to identify where reduced-credit pathways appear to be most viable. Kramer , Peter Nguyen , Jonah Kushner , Sichen Shawn Chao , Min Jung Kim Research Published Aug 20, 2026 Download PDF Additional Downloads Includes Annex I, Opportunities, Constraints, and Field-Level Feasibility; Annex II, Program Inventory; and Annex III, Feasibility Matrix Share on LinkedIn Share on X Share on Facebook Email Policymakers, accreditors, and institutions are reexamining the traditional bachelor’s degree in the United States. For generations, the dominant model of undergraduate education has been a four-year, 120-credit bachelor’s degree. In recent years, there has been growing concern about college affordability, student debt, the amount of time it takes to earn a degree, and the labor market value of postsecondary credentials. This has prompted interest in whether some bachelor’s degrees can require fewer credits without sacrificing quality. In response to a statutory requirement in the Advance Ohio Higher Education Act, the authors of this report examine the feasibility of reduced-credit bachelor’s degree (RCD) programs in Ohio. The authors draw on a national inventory of RCD programs, a review of institutional and specialized accreditation and Ohio regulatory requirements, interviews with institutional and field-level stakeholders, and a survey of graduate and professional admissions leaders in Ohio. They identify where reduced-credit pathways appear to be most viable under existing conditions, what constraints are likely to shape these programs’ development, and what questions remain unanswered. Key Takeaways The feasibility of implementing RCDs in Ohio varies by degree field. The national inventory produced in this study identified 119 programs that had been publicly announced as of May 2026, most of which were in applied and workforce-facing fields. The feasibility of postsecondary institutions implementing RCDs in Ohio varies by the degree field. Business, information technology, cybersecurity, and some related applied fields appear to offer the clearest near-term opportunities to implement RCDs, while fields with extensive clinical and licensure requirements or tightly sequenced courses appear to be less adaptable. Institutional accreditors are open to RCDs, but approval and implementation remain demanding, and these programs require clear academic justification. Most of the identified RCD programs preserve core general education requirements and reduce flexibility elsewhere in the curriculum. Reduced-credit pathways might be promising for targeted student populations, including adult learners and re-engaged students who are seeking more-structured and more-efficient routes to a degree. There is continued uncertainty about how RCDs will be treated for credit transfer between schools, graduate and professional programs, licensure, and employers. Clearly communicating these uncertainties to students will therefore be essential.
