# 面向社区规划者的数据中心选址模型与适宜性地图

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

RAND把数据中心选址模型定位为帮助州和地方决策者比较不同地点成本与收益的技术基础设施工具。技术文档说明，模型把电力、用水、土地、网络、劳动力、环境和社区影响等变量组合为可视化比较，而不是给出脱离地方条件的单一答案。其核心价值在于把数据中心建设从招商口号转化为可审计的多维权衡。作者强调结果依赖输入数据、权重和情景设定，地方政府仍需结合许可、社区协商和实地核验。该限制意味着工具可支持筛选和对话，但不能替代正式环境评估或公共决策。

T L- A 4 617-2 iii About This Tool With the increase in artificial intelligence (AI) use and cloud computing systems, demand for computational resources provided by data centers is growing. The U.S. has over 4,000 data centers, one-third of which are in Virginia, Texas, and California, yet as the sector grows, the locations considered for data centers are expanding rapidly. Powering and cooling these systems impose enormous demands on the energy grid and water supplies, making proper siting of data centers essential. Water supply availability and power generation capacity vary across the United States. Further, all regions of the country are equally viable for new data centers due to the variation in risks from natural disasters, increasing drought and extreme heat. In particular, Texas and California are at risk of environmental hazards ranging from drought, wildfires, hurricanes and severe storms, potentially indicating that water stress and risk of extreme weather are not being considered in siting these data centers. This project is intended to consider the resource tradeoffs important to data center siting. Further, we intend this work to support local decisionmakers as they weigh the tradeoffs of building local or regional data centers and to set local policies to support existing local industries and communities’ needs and services when considering a data center. This project developed a national mapping tool for local decisionmakers to holistically consider the above factors and incorporate them into their decisionmaking when planning, designing, and constructing new data centers. This document provides an overview and technical documentation for this tool, the Data Center Siting Model and Suitability Mapper for Community Planners, which is designed to support state and local decisionmakers considering the benefits and costs of siting a data center in their community. The tool allows for a county-by-county comparison to assess the suitability of a location to host data centers. We intend this tool to support users at the state and federal level who may be considering data centers locations across a region and for local decisionmakers aiming to better characterize the benefits of their jurisdiction compared to other places a data center company may be considering. This tool was developed to be a user-friendly visualization tool bringing together diverse public data sets describing different aspects of data center needs and impacts and to illustrate the results of our siting model. The tool is available at https://www.rand.org/pubs/tools/TLA4617-1.html. Introduction A data center is “a facility that houses and powers a large computer system.”1 Data centers are made up of large banks of computer servers and the power, HVAC, broadband, and water support systems required to operate those systems. Data centers provide capacity for cloud storage systems and cloud-based computing capabilities underpinning many web services, financial and banking systems, social media, online shopping and virtual communication systems. 2 As an increasing number of companies have digitized over the years, increasing incorporation of digital records, and building computing into their workflows. The global economy has become reliant on these systems and by extension the data centers that support them. Global reliance on digital systems further increased after the worldwide shutdowns that followed the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic. 3 The rise in remote work increased demand for digital communication systems such as video calling, email and instant messaging systems. Most recently, data centers are providing the supercomputing power for cryptocurrency exchanges and the large language models behind artificial intelligence (AI)-based technologies such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini, among others.4 As the United States and China compete for global dominance over AI capabilities, demand for these models and development of even more sophisticated AI models is rapidly increasing. 5 Such capability requires substantially more computing power than previous webservices systems and has driven the rise of a new class of data centers: hyperscale data centers. Due to the extreme power and water demands and broadband needs of these systems, identifying the best locations to site these systems is an evolving question.7 This project brings together disparate data sets to characterize the resource demands and potential impacts of hyperscale data centers on communities across the United States. Leveraging these data, we formulated and tested an optimization model to identify the counties most suitable for data center development. To support local governments making decisions about data centers today, we developed a web-based visualization tool to illustrate the results of our optimization model and to allow comparison among data sets describing the resource demands and impacts of data centers. To contextualize our model findings, we analyzed the locations of existing and proposed data centers in the United States. Mid-size Mid-sized data centers range between 2,700 and 6,900 square feet, and are frequently stand- alone buildings that support data storage and average digital computing services. NOTE: LLM = Large language model. 4 Decision Dimensions for Data Center Siting Cities and states across the United States are seeking to attract data centers to their communities as the boom of AI drives demand for more and faster computing infrastructure. Data centers come with the promise of local economic gains through tax revenues and future technology companies with high paying jobs. Yet there are downsides to having a data center in your community. As decisionmakers compare the benefits and costs of data centers, we consider the variables important to data center siting in four dimensions of impacts: physical constraints, environment, community, and governance. Physical Constraints Hyperscale data centers are large and require substantial land area. 10 They require substantial energy inputs to power computing and data storage servers, and all supporting equipment.11 Many data centers rely on water-based cooling systems which require substantial water inputs.12 Lastly, hyperscale data centers require transfer of large amounts of data to support AI training and prediction models and therefore require high-speed broadband connectivity.13 Variables within this category are proxies designed to capture the resource requirements common to hyperscale data centers. Environment Data centers have the potential to negatively impact the local environment. Depending on the energy generation sources – such as natural gas plants or diesel generators – substantial energy demands can increase air pollution. 14 Where communities are already experiencing unhealthy air quality, an increase in PM2.5 or NO2 can have negative impacts on public health.
