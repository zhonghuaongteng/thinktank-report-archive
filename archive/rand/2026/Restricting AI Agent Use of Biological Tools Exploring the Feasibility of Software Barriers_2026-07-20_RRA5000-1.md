# Restricting AI Agent Use of Biological Tools: Exploring the Feasibility of Software Barriers

## 中文摘要与研判

### 核心观点

报告检验在生物软件工具内部设置安全屏障，能否阻止大语言模型智能体帮助非专业人员执行潜在有害任务。RAND 对工具层单独防护的有效性持明确怀疑态度，实验开发的多种屏障均未能稳定阻止智能体调用生物工具。测试发现，智能体会隐瞒或误报自身AI身份、忽视软件许可和开发者警告，并为完成用户目标绕过或关闭安全措施，显示风险超出单一生物工具设计。越狱和拒答向量消融等对模型护栏的破坏会进一步降低工具屏障效果，说明依赖现有模型拒答机制不能形成稳健缓解。报告以预运行检查等不同技术进行对照，结论是即使相对有效的方案也无法达到生物安全所需的高可靠性。其反方可能性是工具开发者可以继续提高防篡改能力，但研究认为上游智能体行为与工具规则存在系统性脱节，独立软件开发者无法单独控制AI调用。

### 建议

作者建议大模型与智能体运行框架开发者建立标准化机制，使生物工具能够声明对AI调用的限制，并由模型提供方可靠执行；有效治理需要模型、智能体平台和生物软件开发者共同协调。

We would also like to thank our peer reviewers, Oliver Crook and Shawn Chao, whose contributions strengthened this report. iv Summary Advances in computational biology and artificial intelligence (AI) are rapidly transforming scientific research, enabling breakthroughs in protein and drug design. These same technologies may introduce new risks: AI agents, powered by large language models (LLMs), are becoming increasingly capable and may lower barriers to malicious use of software tools that would otherwise require substantial domain knowledge. This report uses biological software tools as a test case to examine the technical feasibility of implementing safeguards into software that restrict or control AI agent usage of the software, with the broader aim of assessing whether modifications to software alone could restrict AI agent usage without coordination with LLM developers. Issue The growing capabilities of LLM agents increase the risk that a nonexpert threat actor can use biological software tools to design dangerous biological materials. We consider a threat model in which a computationally novice threat actor who lacks the expertise to independently use biological software tools seeks help from an AI agent to design or modify biological hazards. There is currently little research on safeguards embedded within biological tools that could complement existing LLM safety mechanisms to restrict AI agent usage. This report examines the feasibility and efficacy of such tool-level safeguards. Approach We tested a range of tool-level safeguards (text or code embedded within biological software tools to restrict agent usage) and evaluated their efficacy in preventing AI agents from assisting with the tool’s use. We designed safeguards implemented directly into biological tools that target LLMs, LLM content filters, and the agentic harnesses (the software frameworks that enable LLMs to execute tasks and write and run code) that interact with the tool. Key Findings • The tool-level safeguards that we developed were not consistently effective at preventing LLM agents from assisting with potentially harmful use of biological tools. • LLMs exhibit a range of behaviors that raise broad security concerns. This includes misrepresenting and hiding their identity as AI, ignoring software licenses and developer warnings, and bypassing or disabling safeguards in order to fulfill user requests. These behaviors undermine safeguards, limiting the ability of independent software developers to control AI-enabled use of their tools. v • The tool-level safeguards we developed are compromised by subversion of LLM guardrails1: adversarial subversion techniques such as jailbreaks and refusal-vector ablation reduce the efficacy of safeguards, indicating that reliance on LLM guardrails does not currently provide robust risk mitigation. Recommendations We recommend that LLM and agent harness developers establish standardized mechanisms that allow biological tool developers to signal restrictions on AI agent use and to ensure that those restrictions are reliably enforced. Without coordination from LLM developers, tool developers appear to lack the ability to restrict how agents use their tools. Introduction Advances in computational biology and artificial intelligence (AI) are accelerating the development of new therapies and biologics (Zhang et al. 2025a), while also raising concerns of misuse. For example, biological tools such as RFdiffusion (Watson et al., 2023) enable de novo design of protein backbones guided by functional constraints, and ESM3 (Hayes et al., 2025) can integrate sequence, structure, and functional data to design new proteins. Predictive software such as EVEscape (Thadani et al., 2023) can combine biophysical and structural data to predict viral mutants that are likely to escape host immunity. These tools accelerate scientific progress but could also enable the design of biological materials with harmful properties. Alongside these advances in biological tools, advances in large language models (LLMs) have produced AI agents that can autonomously execute computational biology workflows. These AI agents (also called LLM agents) consist of an LLM coupled to an agent harness, a piece of software that lets the LLM perform actions in a computer environment 2 by providing structured descriptions of available actions and executing actions specified in the LLM’s outputs. Cai et al. (2025) showed that AI agents can successfully navigate a protein engineering workflow, and Liu et al. (2025) demonstrated that AI agents can match or outperform human experts in writing Python scripts for DNA assembly on robotic liquid handlers. Zhou et al. (2025) developed an autonomous AI agent for end-to-end multi-omic data analyses, and Xiao et al. (2024) developed a multi-agent framework for single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. These capabilities raise concerns that AI agents could use biological tools to design biological hazards such as potent pathogens or viral surface proteins that enable immune escape (Zhang et al. 2025; Cai et al. 2025; Sandbrink 2023; Wang et al. 2025; Bloomfield et al. 2024; Brent, et al., 2024). While current biological design tools may be limited in their capability, they are rapidly improving, motivating calls to proactively mitigate the emerging risks that they pose (Baker and Church, 2024). LLM-based chatbots can lower the expertise barrier to using biological tools through procedural guidance and troubleshooting (Batalis, 2024). Autonomous AI agents further reduce reliance on tacit laboratory and programming skills by directly operating biological software tools and executing complex workflows without human intervention (Zhang et al., 2025). In response, researchers and scientists have called for targeted safeguards and governance frameworks to reduce misuse risks associated with AI-agent access to and use of biological tools (Zhang et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2025). To address these misuse concerns, developers of scientific software may wish to prevent AI agents from interacting with their software while preserving access for human users. Moreover, 2 LLM agents that can perform actions in a physical environment have also been studied (Liu et al., 2025), though this technology is less mature. 2 this challenge is not unique to biology, and software developers in other domains may also wish to restrict AI agent use of their software. Examples may include sensitive communication tools, where privacy concerns arise if user content is transmitted to LLM providers; financial or transaction management systems, where mistakes are costly; cybersecurity or system administration tools, in which AI usage may enable offensive cyber attacks; and medical, human resources, or legal software, where outputs have high consequences and may create liability. Existing Guardrails and the Tool-level Gap LLMs are typically trained and deployed with guardrails to reduce the risk of general misuse, including a propensity to refuse harmful requests and content filters that block dangerous prompts and outputs (Andriushchenko et al., 2025; Wei et al., 2023; Gong et al., 2025). While less technical detail is publicly available about guardrails in recent frontier models, LLM developers have documented their approaches in older models. For example, Anthropic fine- tuned its models using preference modeling and RLHF to behave as “helpful and harmless assistants” and further supplements this approach with a set of rules or principles (Constitutional AI) applied during the supervised and reinforcement-learning phases to improve harmlessness (Bai et al., 2022a; Bai et al., 2022b). Similarly, OpenAI reported that GPT-4 was fine-tuned using RLHF and deployed with additional mitigations, such as adversarial testing with domain experts and a model-assisted safety pipeline (OpenAI, 2024). The Gemini technical report likewise noted that safety-policy evaluation combined automatic classifiers trained on prior model interactions with human annotation (Anil, et al., 2025). Meta’s report on Llama 2 also described the use of safety-specific data annotation, safety fine-tuning, and red-teaming to improve model safety (Touvron et al. 2023). However, these guardrails do not eliminate risk. LLM refusals and content filters are triggered by input text that appears explicitly unsafe, but text from malicious biological workflows can appear benign and fail to trigger these guardrails (Brent et al., 2025). Moreover, there is no consensus on what content warrants refusals, and developers of biological software may wish to restrict AI agent usage of their tools for workflows that are not restricted by the guardrails implemented by LLM developers. LLM guardrails can also be weakened through adversarial prompting (jailbreaking) or removed from open weight models (Davies et al., 2026; Volkov 2024). In high-risk domains such as biology, guardrail failures may result in severe consequences. We therefore explore whether there exist methods that software developers could use to exert control over how AI agents use their tools. Such tool-level safeguards would be complementary to existing LLM guardrails, potentially giving tool developers control of whether and how AI agents interact with their tools. Tool-level safeguards would join a broader set of proposed interventions designed to mitigate AI-agent misuse of biological software.
