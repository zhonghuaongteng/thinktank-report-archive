Co-Chairs’ Statement from the 2026 China-UK Science Policy Dialogue between the Chinese Academy of Sciences and The Royal Society on Food Systems and Biodiversity Loss

The food system is one of the greatest causes of global biodiversity loss and an increasingly significant cause of climate change. At the same time, food production is threatened by both biodiversity loss and climate change. Unless we change how food is produced, what foods are consumed and how food waste is avoided, further population growth will lead to increased environmental impacts and reduced resilience of food production. The 2026 China-UK Science Policy Dialogue brought together a group of science, technology and policy experts from China and the UK to identify solutions to this urgent global challenge. This statement summarises the key findings and recommendations from these discussions.

1.  The need for sustainable intensification

Sustainable intensification – reducing the amount of land, water and/or inputs required to produce a given amount of food – will be an essential part of the solution. There is already an extensive evidence base on how to achieve sustainable intensification, especially through methods that improve soil quality. Where the advantages of a new technology or practice are well established, further efforts might be required to promote adoption by farmers and land managers. The China Agricultural University’s Science and Technology Backyards programme is an excellent example of this. Policy and market mechanisms might also be required to help with any transition costs, such as short-term reductions in yields or new machinery.

2.  Technology opportunities

New and emergent technologies can help achieve the goal of sustainable intensification. For example, genomic and wider genetic approaches to plant and animal breeding are already delivering significant increases in productivity and resilience to biotic and abiotic stresses, but have the potential to do much more. Recent advances in our understanding of the genetic basis of advantageous traits, such as resistance to disease, pests and drought, are yet to be translated into crops and livestock in forms that are available to farmers. There is also a need to invest in research and breeding programmes for plants that are grown on smaller scales or as cover crops to increase the feasibility of growing a wider variety of crops to take advantage of marginal land and to restore degraded land. Emergent technologies like enhanced rock weathering and biochar have the potential to achieve productivity improvements and benefit soil carbon sequestration at the same time.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has great potential to accelerate breeding programmes by building on large-scale genomic projects to predict phenotypes. At field level, AI combined with remote sensing technologies can increase the efficiency of input usage and is delivering new capabilities, such as the ability to detect and target protection against insect pests. AI is also helping to integrate robotics into farming systems.

Determining the contribution that any technology can make to sustainable intensification requires large-scale, long-term research. It is important to review whether current methods of evaluation adequately assess climate resilience and compatibility with modern farming methods. Some technologies, such as synthetic microbiomes (microorganisms used to increase productivity and soil biodiversity), have shown promise but require further research to improve their reliability.

3.  Public engagement

Sustainable intensification alone will not be enough to achieve the goal of ensuring there is enough food to meet global nutritional needs while reducing environmental impacts from production. We also need to reduce food loss and waste and shift towards lower impact diets. Efforts to inform people about sustainable production technologies and low impact diets face the challenge of misinformation. Multi-disciplinary research is required into the most effective means of convening societal discussions on food system change.

4.  Policy challenges

One of the goals of sustainable intensification is to reduce the land footprint of agriculture to provide more space for nature. Delivering this outcome will depend on policies that engage farmers in co-design processes to identify and adopt sustainable intensification practices that are suitable to them and policies that protect spaces for nature.

5.  Closer UK-China collaboration

Our discussions revealed extensive shared interests and complementary expertise between UK and Chinese researchers. Areas identified as priorities for follow up research included soil restoration techniques, the use of AI in plant and animal breeding, and the development of precision agriculture and agricultural robots. We also encourage enhanced exchanges for early-career researchers and students to build a sustainable talent pipeline for future UK-China collaboration on food systems and environmental sustainability.

By working closely together, China and the UK have the opportunity to accelerate the development and adoption of solutions to the global challenge of ensuring everyone has a healthy diet while reducing the food system’s impact on the environment.
