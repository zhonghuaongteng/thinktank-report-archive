Diverging AI Governance Structures in the Indo-Pacific

by Ryan Calo and Xinzi Lyu
May 18, 2026

Ryan Calo and Xinzi Lyu consider the different approaches to AI governance of China, South Korea, India, Japan, Singapore, and Australia. Their analysis examines how the underlying distribution of authority influences governance outcomes.

In recent years, governments across the Indo-Pacific have adopted markedly different approaches to the governance of AI, reflecting significant structural divergence even in cases of broadly similar desired outcomes. Regional governments generally seek to harness AI in support of economic development and public-sector innovation, while also addressing growing concerns regarding safety, accountability, and societal impact. Much of the existing literature emphasizes policy content such as ethical principles, sectoral applications, or regulatory scope. This analysis examines how the underlying distribution of authority influences governance outcomes. Institutional configurations can be broadly categorized as either top-down or multi-stakeholder in orientation. Top-down governance refers to centralized regulatory arrangements in which authority is concentrated within specific institutions and is often exercised through binding laws, formal procedures, and state-led oversight. In contrast, multi-stakeholder governance involves distributed coordination among government bodies, industry actors, and civil society, often relying on nonbinding instruments such as guidance documents, voluntary standards, and collaborative mechanisms. These categories are conceptual simplifications, but they help clarify structural differences in how AI governance is developed and organized across jurisdictions. In practice, most Indo-Pacific countries adopt approaches somewhere between these extremes that reflect their particular legal traditions, administrative capacities, and development priorities.

The United States and the European Union exhibit contrasting regulatory structures. U.S. AI policy has been largely precatory (nonbinding) and sectoral, supplemented by state-level legislation and agency-specific initiatives, rather than a comprehensive national framework. U.S. policies are also generally developed with inputs from industry, academia, and civil society. The EU, by contrast, has adopted a centralized and omnibus approach through instruments such as the AI Act, which explicitly organizes AI governance around risk classification and compliance obligations, demonstrating a primarily top-down authority, although informed by consultations with a range of stakeholders.[1] These approaches illustrate different ways of structuring regulatory authority over AI, both of which could influence the development of frameworks elsewhere but do not themselves constitute universal models that Indo-Pacific countries could simply replicate.

Countries in the Indo-Pacific occupy varied positions across the governance spectrum, often combining elements of top-down and multi-stakeholder governance as shaped by domestic institutions and strategic priorities. The sections that follow examine selected national cases not as comprehensive accounts but as illustrations of broader trends in regulatory structure and their implications for innovation, risk management, and regulatory compliance.

China[2]

China represents the most top-down, centralized approach to AI governance among major Indo-Pacific jurisdictions. Relative to other countries in the region, China’s regulatory approach emphasizes unified oversight, early-stage intervention, and system-wide coordination from government bodies across both AI development and deployment.

This approach is grounded in a combination of long-term strategic planning and administrative enforcement. The New Generation of Artificial Intelligence Development Plan released in 2017 frames AI as central to national modernization, including industrial upgrading, administrative governance, and security capacity.[3] Since 2021, a series of administrative measures enforced by the Cyberspace Administration of China have required AI providers to register algorithms and models, conduct security assessments, and ensure the legality of training data sources.[4] Research activities are guided by provisional standards developed by the Ministry of Science and Technology, with further national standards under consideration. In addition, the inclusion of an AI law in the National People’s Congress’s legislative plan in 2024 signals continued movement toward more comprehensive statutory oversight.[5]

This centralized governance structure enhances the state’s capacity to coordinate AI development, promote consistency across applications, and manage system-level risks, particularly in large-scale public-sector and administrative contexts. At the same time, centralized oversight and procedural requirements may shape the range of available innovation pathways for firms and researchers in ways that affect flexibility and diversity in the country’s AI ecosystem. These dynamics illustrate how centralized governance arrangements influence not only risk management but also the organization and direction of AI development within the broader innovation ecosystem.

South Korea[6]

South Korea illustrates a predominantly top-down approach to AI governance, characterized by centralized coordination and a recent consolidation of binding statutory regulation. Relative to other Indo-Pacific jurisdictions, South Korea occupies a position closer to the centralized end of the regulatory spectrum than countries that rely primarily on industry-led voluntary commitments or guidance-based frameworks. At the same time, it is less centralized and comprehensive than system-wide, top-down models.

South Korea’s top-down orientation is reflected in both institutional coordination and the gradual transformation of earlier guidance-based commitments into law. South Korean AI policy is coordinated by the Ministry of Science and ICT and was initially articulated through strategic and ethical instruments. The National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (2019) outlined the country’s ambition to become a global leader in AI development by 2030, while the National Guidelines for AI Ethics (2020) emphasized human dignity, inclusiveness, and trust as core values guiding the development and use of AI.[7] More recently, these commitments have been codified through legislation. The Act on the Development of Artificial Intelligence and the Establishment of Trust (AI Basic Act), which was enacted in January 2025 and entered into force in January 2026, introduces binding transparency and safety obligations for designated AI systems and formalizes a risk-oriented oversight framework.[8]

This regulatory structure strengthens the state’s capacity to provide regulatory clarity and to manage risks associated with higher-impact AI applications, particularly in commercial and public-sector contexts. At the same time, the introduction of binding statutory obligations may increase compliance costs and weaken innovation incentives, especially for smaller firms and early-stage research initiatives operating outside priority or higher-risk categories. South Korea’s approach illustrates how centralized coordination and legal formalization can coexist with an ongoing emphasis on responsible innovation, while highlighting the potential downsides to a predominantly government-driven approach.

India[9]

India occupies a transitional position in the Indo-Pacific AI governance landscape, prioritizing capacity-building and inclusion, while gradually moving toward more formal regulatory structures. Relative to other jurisdictions in the region, India sits between multi-stakeholder guidance-based models and more centralized or statutory approaches, with the state establishing the principles guiding AI governance and development, but deliberately pursuing lighter-touch regulation largely based on existing sector-specific frameworks.

This orientation is reflected in a policy framework that emphasizes strategic coordination and incremental institutionalization. India’s AI policy has been shaped by the National Strategy for Artificial Intelligence (2018) and more recently the AI Governance Guidelines (2025). The latter was developed through a multi-stakeholder process involving government, industry, academia, and civil society and articulates a vision of “AI for all” across socially and economically significant sectors.[10] Other baseline governance requirements have been introduced through measures such as the Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023) and advisory guidance on safety and transparency.[11] Rather than adopting a comprehensive AI-specific regulatory regime, India has pursued a gradual approach in which AI governance mechanisms are layered onto existing digital and data frameworks.

This evolving governance structure supports flexibility and enables rapid experimentation and adoption, particularly in socially and economically significant applications that may be subject to more comprehensive regulation in other countries. At the same time, the incremental and fragmented nature of regulatory development may pose challenges for coherence, consistency, and early-stage risk identification as AI systems scale. India’s approach does not fit neatly into either a top-down or multistakeholder framework, but instead it illustrates an evolving, capacity-first pathway to AI governance. Innovation is emphasized, governance largely consists of sector-specific frameworks and voluntary commitments, and regulatory consolidation remains a work in progress.

Japan[12]

Japan illustrates a coordination-intensive and multi-stakeholder approach to AI governance, grounded in guidance, soft law, and shared regulatory expectations between government and industry. Relative to other Indo-Pacific jurisdictions, Japan occupies a position toward the guidance-based end of the regulatory spectrum, placing less emphasis on binding statutory controls than countries that have pursued more centralized approaches.

This approach to AI reflects Japan’s long-standing reliance on regulation through guidance, combined with centralized mechanisms for strategic coordination. The AI Promotion Act adopted in 2025 empowers the prime minister to coordinate AI-related research, policy, and international cooperation through the AI Strategy Headquarters, reinforcing centralized strategic direction without introducing comprehensive AI-specific regulation.[13] Japan has otherwise relied primarily on nonbinding policy instruments, including the Social Principles of Human-Centric AI (2019), the Cabinet Office–led AI Strategy (2022), the Governance Guidelines for Implementation of AI Principles (2022), and the AI Guidelines for Business issued between 2024 and 2025.[14] While the government plays a central coordinating role in Japan’s AI governance strategy, close government-industry ties and cultural standards around regulatory compliance allow for a flexible, multi-stakeholder approach.

Japan’s governance structure supports flexibility and adaptability in AI development, allowing firms and researchers substantial discretion in how regulatory expectations are interpreted and implemented. At the same time, reliance on guidance and coordination may pose challenges for consistency and enforceability across sectors as AI applications scale and diversify. A governance model centered on shared responsibility and soft law may also face limitations in identifying and addressing higher-risk or frontier AI systems at earlier stages of development. Japan’s approach demonstrates a deeply collaborative model tailored to its industrial strengths and policy environment in which innovation-oriented policy design is balanced against ongoing efforts to manage risk and maintain public trust through nonstatutory means.

Singapore[15]

Singapore exemplifies a highly coordinated form of multi-stakeholder AI governance, relying on cross-agency collaboration, soft law, and structured engagement with industry and civil society. Like Japan, Singapore occupies a position toward the guidance-based end of the regulatory spectrum, while exhibiting a higher degree of administrative coordination and institutional alignment than more decentralized approaches.

Singapore’s orientation is reflected in a governance framework that emphasizes centralized coordination alongside distributed implementation, with governance seen as a shared responsibility between government, industry, and research institutions. Singapore’s National AI Strategy (2023) frames AI as a tool for both public good and national advantage, while responsibility for AI governance is shared across multiple government bodies overseeing digital government, communications, industry development, and data protection.[16] Rather than adopting comprehensive AI-specific legislation, Singapore has relied primarily on nonbinding instruments. Most notably, the Model AI Governance Frameworks issued in 2020 and updated in 2024 articulate expectations for transparency, accountability, and safety through iterative guidance and stakeholder engagement.[17]

This regulatory structure supports policy coherence and facilitates risk awareness across sectors, particularly in commercially deployed AI systems. At the same time, reliance on soft law and collaborative mechanisms could limit the depth and consistency of enforcement as AI applications scale or move into higher-risk domains. Singapore’s role as an innovation hub in Southeast Asia is conducive to the strengths and constraints of a coordination-intensive, nonstatutory model of AI governance that prioritizes trust-building and adaptability over formal legal control.

Australia

Australia has pursued a principles-based approach to AI governance centered on ethical guidance and voluntary adoption developed through structured consultation with industry, academia, and civil society. Like other Indo-Pacific jurisdictions, Australia occupies a position toward nonstatutory governance, placing minimal reliance on AI-specific legislation and relying instead on policy coordination and guidance to promote responsible AI practices.

Australia’s approach is reflected in a sequence of policy instruments that have gradually increased in operational specificity without introducing binding statutory controls. Australia articulated its core governance expectations through the AI Ethics Principles (2019) and the AI Action Plan (2021), and more recently through the National AI Plan announced in 2025.[18] The latter includes plans to establish an AI safety institute and to implement the Guidance for AI Adoption (2025), which emphasizes accountability, transparency, and human oversight across the AI lifecycle.[19] Notably, the National AI Plan does not follow through with earlier plans for more stringent binding regulation. Rather than creating a standalone regulatory regime, these measures are designed to encourage AI adoption while integrating AI guidelines into existing legal and sectoral frameworks.

This governance structure supports flexibility and lowers barriers to innovation by allowing organizations discretion in how governance expectations are implemented. At the same time, reliance on voluntary guidance and sectoral integration may result in uneven uptake, variable compliance, and challenges for consistent risk management across domains, particularly as AI systems become more complex and widely deployed. Australia also demonstrates the evolving approaches to AI governance of Indo-Pacific countries as they look to develop their AI capabilities. The National AI Plan is moving toward guidance-based governance and existing frameworks rather than introducing new binding regulation.

Assessing the Impact of Diverging Indo-Pacific AI Governance Approaches

Over the past five years, AI governance across the Indo-Pacific has evolved along increasingly divergent structural lines. While nearly all jurisdictions recognize AI as a critical technology whose strategic and economic impact justifies the development and implementation of a range of governance measures, the ways in which governments organize authority, enforce norms, and engage with stakeholders vary considerably. These differences lie less in whether AI is regulated and more in how institutional structures shape innovation pathways, regulatory clarity, and risk anticipation.

Some jurisdictions, such as China and South Korea, have adopted more centralized, top-down governance models, characterized by extensive oversight, binding legal frameworks, and early-stage intervention. These structures tend to enhance the state’s capacity to manage system-level risks and align AI development with national priorities. In particular, China’s emphasis on pre-deployment registration and data legality exemplifies how centralized authority can emphasize uniform standards and regulatory coherence. However, top-down models may also constrain flexibility, particularly for smaller firms or research initiatives operating outside priority sectors. By concentrating decision-making within government institutions, these systems risk reducing pluralism and limiting the capacity of governance regimes to adapt to new risks and capabilities as AI evolves.

In contrast, countries such as Japan and Australia have favored multi-stakeholder models that rely on coordination, guidance, and voluntary adoption. These approaches offer greater adaptability and reduce barriers to innovation by allowing actors more discretion in how compliance is achieved. Japan’s emphasis on soft law and institutional coordination fosters agility and sectoral experimentation. These regulatory structures face limitations in addressing high-risk applications, however, particularly as AI systems scale in complexity and scope. The absence of binding obligations could lead to inconsistent uptake, fragmented accountability, and difficulties in early-stage risk identification.

Other jurisdictions, including Singapore and India, represent hybrid or transitional models that do not fit neatly on the spectrum between the two models outlined above. Singapore combines soft law with unusually strong interagency alignment, creating a highly coordinated form of multi-stakeholder governance. India, while currently pursuing light-touch regulation and lacking the governance capacity of Singapore, is gradually moving from a capacity-building and inclusion-first strategy toward more formal oversight, particularly through its data-protection legislation and emerging institutional frameworks. These hybrid approaches illustrate how different governance systems do not fall neatly into one category or another but are developed to fit the unique political, cultural, and economic context of a specific country.

Beyond national differences, AI governance in the Indo-Pacific is increasingly shaped by transnational influences and the evolving nature of AI technologies themselves. Regulatory models circulate through regional cooperation, international standards, multilateral institutions, and policy emulation, contributing to a shared but uneven governance landscape. At the same time, rapid advances in generative and foundational AI systems are testing the limits of existing frameworks and prompting institutional adaptation. Governance is not a static mechanism imposed on technology; it is part of a co-evolving system, reshaped by the risks and capacities of the technologies it seeks to govern. As countries navigate this shifting environment, structural choices between centralization and coordination and between binding law and flexible guidance reflect deeper choices about regulatory clarity, innovation potential, and the capacity to anticipate emerging risks. AI governance in the Indo-Pacific is best understood not as a choice between fixed models, but as a set of structural trade-offs that inform how states respond to technological uncertainty.

Ryan Calo is the Virginia and Prentice Bloedel Professor at the University of Washington.

Xinzi Lyu is a PhD candidate in law at Xiamen University.
