The Cybersecurity Case for Korean Cloud Reform
Financial Sector Reforms Are the Model to Follow
by Nigel Cory
July 14, 2026

Nigel Cory examines the cybersecurity and resilience risks of South Korea’s cloud regime architecture in comparison to schemes of other developed countries. He concludes that South Korea should pursue policy reforms that would align Korean public-sector cloud cybersecurity with the cybersecurity logic that the country’s Financial Services Commission has now embraced.

South Korea’s cloud cybersecurity regime for the financial and public sectors includes restrictive policies that effectively exclude U.S. and other cloud-based service providers—and by extension the cybersecurity tools powered by artificial intelligence (AI) they deliver—through requirements for physical network separation. These requirements mandate the use of dedicated, local data centers and keep internal business networks physically separated from the internet. In the financial sector, until recent reforms, this was mandated by the Electronic Financial Transactions Act. For networks the public sector operates, South Korea’s cloud security certification scheme (formerly known as the Cloud Security Assurance Program, or CSAP) requires workloads at the medium tier and above—those that process personal or nonpublic government information—to be hosted on physically separated cloud infrastructure dedicated exclusively to government use. Both regimes are under pressure to change given the growing role of cloud services in delivering the frontier AI capabilities that modern cybersecurity depends on.

Both regimes reflect a traditional South Korean cybersecurity approach that often diverges from global standards and undermines best-in-class cybersecurity practices. Another example is that South Korean policies require firms to only store data locally (a concept known as data localization), which negatively affects thirteen of the fourteen information security controls in ISO/IEC 27002, the leading international information security standard.[1] In the cloud space, South Korea has continuously treated physical isolation as the default control for sensitive cybersecurity environments, despite a global shift toward allowing public and critical service sectors to use globally distributed cloud services.

South Korean financial and public-sector cloud regimes are changing or under pressure to change due to the growing impact that frontier AI models are having on cybersecurity. On May 25, 2026, Korea’s Financial Services Commission (FSC) announced eligibility for large institutions to apply for a one-year exemption from physical network separation to deploy AI-driven cybersecurity defenses, with the potential for the exemption to become permanent.[2] The decision rests on an explicit acknowledgment that physical separation cannot defend against modern cyberthreats driven by AI and that the defensive tools required to counter them depend on continuous cloud connectivity. FSC vice chair Kwon Dae-young framed the position directly: advanced AI threats cannot be walled off; they can only be managed through continuously updated cloud-connected defense. In contrast, while the former CSAP regime is undergoing reforms (consolidating oversight under the National Intelligence Service, or NIS), these changes do not include removing physical separation requirements at the medium tier and above.

This commentary examines the architectural and operational implications of the divergence between the two regimes; the comparative position of South Korea relative to allied cloud cybersecurity frameworks in the United States, Canada, Japan, Germany, and Australia; and the policy reforms that would align Korean public-sector cloud cybersecurity with the cybersecurity logic the country’s FSC has now embraced for the financial sector.

South Korea’s Problematic Physical Separation Requirements

South Korea has mandated a cloud model that the rest of the world—particularly countries facing significant threats to cyber resilience—has spent a decade moving away from, with requirements for the public sector and financial services to use physically separated local cloud facilities instead of multi-tenant, globally connected cloud services. Both the financial services and public-sector cloud requirements are undergoing reforms, but only the former will remove the physical separation requirement and therefore meaningfully enhance cybersecurity outcomes.

Financial sector. South Korea’s financial sector has operated under physical network separation regulations since 2014, when the Financial Supervisory Service implemented rules under the Electronic Financial Transactions Act requiring financial institutions to physically isolate their internal business networks from any network connected to the internet.[3] On May 25, 2026, South Korea’s FSC temporarily relaxed these long-standing requirements for the financial sector due to the urgent need for organizations to use the latest cloud-based AI tools to defend against cyberthreats. Up to 49 qualified financial institutions—those subject to Electronic Financial Transactions Act requirements, with assets above 10 trillion won and at least 1,000 employees—will be eligible for the exemption, which allows them to use cloud-based AI tools for cybersecurity purposes. The exemption is only for one year and for this limited set of firms, but the FSC is reviewing a full lifting of the rules for firms with sophisticated security and AI capabilities—which suggests that the FSC might make it permanent and broader.[4] This is the most significant single reform in a series that began with the FSC’s August 2024 Network Separation Improvement Roadmap and continued with the April 2026 Enforcement Rules amendment opening Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) for administrative and back-office functions.[5]

The FSC’s decision is an explicit acknowledgment that physically separated networks are not capable of defending against modern threats and that best-in-class AI tools cannot be deployed within existing network regulations. The practical effect of the FSC’s decision is to open Korean financial sector cybersecurity workloads to U.S. and other foreign cloud service providers, as well as the AI and cybersecurity services that depend on them, for the first time since the 2014 network separation regime took effect.

Even with South Korea’s long-term attachment to physical separation requirements, this initial exemption will reinforce itself as it takes hold. Deploying cloud-based security tools requires a financial firm to first stand up a working cloud environment—accounts, network architecture, identity and access controls, encryption, and monitoring—and that buildout is identical whether the workload on top of it is a security tool, a banking application, or a customer database. The competence Korean firms acquire to host the new security tools transfers to any other workload they later put on the same infrastructure, eroding the institutional barrier that has reinforced the network separation regime as much as the regulation itself. Thus, a narrow exception for AI-powered security tools builds the operational foundation and regulatory precedent that makes broader, permanent adoption of cloud tools progressively easier over the next two to four years.

Public sector. The CSAP was established in 2016 under the Act on the Development of Cloud Computing and Protection of Users and was administered by the Korea Internet and Security Agency (KISA) under the Ministry of Science and ICT. The CSAP’s public-sector workloads are classified into three tiers—low, medium, and high—based on data sensitivity and institutional importance. Public institutions are also classified into three groups—group A (central administrative agencies), group B (significant public corporations), and group C (committees under central agencies, basic local governments, and schools). Anything containing personal information—even at the local government level—will be classified above the low tier. A decade after launch, the CSAP had 202 active certificates.[6]

The medium tier of South Korea’s CSAP requires physical separation of servers and storage for the generation, storage, and processing of public-sector workloads classified at the moderate level. This means government cloud workloads be hosted on physically separated infrastructure dedicated exclusively to government use. According to industry estimates, the majority of Korean public-sector workloads—spanning central administrative agencies, public corporations, local governments, and schools—are classified at the medium tier or above.[7] These moderate workloads cover a huge range of the controlled, but unclassified, information that flows through government systems on a day-to-day basis, including citizens’ personal, financial, and health information, such as tax records, benefit and pension data, immigration and identification systems, employee and veteran records, healthcare data processed by federal programs, and financial transactions.

South Korea is considering CSAP reforms, but—in direct contrast to the FSC—this does not appear to include changes to physical separation requirements.[8] The proposed reform consolidates the existing dual-review process—CSAP certification by KISA at the Ministry of Science and ICT alongside a separate security review by the NIS—into a single NIS-led framework aligned with the broader National Network Security Framework (N2SF), which was launched in September 2025. The stated rationale is procedural simplification. While the N2SF’s stated objective is to move Korean public-sector security from physical network separation toward risk-based tiered controls,[9] according to U.S. Congresswoman Carol Miller’s March 2026 letter to U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, the NIS intends to preserve physical separation at the medium tier under the successor framework.[10] If that holds, the architectural exclusion of multi-tenant cloud services, foreign cloud hyperscalers above the low tier, and cloud-connected AI defensive tools remains in place regardless of which agency administers the certification.

Physical Separation Requirements Weaken the Cybersecurity and Resilience Benefits of Cloud Services

This contrasts with the best practice of multi-tenant cloud services, where customers share the same underlying physical infrastructure, with software-enforced isolation (known as logical separation), keeping each tenant’s data and workloads invisible and inaccessible to every other tenant. Because of its unique physical separation requirements, South Korea’s prohibition on multi-tenant cloud architecture in the public sector is unique among the cloud cybersecurity certification schemes used in developed economies, including the United States, Canada, Japan, Germany, and Australia.

Physical separation fundamentally alters the economic and security logic of cloud-based services, exposing South Korean government and critical infrastructure providers to cybersecurity and resilience risks. Cloud computing differs from traditional IT infrastructure, where an organization buys or rents its own servers, storage, and networking equipment and installs this equipment in its own building. The organization’s IT department is responsible for managing the equipment and the services it runs, including managing security access and patches and handling backups. In contrast, cloud services allow organizations to rent computing capacity from a cloud provider and access it over the internet. The cloud provider owns the underlying servers, storage, and networking, distributed across data centers worldwide. The organization pays only for what it uses, scales capacity up or down on demand, and lets the cloud provider handle the hardware, electricity, cooling, security patching, and component replacement.

The globally distributed nature of cloud computing provides several key advantages, especially as it relates to cybersecurity and resilience, which are important in South Korea given its physical separation requirements, its past experiences with data center incidents, the state-sponsored cyberattacks it faces, and the potential for military conflict on the Korean Peninsula. Physical separation forecloses cloud service advantages: it concentrates infrastructure geographically, severs the cross-customer telemetry that makes cloud cybersecurity so effective, and replicates the brittleness of traditional on-premises IT.

Physical separation creates critical cybersecurity vulnerabilities compared with cloud services. A single organization that uses and manages on-premises IT or local, isolated data center services sees only what happens inside its own systems, while a cloud provider sees what happens across millions of customers, billions of devices, and trillions of events per day. Modern cyberattacks use AI-driven techniques—automated reconnaissance, generated phishing, and adaptive malware—that the defensive tools designed to counter them (behavioral analytics, real-time threat intelligence, and machine-learning detection) cannot effectively address inside physically separated environments as the tools themselves are cloud based (namely SaaS) and depend on global connectivity. A customer connects to the SaaS vendor’s cloud over the internet, transfers its own telemetry to it, and receives detections, alerts, and response actions back. Best-in-class cybersecurity services depend on the cloud, in part, as the threat intelligence feeds that drive prevention are time-sensitive in ways that batched cybersecurity updates cannot replicate. Physical separation prohibits the internet connectivity that the entire delivery model assumes.

Global cloud service providers are also more resilient as they do not provide single points of potential IT failure and they have automated “failover,” so that if something happens to one data center (e.g., a fire or a military attack), the workload running on it automatically shifts to another datacenter. The example of Ukraine is particularly noteworthy: facing Russian missile strikes on data centers, Ukraine migrated government records to the cloud in 2022, with then minister of digital transformation Mykhailo Fedorov observing that “Russian missiles can’t destroy the cloud.”[11] Recent Iranian attacks impacting data centers in the United Arab Emirates reinforce the same pattern of risk to regional physical infrastructure. In contrast, the September 2025 fire at South Korea’s National Information Resources Service data center in Daejeon paralyzed 647 government services, took weeks to restore, and exposed how a single physical failure can collapse the country’s digital government.[12]

These resilience risks compound in South Korea’s case because the country faces a state-sponsored cyberthreat environment more intense than almost any other in the world. The NIS reports approximately 1.3 million cyberattack attempts per day against South Korean public-sector systems, with roughly 80% attributed to North Korea.[13] The NIS is responsible for defending Korean critical infrastructure across both public and private sectors from these threats, and yet it remains one of the key proponents of the regulatory restrictions that undermine South Korea’s defenses.

The Uniquely Restrictive and Closed Korean Public-Sector Cloud Scheme

In addition to the technical challenges outlined above, the CSAP is a market access barrier, as it is clearly more restrictive and discriminatory than other major public-sector cloud cybersecurity programs. U.S. cloud service providers (AWS, Google, and Microsoft) only hold CSAP certification at the low tier, within group C services, and for infrastructure-as-a-service. The CSAP’s discriminatory treatment of foreign firms is clear when compared with how the United States, Canada, Japan, and Australia handle their equivalent low- and moderate-sensitivity public cloud workloads. The more limited number, type, and source of providers show that Korean public agencies do not have access to the number and variety of technology providers.

The moderate level of the U.S. Federal Risk and Authorization Management Program (FedRAMP) does not require data localization and fully permits multi-tenant infrastructure with logical separation between tenants. The U.S. National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) authoritative technical guidance—Special Publication 800-53, which underpins FedRAMP and many allied cloud cybersecurity programs—treats physical separation between networks and logical separation between networks as two ways of accomplishing the same security objective, with neither being presented as more secure than the other.[14] FedRAMP’s moderate tier accounts for 359 of 519 authorized packages (69% of the total), and foreign-headquartered firms participate at this tier without restriction, including from Australia, Canada, Israel, Germany, and elsewhere. The United States only mandates physical separation in the Department of Defense’s Cloud Computing Security Requirements Guide at impact level 5, which is reserved for unclassified national security systems.[15]

Canada’s GC Cloud Framework Agreements takes the same approach: 77% of its 1,868 approved services are rated “protected B.” Canada’s moderate-level equivalent allows multi-tenant infrastructure operated by U.S. hyperscalers and other foreign firms.[16] Germany’s C5 imposes no multi-tenant prohibition.[17] Australia’s Information Security Manual and its companion publication explicitly endorse multi-tenant cloud architecture at the protected classification—Australia’s highest unclassified tier—covering most Australian government, healthcare, and education data.[18]

Japan’s Information System Security Management and Assessment Program (ISMAP), while structured as a binary rather than a three-tier system, covers what FedRAMP would call the moderate tier through its main ISMAP track. It authorizes multi-tenet cloud services.[19] Approximately 40% of ISMAP’s 96 registered services come from foreign providers, including AWS, Google, Microsoft, Oracle, Salesforce, and a growing wave of SaaS platforms such as Snowflake, Databricks, Zendesk, Cloudflare, and Splunk (which were all added in 2024–26). ISMAP authorizes the multi-tenant cloud from various U.S. providers.

Options for South Korea to Pursue Broad Cloud and Cybersecurity Reforms

The May 25 FSC decision has shifted the analytical ground regarding cloud cybersecurity regulations in South Korea, with implications for the future of the CSAP. South Korea should use this moment to enact broad reforms that more effectively advance the country’s cybersecurity objectives. The oft-used Korean argument that physical separation was a non-negotiable national security issue always lacked technical merit. After May 25 it is clear that even South Korea recognizes this is no longer tenable as a cybersecurity or trade policy position either. South Korean regulators should extend the FSC’s logic to the CSAP and bring it into alignment with international cloud and cybersecurity best practices.

The following substantive policy steps describe what a pathway to meaningful CSAP reform could look like:

South Korea should remove the requirement for physical separation of cloud servers and storage at the medium tier. The CSAP should instead follow the United States’ FedRAMP, Japan’s ISMAP, Canada’s GC Cloud, Germany’s C5, and IRAP (Information Security Registered Assessors Program) in permitting multi-tenant cloud services with logical separation at the medium- or moderate-impact tier. Elevated controls should be reserved for the small subset of workloads that genuinely warrant dedicated infrastructure.
South Korea should modernize its data localization requirements. Frameworks in other like-minded countries permit data residency at the option of the contracting agency for the most sensitive workloads rather than as a blanket requirement across an entire impact tier. The CSAP should follow that pattern.
South Korea should recognize alliance status as a positive trust indicator in the CSAP. For example, Germany’s IT Security Act 2.0 distinguishes between NATO and non-NATO supply chain sources. South Korea continues to treat U.S. cloud providers as untrusted entrants in the public-sector market, despite the U.S.-Korea Mutual Defense Treaty, integrated military operations on the Korean Peninsula, and intelligence-sharing arrangements that operate at the highest levels of both governments.
Conclusion

South Korea’s FSC acknowledged what the global cybersecurity community has argued for years: physical network separation is the wrong answer to cyberthreats, particularly modern AI-driven ones, and the defensive tools required to counter them need the cloud. The CSAP’s public-sector regulators face the same threat environment and the same defensive tools. Substantive CSAP reform is in South Korea’s self-interest on cybersecurity, economic, and alliance grounds.

The Trump administration is pressing South Korea to open the CSAP to U.S. cloud providers as part of broader trade negotiations. U.S. Congresswoman Miller’s March 2026 letter to U.S. Trade Representative Greer formally identified the CSAP as a structural trade barrier requiring removal of physical separation at the medium tier. While South Korean officials hopefully recognize the better pathway presented by the FSC’s models, the United States should also work to press for substantive CSAP reform—not just procedural reform that leaves the underlying market access barriers in place.

Nigel Cory is a Director at Crowell Global Advisors and a Nonresident Fellow at the National Bureau of Asian Research.
