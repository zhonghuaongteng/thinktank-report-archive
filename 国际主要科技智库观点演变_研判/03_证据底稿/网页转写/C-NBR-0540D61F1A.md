The Road Ahead for AI in Vietnam
Why Implementation of Its Law on AI Will Determine Competitiveness
by Nigel Cory
February 27, 2026

Nigel Cory argues that Vietnam should pause and recalibrate implementation of its new AI law so that rules are proportionate, workable, and focused on genuine risk rather than broad, process-heavy compliance.

Vietnam is rapidly approaching a critical fork in the road on artificial intelligence (AI) as its new AI law and implementing framework take effect. The Law on AI (recently enacted on December 10, 2025) establishes Vietnam’s national framework, while the government’s implementing package includes a prime ministerial decision to define the list of high-risk AI systems and a decree to operationalize compliance procedures, all slated to take effect on March 1. The law establishes the destination—safer, more trusted AI, and AI competitiveness—but the decree and decision determine the road Vietnam takes to get there. With this package, Vietnam joins a small group of jurisdictions, including the European Union (AI Act 2024) and South Korea (AI Basic Act 2024), with a comprehensive AI law.

The choice at this juncture is whether Vietnam takes time to build a practical, proportionate implementation framework that supports AI adoption, investment, and innovation, or moves ahead with a more prescriptive approach that imposes broad compliance burdens before global standards and implementation guidance have matured. The latest draft decree (February 24, 2026) includes several improvements from earlier drafts, suggesting that it could continue to be revised further. However, the draft currently still includes many provisions that point Vietnam too far in the direction of a more prescriptive approach, which would undercut the country’s ambition to lead on AI development and deployment in Southeast Asia.

Vietnam’s implementing regulations have several distinct features that set them apart from emerging global practices. As published for consultation, this framework gives the implementing rules enormous practical importance: they define which systems fall into the high-risk category and what registration, documentation, reporting, labeling, and disclosure obligations follow. If those triggers are arbitrary, low, or broad and the accompanying procedures overly prescriptive, the result is not just stricter regulation; it is higher fixed costs, slower deployment, and reduced access to AI tools, particularly for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) and local AI developers. Vietnam’s private sector has expressed similar concerns, with the Vietnam Chamber of Commerce and Industry stating that excessive rigidity could slow innovation. The February 24 draft decree partially addresses some of these risks by building in some proportionality and simplified record-keeping requirements for SMEs. But it also confirms how much of the real compliance burden still depends on how the high-risk list is drawn, which high-risk systems require third-party certification, and how broadly medium-risk systems are treated for notification, transparency, and labeling.

These regulations require careful consideration and calibration as they will have a significant impact on the country’s digital development. AI is poised to reshape virtually every sector of the Vietnamese economy in different ways—from manufacturing and logistics to finance, healthcare, education, and public services. This highlights the risks of imposing broad, prescriptive, and restrictive requirements horizontally across a range of sectors and use cases while the technology, technical standards, and implementation practices are still evolving. Vietnamese AI providers have highlighted that detailed technical requirements should not be hard-coded into laws and regulations while the technology and standards are still moving. Vietnam should consider whether its economic and innovation interests are best served by rapidly implementing high-impact AI rules before meaningful consultation and practical guidance have clarified the trade-offs of different approaches.

Vietnam’s approach draws on an existing risk-based AI governance model—that of the EU—but significantly extends it in implementation design: broader high-risk triggers, more prescriptive operational requirements, and a faster, heavier rollout across a wider set of systems. That combination—not the existence of regulation itself—is what risks setting Vietnam apart from the EU and emerging best practice on AI elsewhere around the world. If Vietnam proceeds with the decree and decision in their current form, it will move unusually quickly to apply a heavy implementation regime across a wide range of AI systems.

Other jurisdictions with comprehensive AI frameworks have generally paired passage with phased implementation and extensive secondary guidance—codes of practice, standards, and regulator playbooks—precisely because the compliance architecture has to mature alongside the technology. For example, the EU AI Act entered into force on August 1, 2024, but has phased implementation through 2027. Reflecting implementation challenges, the European Commission proposed adjusting the timing of high-risk AI obligations under its Digital Omnibus package so they are linked to the availability of standards and other support tools, rather than assuming all compliance infrastructure will be ready on the original schedule. South Korea’s AI Basic Act similarly included a one-year transition period and a consultation-based enforcement decree process.

Beyond the fact that Vietnam’s draft framework is being rushed into effect on a much faster timeline than most comparable jurisdictions, the core substantive problem still begins with how “high risk” is defined and operationalized. Earlier drafts of the decree relied on broad, automatic, prescriptive, and arbitrary low-threshold triggers for labeling systems high-risk. Under this approach, general-purpose AI systems—including recommendation engines, chatbots, content generators, and productivity tools—would have been treated as high-risk based on user counts or geographic reach rather than use-case-specific risk. This is a poor proxy for harm. A chatbot used across two provinces is not inherently high-risk simply because it scales. Risk depends on the use case, the sector, the decision being made, and the safeguards in place—not a headcount or a map. These designations went further than regulations such as the EU’s AI Act and South Korea’s Basic AI Law by categorizing ordinary, low-risk systems as high-risk and by relying on automatic numeric thresholds not in line with actual risk-related realities.

High-risk designation is now anchored in the accompanying prime minister’s decision, which is built on criteria that include level of impact, use in essential sectors, and scope of impact. But in essence, scale remains a proxy for risk. The latest draft decree also tries to set out exclusion principles to keep low-risk systems out of the high-risk tier, such as systems used internally, systems with meaningful human override, systems still in controlled testing, and systems that only provide advice rather than making final decisions.

Without careful calibration, these designations will sweep up a lot of AI systems. For example, in manufacturing and logistics, firms may use AI for quality control, predictive maintenance, routing, or inventory forecasting—tools that are often low-risk in function but high-value for productivity. If these systems are swept into high-risk compliance requirements because of scale thresholds (i.e., the fact that they are useful to a large number of manufacturers across multiple Vietnamese provinces) rather than actual use-case risk, adoption will slow precisely where Vietnam stands to gain most from diffusion.

The distinction for high-risk AI is not technical nitpicking. It goes to the heart of whether Vietnam’s AI framework will support broad adoption or unintentionally suppress it. Overly broad high-risk classification raises fixed compliance costs across a large share of ordinary tools. That can reduce the range of AI products available in the market, slow enterprise experimentation, and discourage integration into core business functions. Lower availability and slower deployment, in turn, reduce adoption by firms, especially small ones that lack the resources to absorb delays, customization costs, or legal review. This can reduce the number of AI products in the market, which could slow productivity gains associated with the widespread adoption of AI across the broader economy.

As drafted, the decree and decision risk compounding the impact of capturing so much AI with a sweeping set of burdensome obligations, such as registration, pre-market notification, technical documentation, and conformity assessments. Applied narrowly and proportionately, these steps and tools could support accountability. Applied broadly, they could raise fixed costs, discourage investment, limit local experimentation, and delay or block the deployment of leading global AI tools and models. Vietnamese business voices have already urged regulators to avoid unnecessary administrative barriers, especially for startups and smaller firms. The February 24 draft decree makes two useful changes in this direction: it states that notification is not a licensing procedure, and it narrows and clarifies (voluntary and third-party) conformity assessment requirements for high-risk systems. But considerable risks remain if high-risk is defined broadly in the prime minister’s list of systems, as conformity assessments can become a de facto barrier to market entry if assessment bodies, testing capacity, and recognition rules are not ready.

The draft decree also uses highly prescriptive content-labeling and disclosure mandates, including rules on label placement, visibility, and notification frequency across audio, video, and image formats. For example, short AI-generated audio would require spoken notices, with repeated notices for longer clips. This approach is surely well-intentioned, aiming to address concerns about misleading AI-generated content, but it risks degrading user experience and fostering “banner blindness,” where users ignore warnings because they are ubiquitous or not practically relevant—thereby undermining the actual goal of the policy. For example, Australia’s official guidance on AI transparency emphasizes a flexible mix of labeling, watermarking, and metadata recording, selected based on context and impact, while the EU AI Act’s Article 50 focuses on machine-readable, detectable marking and technically effective, interoperable solutions. The European Commission is also developing a code of practice to support implementation rather than imposing mandates before this set of supporting technical documents can be developed by regulators in consultation with experts and industry. The February 24 draft decree strengthens some parts of Vietnam’s approach by requiring technical marking for AI-generated content in machine-readable form (e.g., metadata, signatures, and watermarking), while treating technical marking for text as voluntary. At the same time, it still prescribes display-label formats in specific high-confusion contexts—such as AI content that simulates real people or recreates real events—which risks sliding back into user interface micromanagement.

Transparency is another area where Vietnam’s approach to implementation diverges from global practice. As drafted, the regulations could permit potentially broad access to information about AI systems without clear legal justification, reviews, or limits. The draft does not clearly tie the disclosure obligation to a defined legal trigger or cause; it relies on a broad standard allowing requests for “clarification” when linked to a requester’s “lawful rights and interests.” This could create a heavy administrative burden for AI providers. It also raises concerns about commercial confidentiality. While the draft decree includes guardrails—stating providers need not disclose source code, model weights, or trade secrets—it also contemplates that upon a “lawful request” providers may need to provide or publicize information, including a high-level description of system logic and decision-making. This process should be more detailed and carefully managed to avoid creating an administrative burden. Regulators should have full access for oversight, but disclosure to nongovernmental requesters should first be reviewed by a government agency and limited to clearly defined legal grounds and a demonstrated, case-specific need.

Incident reporting is similarly vulnerable to over-design. Earlier drafts of the decree mandated disclosures within 24 hours for “serious incidents” involving high-risk AI systems. This may sound reasonable, but it is operationally impractical for complex systems. AI troubleshooting typically requires triage, scoping, and root-cause analysis over several days. The February 24 draft decree moves toward a more workable approach by setting preliminary reporting deadlines of 48 hours (high-risk systems) and 72 hours (medium-risk systems). These deadlines are more reasonable and in line with international approaches (such as the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice), which generally require reporting “without undue delay,” often using staged reporting (initial notice followed by updates as facts are confirmed). Vietnam should align with international best practice to set comparable reporting timelines to reduce ambiguity and discourage defensive over-reporting.

Vietnam went beyond global frameworks in early drafts of the decree by proposing that AI deployed in “important applications” be hosted on local AI infrastructure, including local data storage, subject to agency-granted exceptions. This would have disrupted the cross-border, cloud-based architecture that underpins modern AI development and deployment. It would also act as a potentially broad digital trade barrier. The February 24 draft decree moderated this language. Instead, it establishes an infrastructure prioritization framework for “important AI applications” in essential fields—anchored to another prime minister’s list—that prioritizes use of state-invested infrastructure and infrastructure in Vietnam. The decree also encourages combined domestic and foreign cloud deployment, allowing overseas cloud processing for nonrestricted data (based on risk assessments). However, it further requires organizations running “important applications” on foreign cloud services to submit a conversion plan to limit this use.

Even with the improvements in the February 24 draft decree, Vietnam’s implementing decree and decision would raise costs, reduce AI adoption, and slow economy-wide innovation. They could also create unnecessary trade friction and function as barriers to market access for global AI tools and models. In the worst case, they could prompt AI developers and deployers to scale back plans for, delay launches in, or avoid altogether the Vietnamese market. The burden would fall disproportionately on smaller domestic firms, which are least able to absorb compliance costs but are most important to a vibrant local AI ecosystem.

The EU’s competitiveness and innovation challenges—including the need to reduce barriers that prevent innovative firms from scaling and to make processes less bureaucratic—are now well documented, most notably in Mario Draghi’s September 2024 report on European competitiveness. Vietnam risks importing the same problems if it locks in prescriptive AI requirements on an implementation timeline not matched by any other jurisdiction with similarly comprehensive regulation. The country should pause and recalibrate implementation of its AI law—through the decree and related guidance—so that rules are proportionate, workable, and focused on genuine risk rather than broad, process-heavy compliance. AI’s rapid advance could deliver significant gains in productivity and growth for Vietnam. The destination—safer, more trusted AI and stronger national competitiveness in AI—is the right one. The question is whether Vietnam takes a practical, innovation-oriented road to get there or a more rigid path that slows AI adoption, investment, and innovation.

Nigel Cory is a Director at Crowell Global Advisors and a Nonresident Fellow at the National Bureau of Asian Research.
