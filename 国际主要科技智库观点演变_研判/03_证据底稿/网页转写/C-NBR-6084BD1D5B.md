South Korea’s Approach to AI Governance
Mitigating Risks and Ensuring AI Safety
by Myuhng-Joo Kim
March 9, 2026

Myuhng-Joo Kim explains how South Korea’s AI Basic Act, which came into full effect in January 2026, prioritizes AI technology development and industrial promotion, while also specifying measures to protect citizens from AI-related risks and to enhance trust in AI.

Policy-level governance for the development of artificial intelligence technology and the establishment of a new AI ecosystem in South Korea began in earnest in 2016. That year, the Go match between AlphaGo and grandmaster Lee Sedol not only attracted global attention but also delivered a tremendous shock to the Korean public. Parents, businesses, and government alike began formulating responses to the rapid development of AI. Until then, AI research in South Korea had been carried out mainly by individual researchers or teams within universities and research institutes. However, that event triggered the establishment of mid- to long-term plans at the governmental level, along with the creation of new organizations and systems for AI technology development and industrial promotion. Importantly, alongside the emphasis on technological progress, a recognition emerged that mitigating the side effects and negative impacts of AI through governance mechanisms was equally essential. This led to the establishment of the AI Ethics Research Committee. I served as chair of this committee, and after two years of research we released Korea’s first AI ethics guidelines, the “Seoul PACT,” in March 2018.[1]

Since then, even as ruling and opposition parties have alternated in power, AI technology development and industry promotion have consistently remained a top priority on South Korea’s political agenda. In December 2020 the government published the second version of the AI ethics guidelines.[2] The guidelines are a multilayered framework that places the highest value on “human-centered AI,” which is grounded in three core principles and articulated through ten key requirements, including human rights, fairness, safety, accountability, and transparency. Building on this foundation, the government released domain-specific guidelines for AI developers and has consistently carried out AI ethics impact assessments based on UNESCO’s methodology.

South Korea’s AI Basic Act

South Korea’s AI governance is most clearly embodied in the AI Basic Act, passed by the National Assembly in December 2024. After the legislative announcement, a one-year preparation period was carried out for the implementation of the framework. During this time, detailed enforcement decrees were drafted, and numerous notifications and specific guidelines were prepared. These were revised through public hearings with a wide range of stakeholders. In particular, to reduce the regulatory burden on companies and provide sufficient time for preparation, a one-year grace period was introduced before the AI Basic Act went into full effect in January 2026. To fill the gap created by this regulatory deferral, a master plan for building a national AI safety ecosystem was announced.

The AI Basic Act prioritizes AI technology development and industrial promotion, while also specifying measures to protect citizens from AI-related risks and to enhance trust in AI. Among the new organizations established under the legislation, the highest-level governance body is the National AI Strategy Committee, chaired by the president and composed of cabinet ministers and civilian members. This body serves both as an advisory and decision-making committee. Major policies are debated, approved, and implemented immediately through this mechanism. With the inauguration of a new government in 2025, securing AI competitiveness has become a top national policy priority, and the authority and role of the National AI Strategy Committee have been further strengthened.

Two other noteworthy organizations formally established by the AI Basic Act are the AI Policy Center and the Korea AI Safety Institute (AISI). The AI Policy Center is tasked with developing comprehensive AI policies and promoting the establishment and dissemination of international norms. In contrast, AISI is defined in Article 12 of the AI Basic Act as follows: “The Minister of Science and ICT may operate an AI Safety Institute to perform tasks professionally and efficiently to secure AI safety, protecting people’s lives, physical well-being, property, etc., from risks associated with AI and maintaining a foundation of trust in an AI society.” The legislation further specifies seven main functions of the institute: (1) definition and analysis of AI safety-related risks, (2) research on AI safety policy, (3) research on AI safety evaluation criteria and methods, (4) research on AI safety technologies and standardization, (5) international exchange and cooperation related to AI safety, (6) support for securing the safety of AI systems pursuant to Article 32 (frontier AI), and (7) other tasks related to AI safety as prescribed by presidential decree

Korea AI Safety Institute

Korea AISI was initially established even before the AI Basic Act was introduced. Following the London AI Safety Summit in November 2023, which sparked a global trend among leading countries in AI research and adoption to create national AI safety institutes, South Korea in November 2024 became the sixth country in the world to do so. Since then, Korea AISI has actively participated in the AISI International Network, which includes institutes from ten countries and is structured into three collaborative tracks for joint international research: Track 1 conducts joint research on deep fakes. Track 2 focuses on multi-language and multicultural testing, cybersecurity testing, and Agent AI safety testing, with joint testing conducted among participating institutes. Korea AISI has been fully engaged in these initiatives. Track 3 carries out research on methods for identifying and evaluating AI risks.

Beyond its engagement in international AISI research and dialogues, Korea AISI essentially serves as the portal and hub for AI safety in the country. The institute has introduced a new approach to defining AI safety problems, moving beyond the conventional method of classifying and storing new risk instances in categories or databases. Instead, Korea AISI employs a risk-mapping methodology that analyzes the causes of a risk, identifies its impacts, and assigns responsibility for policy measures to mitigate the risk to the relevant government ministry.

For causal analysis, the Institute uses two connected three-dimensional charts (see Figure 1). The charts follow the MECI (mutually exclusive, collectively inclusive) principle: components are nonoverlapping, and together they form a complete set. On the left-side chart, each of the three axes represent a component of AI risk: risk actor (x-axis), AI life cycle (y-axis), and deficient domain (z-axis). Risk actors include general users, malicious users, tool-based AI, and autonomous AI. The stages of the AI life cycle are design, development, deployment, and use and rollback. Deficient domain encompasses causes such as technical deficiencies, lack of social or cultural acceptance, gaps in policy and regulation, and insufficient industrial preparedness.

Each risk instance mapped in the figure carries a timestamp for when it first occurred or could occur and a severity attribute for impact. When risks are inserted into the risk-mapping system alongside these timestamp and severity characteristics, the system can track dynamics, anticipate potential risks, and enable timely preventive measures. If the actor is human, preventive safety education may be provided in advance. If the actor is autonomous AI such as Agentic AI, guidelines for safe deployment of AI can be predeveloped.

The right-side chart represents responses: the impacted domain (x-axis), the violated AI ethics principle (y-axis), and the responsible ministry (z-axis). As AI technology evolves, AI ethics principles also shift. For example, the rise of generative AI introduced copyright protection as a new principle and reinforced the principle of transparency in response to the emergence of indistinguishable synthetic content. This chart allows policymakers to detect new ethical trends and ensures rapid responses from the appropriate ministries.

The risk map thus serves as the starting point for AI safety evaluation methodologies and directions, another one of Korea AISI’s main functions under the AI Basic Act. Certain risks cannot be technically assessed and instead require social or statistical evaluation, which the institute requests the relevant ministries to conduct. For technically assessable risks, Korea AISI conducts evaluations across multiple dimensions. Based on both internal and external evaluations, the institute regularly publishes comprehensive safety reports. AI safety reports can be issued by evaluating the safety level of an AI model, focusing on the cause side of the risk map. Likewise, a national AI safety report can be issued by assessing the government’s level of response, focusing on the response side of the risk map. These reports reduce public anxiety, provide essential safety information for AI adoption, and inform government decision-making.

Most technical evaluations of AI models require tools, datasets, or evaluation scenarios. In addition to leveraging experience and datasets shared through the AISI International Network, Korea AISI is also conducting research on how South Korea can effectively adopt globally used tools and datasets. Recently, the government launched a competitive process to develop a sovereign large language model that is globally competitive, which is colloquially known as the K-AI initiative. Fifteen corporate consortia applied, and five have been shortlisted. Over the next two and a half years, these five will undergo various evaluations leading to the selection of two finalists. While performance and efficiency are central to these evaluations, safety and trustworthiness assessments—led by Korea AISI—are equally important. To this end, the institute is building a diverse range of specialized datasets.

Additionally, Article 32 of the AI Basic Act assigns Korea AISI the responsibility of ensuring the safety of frontier AI, defined in South Korea as models with accumulated computational power exceeding 10^26 FLOPs. This threshold, which is stricter than that set by the European Union, means that while such models may not yet exist, Korea AISI will soon take on an expanded role as technology advances.

Though less than a year old, Korea AISI has already engaged in international collaboration on AI safety, another primary function of the institute. It has signed memoranda of understanding with France’s nonprofit Safer AI and the U.S. firm Scale AI, has joined the Partnership on AI as a partner member, and is collaborating with the Future of Life Institute to ensure compatibility with the EU AI Act. It is also working with the Association of Southeast Asian Nations, a key emerging player in Indo-Pacific AI governance, to share AI safety expertise and has signed memoranda of understanding with Poland and Singapore to strengthen global AI safety initiatives.

Korea AISI also has its own R&D team, currently working on real-time multi-modal deepfake-detection technologies, which are expected to be deployed on social media platforms via Korean social-networking service companies after the project’s completion. In parallel, the team is researching resilience-focused AI models capable of maintaining normal functionality during external attacks. The “kill switch” function for fully autonomous AI—explicitly mandated in the AI Basic Act—is also being studied as an emergency safeguard.

Korea AISI incorporates AI security within its broader safety mandate. It has established a dual-use task force to address risks related to CBRN-E (chemical, biological, radiological, nuclear, and explosives) weapons. This team includes subject matter experts in chemistry and biology. It has already conducted a week-long workshop on AI security at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory with support from the U.S. State Department, and another workshop was held in South Korea in November 2025. As a nation still technically at war, South Korea treats dual-use risks with the utmost seriousness, addressing national security, cybersecurity, and personal crime within the scope of AI safety.

Korea AISI also leads the AI Safety Consortium, composed of 25 participating institutions from industry and academia. The consortium discusses applications of the AI safety evaluation framework, upgrades global safety reports into Korean-specific versions, sets directions for joint research, and facilitates expert exchanges. In this way, Korea AISI has positioned itself as a key pillar of South Korea’s AI governance framework, connecting policy goals with technical evaluation practices. So far, the institute has made steady and visible progress in establishing the core structures of the national AI safety ecosystem.

Looking Forward: South Korea’s AI Governance under the AI Basic Act

South Korean AI governance is now anchored in the AI Basic Act, which is just now taking effect. Beyond the AI Policy Center and AI Safety Institute, the legislation also mandates the creation of organizations such as an AI data center, AI testbed facilities, AI clusters, a private AI ethics committee, and the Korea AI Promotion Association. Once these organizations are established and activated, South Korea’s AI promotion and governance will become far more visible and concrete. Even prior to the AI Basic Act, however, AI ethics guidelines and mid- to long-term AI development plans had already laid substantial governance foundations. Through Korea AISI, South Korea has advanced global cooperation on AI safety, defined policy directions, and developed unique evaluation frameworks for identifying safety-related risks. Looking forward, the full implementation of the AI Basic Act and better international collaboration through Korea AISI are expected to significantly enhance Korean AI governance.

Myuhng-Joo Kim is the Founding Executive Director of Korea AISI and a leading figure in AI governance and ethics in South Korea. Formerly a professor at Seoul Women’s University, he spearheaded the development of South Korea’s first national AI ethics guidelines, the Seoul PACT. He currently serves as an expert for the OECD’s Global Partnership on AI and as vice president of the Korea Copyright Commission.
