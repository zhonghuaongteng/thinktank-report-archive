The Indo-Pacific as the Epicenter of AI Risk

by Husan Chahal
March 9, 2026

Husan Chahal argues that given the Indo-Pacific’s pivotal role in the global AI landscape, without deliberate action, the same dynamics propelling its growth could also set the stage for the first major AI-related catastrophe.

The Indo-Pacific has become not only the world’s most dynamic region for the development of artificial intelligence but also increasingly its most fragile. Nowhere else do such vast concentrations of computing power, research talent, and strategic rivalry coexist within a patchwork of governance frameworks and infrastructure chokepoints. The same forces fueling the region’s technological rise are also making it a source of global AI instability and an epicenter of AI risk. Understanding this paradox is crucial: the Indo-Pacific will shape not only the pace of AI progress but also the trajectory of its most severe risks, including those that could lead to catastrophic consequences.

An Uneven Race between Innovation and Safety

At the center of the region’s risk profile lies the accelerating U.S.-China race to lead in frontier AI. Both nations view AI as foundational to economic strength and military advantage. Washington’s export controls on advanced chips—despite a recent détente—have pushed Beijing to accelerate its own campaign to become self-sufficient in AI and semiconductors. This has fueled a feedback loop of strategic competition in which each side’s moves to secure an edge intensify the other’s response, leading to tighter controls, faster development, and growing pressure to prioritize speed over safety.

The faster each side moves to achieve breakthroughs in frontier AI development, the greater the temptation to cut corners on model testing, interpretability, and alignment. Both governments have published high-level principles for “trustworthy AI,” yet domestic enforcement and coordination remain limited.[1] Mutual suspicion also makes U.S.-China cooperation on frontier safety research politically costly. What results is a high-stakes, low-trust environment where risk tolerance rises just as model capabilities become more powerful and less predictable.

A Fragmented Governance Landscape

Beyond the two superpowers, the Indo-Pacific’s regulatory patchwork compounds these vulnerabilities. Countries such as Japan, Australia, India, Singapore, and Indonesia are pursuing distinct AI governance models, reflecting their different political systems and economic priorities.

Japan’s approach is industry-led and collaborative, emphasizing innovation and self-regulation.[2] Australia has opted for a risk-based model that combines voluntary safety frameworks for industry with existing digital accountability principles.[3] Singapore’s sandbox testing system prioritizes agile experimentation, while India’s policy statements indicate a preference for minimal intervention to accelerate growth.[4] However, unlike the United States, United Kingdom, or European Union—where formal and ongoing processes on AI safety and governance have taken shape—the Indo-Pacific still lacks sustained, structured dialogues of its own, with most regional discussions remaining ad hoc or shaped primarily by regulatory and normative frameworks developed outside the region.

The diversity in approaches to AI governance reflects the region’s vitality but also its fragility. Without shared safety baselines or coordination mechanisms, even well-intentioned AI deployments can interact unpredictably across borders. A model trained under one regime’s looser content controls might be integrated into another’s defense or critical infrastructure applications without proper safety assurances. Companies might relocate AI development or deployment to jurisdictions with lighter oversight, triggering a regional race to the bottom in safety and transparency standards. The absence of harmonized norms and formal cooperation mechanisms leaves the region exposed to cascading risks that no single government can anticipate or contain.

Compute and Data Chokepoints

The Indo-Pacific’s digital and physical infrastructure deepens these systemic interdependencies and introduces the region to additional risk. The Indo-Pacific hosts several of the world’s most critical compute chokepoints:

Taiwan’s TSMC produces the vast majority of the world’s advanced semiconductors.
South Korea dominates in memory fabrication and semiconductor packaging.
Japan supplies key materials and advanced lithography equipment for semiconductor manufacturing.
Singapore anchors regional data centers and cloud-computing hubs.
India hosts (and supplies) one of the world’s largest pools of semiconductor and AI engineering talent.

This geographic concentration of compute power and inputs critical to the AI tech stack means that disruptions, whether through conflict, natural disaster, or cyberattack, could ripple across the Indo-Pacific and global AI ecosystem. A crisis affecting Taiwan’s semiconductor foundries, for instance, could halt the training of next-generation models worldwide. Yet these physical dependencies are rarely discussed in AI safety frameworks, which tend to focus on algorithmic risk while overlooking the fragility of the underlying infrastructure that sustains AI development.

Japan’s recent “chip diplomacy,” South Korea’s semiconductor alliances, and Singapore’s data-protection agreements demonstrate a growing awareness of these vulnerabilities. But none of these initiatives embed AI safety as a core objective. Additionally, the absence of any meaningful form of compute governance (mechanisms to track, license, or monitor the use of large-scale compute resources) leaves frontier model development largely unobserved and uncoordinated across national boundaries. Taken together, the Indo-Pacific’s concentration of physical compute chokepoints and the lack of cross-border compute governance mean that AI development is both highly fragile and largely unmonitored, creating systemic risks that no single country can manage alone.

Weak Safety Culture in Frontier Development

In addition to vulnerabilities stemming from systemic and geopolitical factors, frontier AI models emerging from the region illustrate how capability can outpace safety. Chinese developers such as Zhipu AI and DeepSeek show frontier capability signals comparable to Western systems, vis-à-vis performance across benchmarks or large model launches. Yet they trail across several key AI safety indices, showing weak safety and transparency practices.[5] Similar patterns appear elsewhere in the Indo-Pacific, where several emerging frontier developers have prioritized rapid capability expansion, particularly around linguistic coverage and localization, while devoting comparatively less public attention to standardized safety documentation, independent evaluation, and disclosure practices now common among leading Western frontier labs.[6]

This gap between capability and oversight reflects a broader regional issue. Across much of the Indo-Pacific, safety considerations are still developing alongside ambitious innovation agendas. The region has moved quickly to advance frontier model capabilities, but the institutional architecture and in-house safety methodologies needed to manage those systems are still maturing. This creates a structural imbalance between the pace of capability development and the mechanisms designed to govern it. As advanced models become more capable of autonomous reasoning, multi-step planning, or integration with external tools, insufficient safety practices will raise not just national but transnational risks, requiring increased international coordination to mitigate them.

A Limited Conversation on Mitigating Catastrophic Risk

Across most of the Indo-Pacific, public and policy conversations around AI risk remain limited to near-term concerns such as data privacy, misinformation, or job displacement. While these are important, they leave little room for discussion of systemic or existential risks—those involving loss of control, large-scale misuse, or unintended escalation between rival states. Focusing only on near-term harms risks locking in governance frameworks that are poorly suited for more advanced systems. As models scale in autonomy, generality, and strategic behavior, early institutional choices will shape whether governments retain meaningful control later, when corrective action could be far more costly or infeasible.

Despite these existential risks, AI is still widely viewed by Indo-Pacific countries as an engine of economic growth rather than a source of potential catastrophe. Few governments have developed contingency plans for major incidents, and most lack institutional capacity for monitoring or crisis coordination. As noted in a recent report, detecting and responding to a significant loss-of-control event requires not only technical understanding of AI models but also whole-of-society planning and coordination among governments, industry, and civil actors.[7] Yet such coordination currently remains minimal across the region. The result is a preparedness gap: while Indo-Pacific countries are among the fastest adopters of AI tools in both the private and public sectors, their ability to manage systemic risks as those tools continue to evolve lags far behind.

How These Risks Converge

The Indo-Pacific’s geopolitical dynamics, in addition to structural and institutional gaps, make the region particularly vulnerable across multiple dimensions. AI risks include the following risks with potentially catastrophic outcomes.

Misaligned AI. Frontier systems trained without rigorous alignment could behave unpredictably when deployed in sensitive domains like defense logistics, finance, or energy management. In a region characterized by rivalries and limited communication, even a contained malfunction due to a misaligned model could be misread as an adversarial act, escalating geopolitical tensions.

AI-enabled weapons of mass destruction and cyberweapons. The intersection of advanced AI, open-source research culture, and rapidly expanding cyber and biotechnology capabilities lowers the barriers to entry for high-impact misuse. In geopolitical terms, this diffusion of opportunity erodes traditional deterrence and nonproliferation regimes, increasing the risk that state or nonstate actors acquire destabilizing capabilities faster than international controls can adapt.

Societal and governmental destabilization. Generative AI systems are already reshaping information ecosystems across the region, from disinformation campaigns during elections to synthetic media inflaming ethnic or territorial disputes. At scale, these dynamics can weaken democratic processes, intensify polarization, erode trust in institutions, and strain already-fragile state legitimacy.

Bifurcation of regional AI ecosystems. As advanced AI capability consolidates in the United States and China, middle powers such as India, Japan, and South Korea face intensifying pressure to align strategically with one of the two poles. While regulatory regimes remain fragmented, this bifurcation increases the risk of parallel AI ecosystems in the Indo-Pacific with divergent governance philosophies. Infrastructure, supply chains, model access, and standards may increasingly align with one of two competing ecosystems, reducing the space for genuinely multilateral or region-specific safety coordination.

Loss of control. The convergence of competitive secrecy, uneven safety standards, and fragile infrastructure makes a regional loss-of-control event more plausible. If an advanced system were to begin operating beyond its developers’ capacity to contain it—whether through autonomous replication, strategic manipulation, or cascading failures across digital systems—the resulting crisis would likely outpace national response capacities and expose the absence of any credible regional or international containment mechanism.

Charting a Safer Path Forward

The Indo-Pacific’s defining paradox is that its very strengths—innovation capacity, technical expertise, and economic dynamism—are what make it so exposed to AI risk. The region accounts for the majority of global AI investment and research output, yet its collective risk-management capacity remains minimal. The implications are global. A misaligned or unsafe frontier model released in the Indo-Pacific would not stay geographically contained. Its effects on information integrity, infrastructure, or decision-making would quickly propagate through the international system. Similarly, a supply-chain disruption or compute crisis in East Asia could halt AI innovation and deployment worldwide.

Given the Indo-Pacific’s pivotal role in the global AI landscape, without deliberate action, the same dynamics propelling its growth could also set the stage for the first major AI-related catastrophe. The task for policymakers is to build mechanisms that reduce these vulnerabilities without stifling innovation—that is, to pursue innovation and safety simultaneously. Building regional architecture for AI safety will be central to that goal. Governments could begin by pursuing the following measures:

Establishing an Indo-Pacific AI safety forum, modeled on aviation safety boards, to collect and investigate AI incident reports, share best practices, and support capacity building for national regulators.
Developing shared early-warning systems, enabling governments and private labs to detect and communicate anomalous model behavior such as unexpected autonomy, deception, or emergent capabilities through established protocols.
Integrating AI risk into existing regional dialogues such as the Quad, ASEAN, and APEC to help link technical experts with strategic planners, ensuring that AI safety remains part of broader digital and security conversations.
Promoting transparent model evaluations through a safety disclosure baseline that requires companies to publish documentation on training data, evaluation methods, and risk-mitigation processes before deployment.
Building capacity for whole-of-society preparedness by supporting universities, think tanks, and civil-society organizations to research and communicate catastrophic risk scenarios, fostering public understanding and institutional readiness.

The Indo-Pacific’s innovation capacity, talent, and infrastructure make it a potential model for responsible technological leadership—but only if those strengths are matched by foresight and coordination. The world’s most dynamic region is also its most exposed. Managing that paradox will define not just the future of the Indo-Pacific but the safety and stability of the global AI order.

Husan Chahal is an AI policy researcher specializing in AI risk, technology governance, and international security. She is currently working as a Senior AI Risk Analyst for the AI Risk Explorer Project at the Observatorio de Riesgos Catastroficos Globales. She is also a Nonresident Fellow at the National Bureau of Asian Research.
