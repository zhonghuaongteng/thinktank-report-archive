First, the U.S. Congress should direct and fully fund
the U.S. National Science Foundation’s Directorate
for Technology, Innovation and Partnerships
(TIP) to serve as the primary vehicle for U.S.-Japan
collaboration on quantum commercialization. TIP is
the only U.S. federal directorate explicitly designed
to move technologies from research into deployment,
requiring industry participation and focusing on
fields essential to national competitiveness, including
quantum. Today, most U.S.-Japan commercial
quantum activity occurs only when individual firms
choose to pursue it. Quantinuum is a clear example:
headquartered in Colorado, it has only two strategic
commercial partnerships globally, and both are
in Japan, with Mitsui and SoftBank. This pattern
demonstrates two key takeaways: Japan has strong
industrial demand for applied quantum solutions,
and U.S. firms already see the country as a viable
commercialization partner. But it also illustrates the
limitation of relying on firm-by-firm initiatives alone.
There is currently no U.S. federal mechanism to
systematically match U.S. hardware and algorithmic
innovations with Japanese industrial challenges or to
scale these partnerships beyond bespoke deals, but
TIP could fulfill this role.
Japan’s closest counterpart to TIP is the Cross-
ministerial Strategic Innovation Promotion Program
(SIP), which coordinates mission-oriented R&D
across ministries and industry. Structured TIP-SIP
collaboration would fill the current gaps and ensure
that benefits flow in both directions. A joint program
could take promising U.S. quantum hardware,
software, and algorithms and match them with
Japan’s industrial testbeds—i.e., automotive, logistics,
and advanced manufacturing—where early quantum
tools are already being trialed. It would also help
prevent asymmetric gains. For example, Denso’s work
with North American quantum firms on optimization
has created efficiencies for Japanese manufacturing,
but there is no formal channel to facilitate equivalent
deployments or market returns in the United States. 5
A coordinated TIP-SIP model would require that
jointly supported projects be demonstrated in both
economies, accelerate commercial maturity on both
sides, and ensure that valuable applications emerging
from collaboration strengthen both countries’
industrial bases rather than only one.
Second, the United States should create a structured
quantum infrastructure offering for Japan. Japan
needs dependable access to quantum hardware
to advance its applied R&D, develop domestic
software capabilities, and train the workforce that
will eventually deploy and maintain these systems.
Japanese institutions are already turning to U.S.
companies for this purpose. The National Institute
of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology,
Japan’s largest public research organization, has
partnered with the American quantum company
IonQ to use its systems for applied research and
early capability development. 6 But deals like this are
narrow and difficult to scale. They depend on one-
off commercial arrangements, provide access to only
a single hardware approach, and cannot deliver the
stable, long-term infrastructure needed to grow a
national ecosystem.
A better model comes from the United States’
Quantum User Expansion for Science and
Technology (QUEST) program, which created
a structured mechanism for giving American
universities, labs, and researchers access to U.S.
quantum-computing systems.7 A bilateral version of
this initiative, coordinated with Japan, would create
a dedicated channel for Japanese universities, labs,
and researchers to access U.S. quantum hardware,
standardized interfaces, and shared experimentation
environments. This would offer a genuine win-
win outcome: Japan would gain reliable, diversified
access to U.S. quantum systems for R&D, software
development, and talent training, while U.S. firms
would gain a stable, government-backed route into a
critical market.
