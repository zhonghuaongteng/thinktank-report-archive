2 Abstract This report investigates the coverage of Quantum Technologies in news media reporting compared to the topics, volumes and geographic distribution of scientific publications in the field. The online news media analysed in this report originate both from mainstream media outlets and Information Manipulation Sources (IMS). The IMS set used in this study represents a collection of online open - source news media outlets identified either through the European External Action Service’s assessments, as outlined in its reports on Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference Threats1, or by independent fact-checking organisations, as potential spreaders of mis- and disinformation. The set also includes structured and coordinated propaganda networks, such as the Russia-linked Pravda network analysed in the Portal Kombat report2 and the related expanded network examined in reports by the Digital Forensic Research Lab3.

Overall, this media analysis on Quantum Technologies highlights the dominating presence of technological breakthroughs and commercialisation from US companies. The EU media pays particular attention to the EU’s efforts to position itself as a global leader in quantum technologies, but the main focus lays on the intense competition with the US and China.

Innovation data, however, reveals a more diversified and complex global landscape, with the EU playing a leading role in scientific developments (both in terms of output volume and impact) across technology domains, although lacking strong industrial anchoring. The US consistently ranks among the leading actors, dominating the computing domain and playing an influential role in quantum sensing. While China plays a comparative less prominent role overall, it emerges as a leading actor in the broader domain of quantum communication, and shows strength in selected technologies within quantum computing and sensing. Among smaller economies, the UK stands out as one of the most prominent contributors, with consistent presence in the top contributors across technology domains. Other significant global players include Japan, displaying particularly strong industry engagement, as well as Canada, Switzerland and, to some extent, India and Israel. Comparing media coverage with innovation data reveals that media attention is concentrated on the quantum domain furthest from deployment, while the domains closest to real -world application receive comparatively little coverage . Media reporting on quantum technologies concentrates on high-profile, corporate announcements, notably by Google and Microsoft , of developments towards future fault-tolerant quantum computing. The two domains already approaching application feature far less prominently quantum communication, where post-quantum cryptography solutions are being standardised by the US National Institute of Standar ds and Technology (NIST) in partnership with academia, industry and governments; and quantum sensing, where applications are already progressing toward broad deployment across sectors such as navigation, Earth observation, and metrology. Neither domain depends on the development of quantum computers: quantum sensing exploits quantum phenomena to improve measurement , while post-quantum cryptography aims at developing classic cryptographic methods that will be resilient to attacks by future quantum computers. As media attention follows announceable headline events rather than deployment proximity, public discourse concentrates on the least developed technologies while the quantum applications entering

3 practical use ( and the decisions they already require, such as the migration to post -quantum cryptography) remain largely outside of the public debate.

Key findings in media: − From December 2024 through April 2026, quantum technologies were mentioned in 22,838 articles from Mainstream media, and in 1,555 articles from IMS. For comparison, quantum technologies received coverage equivalent to about 20% of the generative AI attention in mainstream media and around 80% in IMS. − The main reporting peaks in mainstream media revolved around Google’s launch of its quantum chip “Willow” (9 to 11 December 2024), Microsoft ‘s announcement of its quantum chip “Majorana 1” (20 February 2025 and Google’s development of new algorithm “Quantum Echo” with performance superior to the best traditional supercomputers (23 October 2025). − The main reporting peaks from IMS focus on Microsoft’s “Majorana 1” announcement, a Russian breakthrough on rapid quantum-state transfer (10 July 2025), Chinese researchers’ photon-qubit teleportation experiment (22 July 2025), the US -UK Technology Prosperity Deal on AI and quantum technologies (18 September 2025), and Russia’s seven-level quantum system rivalling a 72-qubit processor (24 December 2025). − The major themes in mainstream news are Technological breakthroughs and applications in quantum; Investments and research collaborations; Commercialisation and market growth; European and global quantum strategies, policies and geopolitical competition; and Challenges and concerns related to quantum. - The main narratives in the Information Manipulation Set (IMS) relate to Chinese dominance in quantum computing; US decline in quantum computing; European irrelevance in quantum computing; Quantum computing as a threat to global stability; European dependence on US and Chinese technology without capabilities to develop own quantum technologies ; and Quantum computing as a threat to global stability overstating its potential for geopolitical coercion. − Positive sentiment dominates both mainstream media (57 %) and IMS (61 %), followed by neutral sentiment, with only a small share of negative sentiment ( 6 % and 8 % respectively). This pattern illustrates that media coverage tends to emphasise the positive aspects of the development of quantum technologies, their potential applications and market dynamics, rather than the limitations and risks associated with the technologies. − Mainstream news is dominated by the framing dimensions Capacity and Resources and Policy Prescription and Evaluation, highlighting the technical infrastructure, funding and human capital required for Europe’s quantum agenda, together with regulatory measures, while security concerns are prominently featured in non-EU IMS sources. − Prominent Entities in both mainstream media and IMS are the tech companies Google, IBM and Microsoft, driving the development of quantum computing. The EU ranks fourth, mainly portrayed with its efforts to position itself as a global quantum leader.

1. Innovation data reveals sustained developments by the innovation community across quantum domains over the past decades , with a clear acceleration after 2017. However, while quantum sensing and quantum communication exhibit the highest growth over the full

4 period, they show early signs of deceleration in the most recent years. By contrast, quantum computing sustains the highest recent growth, concentrated in areas like quantum machine learning and AI, algorithms, software and error correction rather than hardware platforms. 2. The gap between media attention and the state of technolog y development is particularly evident in the area of Post -Quantum Cryptograp hy (PQC) . While quantum computers capable of breaking current cryptographic systems remain far from reali sation, quantum- resistant cryptographic schemes are already moving towards standardisation and implementation. Although the threat remains real, especially considering ‘harvest now, decrypt later’ strategies, the timeline is less stringent than media sometimes portray, and the global innovation community , including governments, academia and industry , is currently preparing migration to post-quantum cryptographic systems. 3. Conversely, the most mature quantum domains, quantum sensing and quantum communication, are systematically underrepresented in media coverage. While media attention focuses on experimental advancements in quantum computing, quantum sensing applications are currently deployed in navigation, E arth observation and metrology, and developments in post quantum cryptography and quantum communication are significantly more mature than suggested by prevailing threat narratives. 4. The analysis of innovation data provides a more nuanced view on global competition compared to media coverage . While mainstream media coverage primarily emphasises US dominance, the innovation data points to a more diverse global landscape, with the EU challenging US leadership in quantum sensing and, to some extent, computing, and China emerging as a leading global player in quantum communication. 5. Furthermore, the innovation data do not generally support narratives circulating within IMS sources, which appear to exploit the technical complexity of quantum technologies to amplify geopolitical threat perceptions and to downplay Europe ’s positioning in the global quantum landscape.

Methodology The media analysis was performed on news from December 2024 until April 2026. It provides an overview of the most important news and reporting trends related to this topic, looks into its perceived societal and economic implications, and aims at raising awareness of manipulative narratives. The analysis of innovation metrics covers scientific publications, patents and EU funded R&D projects between 1996 and 2025, providing a bibliometric and geographic overview of the main quantum technology domains, an assessment of their maturity in terms of Technology Readiness Levels, and a benchmark of the leading global players. The full methodology is described in Annex. By examining reporting trends and narratives from both mainstream media and information manipulation sources and comparing with actual innovation data, the report highlights how these can skew the public discourse and influence policy and perception in the EU, the US, China, and Russia.

5 Introduction This science media intelligence report focusing on Quantum technologies is part of the joint publication series of the European Science-Media Hub (ESMH) and the JRC Data Intelligence for Policy Unit, which covers a wide range of scientific topics relevant to the activities of the European Parliament’s Panel for the Future of Science and Technology (STOA). Recent milestones have significantly accelerated public and political interest in the field of quantum. In late 2024 and early 2025, both Google and Microsoft unveiled new quantum chips, “Willow” and “Majorana 1” respectively, representing distinct and competing approaches to building practical quantum computers. The importance of quantum technologies was also recognised by the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics awarded to John Clarke, Michel H. Devoret and John M. Martinis . At the policy level, the European Union is advancing its Quantum Strategy 4, with the EU Quantum Act expected to be adopted in 2026 as a landmark regulatory framework for the sector.5

Understanding how quantum technologies are portrayed across mainstream and information manipulation sources in the EU, the US, Russia, and China , is essential for grasping their perceived societal and geopolitical impact, and for identifying false or misleading narratives. By analysing reporting trends, narrative spikes, and strategic framings in media coverage between December 2024 and April 2026, this report aims to shed light on how public discourse on quantum technologies is being shaped – and potentially manipulated.

The analysis was conducted on online articles published by media outlets in EU Member States, the US, Russia and China between 1 December 2024 and 27 April 2026. To reveal the main events and narratives in the media, an automated topic-clustering algorithm was employed on data gathered by the Europe Media Monitor (EMM) from online mainstream media and sources from the Information Manipulation Set (IMS).

The media sources part of the IMS used in this study represents a collection of online open -source news media outlets identified either through the European External Action Service’s assessments, as outlined in its reports on Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference Threats 6, or by independent fact-checking organisations as potential spreaders of mis- and disinformation. The IMS also includes structured and coordinated propaganda networks, such as the Russia -linked Pravda network analysed in the Portal Kombat report7 and the related expanded network examined in reports by the Digital Forensic Research Lab 8. As information manipulation knows no borders, the analysis includes worldwide sources to understand its global spread. In addition, we provide a bibliometric and geographic overview of current developments the quantum-technology based on innovation data between 1996 and 2025, covering scientific publications, patents, and EU funded R&D projects. This illustration of genuine scientific development serves as a contrast to the narratives and attention patterns that dominate media coverage.

5. Challenges and concerns Persistent issues such as qubit coherence, scalability, security implications for cryptography and regulatory uncertainty are highlighted as barriers that temper the hype surrounding quantum technologies. Qubit coherence and error rates remain fundamental scientific hurdles, even as approaches such as fluxonium qubits claim lower error margins and improved scalability. Translating laboratory prototypes into industrial -scale manufacturing compounds the problem: large -scale production, reliable error-correction schemes and consistent hardware quality are still unresolved, as highlighted by recent efforts to address “quantum utility” bottlenecks. [etn.se, siliconangle.com, finanznachrichten.de, bolsamania.com]. Beyond technical issues, quantum computing introduces profound security concerns. The capability to break current cryptographic schemes threatens critical infrastructure and sensitive data, prompting urgent calls for quantum -safe encryption. This security anxiety is echo ed by IT professionals expressing their concern over the impact on cybersecurity. 36 Comparative Analysis of Media narratives and Scientific Landscape The analysis reveals a consistent gap between media coverage and innovation activity. Media coverage primarily concentrates on high-profile hardware announcements - such as the unveiling of new quantum chips by major corporations like Google and Microsoft - emphasising their potential to revolutionise industries and conveying a perception of imminent transformative change. This attention aligns overwhelmingly with the quantum computing domain, and within it with fault- tolerant hardware that remains far from deployment. Innovation data, by contrast, show quantum communication and quantum sensing to be comparably active to quantum computing and, on several indicators, closer to application. The pattern reflects the selection logic of news rather than the distribution of technical progress: corporate breakthrough claims arrive as discrete, dateable events, whereas standardisation and gradual deployment generate few comparable news moments and therefore less coverage. In the landscape of post -quantum cryptography, media narratives often describe an urgent need to address potential threats that quantum computing could pose to conventional cryptographic systems. The urgency is grounded in the projected capabilities of quantum computers to break existing encryption methods, posing significant risks to cybersecurity. However, scientific evidence indicates that the hardware required to realise such machines remains far from current capabilities, with fault- tolerant quantum computers still at TRL 3 -5 and several competing hardware approaches currently being explored, none of which has yet demonstrated the scale or error rates required. Moreover, solutions such as lattice-based cryptographic algorithms are already in the deployment phase . The urgency conveyed by media coverage is therefore real but mislocated: while the threat of capable quantum computers is genuine but not imminent , the transition to post- quantum cryptography remains time -critical because full migration will require several years of sustained effort to complete.

Quantum sensing, identified as the most mature domain in quantum technologies, receives comparatively less media coverage. Despite its readiness for practical deployment and applications in fields like navigation and metrology, sensing rarely appears in reporting, which favours experimental developments in quantum computing. Developments in the sensing domain, such as quantum accelerometers, quantum gravimeters, inertial navigation systems and cold atom quantum sensors, are closer to real -world deployment, and worth monitoring given their relevance for future GNSS-independent navigation systems , Earth observation a s well as their potential dual use applications.

When it comes to the performance of the major economic players, media coverage tends to emphasise achievements by US corporations while overlooking the diversity and complexity of the global quantum innovation ecosystem. A comparison with innovation data reveals a more balanced picture, in which leadership varies across technology domains and cannot be reduced to a single dominant actor. This is particularly true for quantum computing, where the field remains in a fluid phase of technological development. M ultiple hardware platforms and computing paradigms are being pursued in parallel, and no single technological approach has yet emerged as the dominant pathway towards scalable, fault-tolerant quantum computing.

Sources within the Information Manipulation Set (IMS) exploit the technical complexity of quantum technologies to amplify geopolitical threat perceptions, such as quantum computing as a tool for coercion, while downplaying European advancements and stressing US decline regarding quantum technologies. By contrast, the innovation data does not support these narratives. The US remains the leading actor overall, particularly in quantum computing, supported by a strong industrial ecosystem. At the same time, the EU rivals the US in scientific excellence and even outperforms it in specific

China’s position, while significant, appears more limited and domain -specific than portrayed by IMS narratives, whereas Russia does not emerge as a major player across the scientific and innovation indicators considered. 38 Conclusions The report presents the results of an analysis of news coverage on quantum technologies, from mainstream and Information Manipulation Sources in the EU, the US, China and Russia . This is complemented by an assessment of the underlying scientific progress and the key actors driving the field from 1996 until 2025. Based on the analysis of media coverage from 1 December 2024 to 27 April 2026, this report concludes the following regarding the topic of quantum technologies: − Peak reporting – The analysis shows a marked contrast between mainstream media and the information-manipulation set (IMS) in their reporting on quantum technologies. Mainstream outlets primarily foreground announcements from large corporations such as Google and IBM, reflecting the commercial milestones that dominate the public discourse. In contrast, IMS sources mainly highlighted the active role of Russia and China by stressing solutions and milestones achieved by Russian and Chinese scientists. − Thematic focus – Mainstream coverage is centred on technological breakthroughs and applications, research collaborations, investments, commercialisation and market growth, European and global strategies/policies and associated challenges. IMS narratives highlighted perceived Chinese dominance, US decline, European marginality, quantum computing threats to global stability, European reliance on US/Chinese technology and its use for geopolitical coercion. − Sentiment – Positive sentiment prevailed in both mainstream and IMS sources, suggesting that media reporting favours the promise and market potential of quantum technologies over their limitations and risks. − Framing dimensions – Both media groups primarily framed stories around Capacity and Resources and Policy Prescription and Evaluation, emphasising infrastructure, funding and human capital required for Europe’s quantum agenda, together with regulatory measures. Security concerns were more prominent in non-EU IMS sources. − Key actors – The most frequently mentioned entities were the technology firms Google, IBM and Microsoft, which drive quantum computing development. The EU appears as the fourth-most cited actor, mainly portrayed in the context of its ambition to become a global quantum leader.

The analysis of innovation data shows a robust and expanding scientific output across the three quantum domains: communication, computing and sensing. The US remains the most influential player in quantum computing overall, contributing the largest share of highly cited research. The EU challenges this leadership in specific areas of quantum computing , such as annealing and optimization, while leading in quantum sensing. China’s rapid growth in innovation output has not yet translated into comparative international impact, except in quantum communication where Chinese institutions figure among the most influential organisations globally. Media coverage, by contrast, is heavily skewed toward high -profile hardware announcements and commercial milestones, particularly from US companies such as Google and Microsoft . The urgency media outlets convey about the threat posed by future fault-tolerant quantum computers to national security is real but mislocated: quantum computing hardware remains at the proof -of-concept to laboratory-validation stages, with fault-tolerant machines far from deployment, while the genuinely

39 time-critical task - the migration to post- quantum cryptography, given 'harvest now, decrypt later' strategies - receives little attention. Quantum sensing, the most mature domain , with technologies progressing from prototype demonstration to operational deployment in navigation, metrology, and Earth observation, likewise receives comparatively little coverage. The overall picture available from media coverage alone thus differs substantially from the one described by the innovation data, including regarding Europe's position in the global innovation landscape.

45 Annex II – Methodology for innovation data analysis Detection of Emerging Technologies (Weak Signals) The methodology applied in this study follows the approach developed by Unit T.5 of the JRC to detect ‘weak signals’ of promising early -stage technologies 25. The analysis is conducted using the TIM Technology26 platform, an advanced monitoring system integrating innovation data, natural language processing (NPL) techniques and Artificial Intelligence.

Two document corpora are used for the detection of weak signals: the Scopus database of scientific publications from Elsevier27 and the PATSTAT database of patents from the European Patent Office28. Weak signals are identified through a text-mining process that generates a large dictionary of multi- word scientific and technological concepts extracted from titles, abstracts and keywords of recent publications and patent documents. Concepts are subseq uently normalised, grouped and ranked by relevance using a modified TF-IDF approach. This dictionary forms the basis for automated semantic queries used to construct document collections across the full-time period from 1996 to2025.

Two complementary detection processes are applied. The systematic ” process generates document collections for each concept in the dictionary. Datasets are then ranked using an activeness indicator, which measures the share of documents published in recent years relative to the full time period. In parallel, a targeted process relies on expert-defined semantic searches to retrieve documents related to specific technological topics and to identify emerging concepts with high recent activeness.

Promising raw weak signals are subsequently reconstructed in TIM Technology through refined search queries incorporating synonyms and alternative terminology to maximise document retrieval. The final selection of weak signals is based on activeness indicat ors for the period 2022 –2025: Signals with activeness between 70% and 100% are automatically retained, while those between 50% and 70% showing strong recent growth are also included based on the assessment of analysts. Using this procedure, 193 weak signals were identified.

Weak signals were subsequently grouped into broader technology clusters using the community detection Leiden algorithm29 to uncover the network structure of frontier research in the field. Finally, technology clusters were assigned to quantum domains by analysts supported by large language models (LLMs) (Anthropic’s Claude Sonnet 4.5).

47 Annex III – Domain-focused technological landscape Quantum Computing The signals detected clearly mark the internal divide of quantum computing research into two different paradigms with different hardware requirements, different time horizons, and different publication dynamics. Research is split between extracting near-term value from noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) devices and pursuing the long-term goal of fault- tolerant quantum computers. On the one hand, the focus for extracting near- term value is on leveraging current NISQ-devices for practical applications in finance, chemistry, and medicine. Technology fields such as Variational & Hybrid Quantum Algorithms, Quantum Machine Learning, quantum annealing and optimisation, and quantum software tools are growing rapidly (CAGR > 80 % for some fields in 2017-2021). They aim to deliver demonstrable advantages in finance, chemistry, biology and medicine with the limited qubit numbers and short circuits currently available. On the other hand, the research and innovation community maintains the long-term goal of fault- tolerant architecture, which stands beyond current capabilities. The dominant effort is in Quantum Error Correction and fault-tolerant Quantum Hardware and Computing Platforms . Despite three decades of work, growth is modest (CAGR ≈ 16 %) and most signals sit at early -to-mid technology readiness levels. No operational deployments exist yet.

Quantum Sensing Quantum sensing is the most advanced area of quantum technology, with a literature base of more than seven thousand papers covering imaging, metrology, timing, navigation and photonic detection. Diamond-based nitrogen -vacancy sensors have already reached a stage where commercial instruments such as magnetometers and microscopes are available, and rubidium atomic clocks are fully deployed in global navigation satellite systems and telecommunications networks. Other sub-fields—quantum accelerometers, gravimeters and cold -atom interferometers—have progressed to the point of laboratory demonstration and are being investigated for navigation that does not rely on satellite signals, for defence applications and for Earth -observation from space. Single photonic sensing includes quantum lidar and ghost-imaging devices that are at early demonstration level, while quantum radar remains in an experimental phase because of unresolved microwave -photon engineering challenges. The same single-photon infrastructure also underpins high-maturity time-bin quantum key distribution, illustrating a convergence between sensing and secure communications. Novel material approaches such as metal-organic-framework spin-qubits and magnon-based sensors are still at early research stages, and a rapidly expanding strand of work on quantum materials and photonic sources —characterised by a very high rate of patenting —indicates strong commercial potential despite its recent emergence.

Quantum Communication Quantum communication is the smallest dedicated quantum-technology field, when excluding the hybrid Post-Quantum Cryptography, treated separately in this chapter. The only cluster in this domain covers different aspects of quantum networks and quantum-key-distribution (19 signals, 1160 documents. The infrastructure side includes already mature trusted-node repeaters (operating at the highest readiness level) and satellite -based key-distribution and multi -node terrestrial networks at

48 demonstrator stage, alongside very early-stage concepts such as network non-locality and large-scale quantum networking. The protocol side shows a clear readiness ladder: current commercial schemes rely on physically secured relay points, limiting security, while frontier work focuses on eliminating those trusted nodes. Demonstrator- level approaches include entanglement -based key -distribution, measurement-device-independent techniques that remove the need to trust detectors, and twin-field key-distribution that extends transmission distance and reduces the number of required relays, all aimed at achieving fully device-independent quantum communication.

Post-Quantum Cryptography cluster Post-Quantum Cryptography and Secure Comms (PQC) (7 signals, 5,216 documents) is structurally unique as the core objective is not to develop quantum technology but to defend against it. Its purpose is to safeguard current information -technology systems against future fault -tolerant quantum computers that could break classical public-key schemes. The field expanded sharply after the US National Institute of Standards and Technology launched its standardisation competition in 201630, nearly doubling output in the five years that followed and maintaining a 22 % compound annual growth rate between 2021 and 2025. Standardised lattice -based algorithms such as Kyber, Dilithium and FALCON have reached the highest technology -readiness levels (8 -9) and are already being deployed, while alternative code- based and isogeny -based schemes remain at intermediate readiness (6 -7). The research frontier (TRL 3 -5) is occupied by post -quantum homomorphic encryption, blockchain integration and diversification of mathematical foundations to guard against possible weaknesses in lattice -based constructions; the quantum -attack algorithms themselves (Grover, Shor) are only at demonstrator stage.
