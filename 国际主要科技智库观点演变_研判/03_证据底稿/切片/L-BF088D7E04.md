Summary
Frontier AI systems are developed primarily in the United States and increasingly in China. If Europe is to
influence how AI is governed internationally, it will need sufficient domestic capability to warrant a seat at
the table where the relevant rules are written and norms decided.
While Europe’s lag in frontier AI has multiple underlying causes, with a lack of European capital chief
among them, this paper focuses on a more specific challenge: to train the most advanced AI models,
compute has until recently had to be concentrated at a single site, with hundreds of thousands of specialised
chips operating in close proximity. This condition gives rise to three types of bottlenecks which stand in the
way of building frontier-scale training capability in Europe: power, compute and politics. Connecting large
datacentres to European grids currently takes seven to ten years, delivery times for critical equipment such
as transformers and turbines have risen substantially, and no single European site currently supplies, or is
on track to supply by 2030, the 5 to 10 gigawatts projected to be needed to train a frontier AI by then.
Coordinating any concentrated buildout of this scale would also require political alignment across the EU’s
member states, a historically time-consuming endeavour.
Distributed training could offer relief : it allows datacentres in different locations to work together on the
same training run, coordinated by a central entity, without needing to be physically co -located. Research
progress since 2023 has substantially reduced the bandwidth between sites these techniques require, making
them increasingly practical at scale. This paper examines whether distributed training can support European
frontier AI capability, given the conditions shaping Europe’s compute buildout, d rawing on a literature
review, expert interviews, and quantitative estimation of compute and power requirements.
Findings
Distributed training offers substantial relief for Europe’s power and political-coordination bottlenecks but
does not narrow Europe ’s underlying chip gap. What it does change for compute is whether the chips
Europe has can be pooled into a single resource for frontier training, rather than remaining fragmented
across individual clusters too small for frontier work. The following paragraphs examine each bottleneck in
turn.
Power. Distributed training offers substantial relief in terms of Europe ’s power constraints. Utilities are
typically more willing to commit power to a portfolio of smaller sites than to a single multi-gigawatt facility,
where the scale of the commitment creates qualitatively different infrastructure requirements and a much
larger planning risk. Connection requests for smaller sites can also proceed in parallel through different
national queues, compressing overall calendar timelines compared to a single ve ry large grid connection
request. Spreading compute across grid regions furthermore allows workloads to be matched to local energy
