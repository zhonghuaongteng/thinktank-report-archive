2 Abstract This report presents the 2024 edition of the Innovation Output Indicator (IOI), a composite indicator published by the European Commission since 2013 to offer an output-focused metric of innovation performance at the country and EU levels. The IOI measures countries’ capacity to derive econ omic benefits from innovation by tracking the extent to which innovative ideas reach the market, create knowledge -intensive jobs, and increase country technological capability. The IOI was developed in response to a 2011 European Council request to improve innovation progress tracking. It also serves as a flagship indicator for assessing the progress of the underlying objectives of the European R&I Fr amework Programmes and contributes to the European Commission's Directorate -General for Research and Innovation (DG RTD) biennial assessment of innovation performance in the European Union, complementing the European Innovation Scoreboard. The report prese nts the latest results for the IOI composite metric and its underlying indicators for 46 countries, including European Union (EU) Member States (MSs) and selected EFTA, OECD and emerging economies. According to the IOI, Sweden , Ireland and Germany are the best performers in the EU and are followed by Finland and Luxembourg. Sweden outperforms the other EU countries in terms of IP applications, Ireland performs very well in terms of employment in knowledge intensive industries, while Germany performs the best across the EU i n terms of its domestic value added content of its kn owledge-intensive manufacturing exports . On the other hand, Romania, Latvia and Bulgaria reported the lowest performance among EU countries. The largest improvements in the IOI between 2013 and 2023 were recorded by Lithuania and Estonia, with the majority of EU MSs also showing an improvement in their IOI performance . The EU 27 leads Canada, Australia, and China, but it continues to lag behind its main economic partners , namely the United States, South Korea and Japan. The EU27's performance has improved ov er time, albeit at the same or at a slower pace than its main economic partners. This suggests that the United States, South Korea, and Japan are maintaining or increasing their lead over the EU27, whereas China is rapidly narrowing the gap. This report al so includes an extension of the IOI to the regional level for the EU27. The first, exploratory edition of the Regional Innovation Output Indicator (RIOI) utilizes both established and novel datasets to evaluate the performance of EU NUTS -2 regions in terms of innovation outputs. It provides a snapshot of regional activity, covering technological and non -technological innovation, as well as regional contributions to value added in trade, employment, and innovation activities. The results for the RIOI showcase the regional divide between the south -east and north -west - of the EU in terms of innovation output performance, with regions of Central and Western EU countries such as Germany, Sweden, Belgium, and Netherlands performing in general better that those of Eastern and Mediterranean EU countries. Exceptions in this pattern are capital and major city regions in the Eastern and Mediterranean Europe, which excel compared to the rema ining regions of their country. These results are confirmed by a spatial autocor relation analysis which showcases the clustering of high -performing regions in North-western Europe and low -performing regions in the South- east. The five best performing NUTS -2 regions in terms of the RIOI are Stockholm, Oberbayern, Berlin, Praha, and Ile-de-France, while Germany and Sweden showcase respectively eight and four regions in the top 20 performers.

4 1 Introduction The Innovation Output Indicator (IOI) is a composite indica tor that aims to support policy makers by offering an output-oriented metric of innovation performance at the country and EU levels . As a monitoring tool with a recognised policy relevance and impact , it has been developed by the European Commission since 2013 in response to the E uropean Council ’s request to develop a “single integrated indicator to allow a better monitoring of progress in innovation” (European Council, 2011 and 2012). The IOI measures a country’s capacity to derive economic benefits from i nnovation by tracking the extent to which innovative ideas reach the market, create knowledge -intensive jobs, and increase technological capability. The Commission uses the IOI to assess progress against the underlying objectives of the European R&I Framew ork Programmes (see, e.g., European Commission, 2024 a). The information provided by the IOI complements other benchmarking tools, such as the European Innovation Scoreboard (European Commission, 2024b), which is also produced by the European Commission, and it feeds into the Commission’s flagship biennial assessment of innovation performance in the European Union (European Commission, 2024c). The IOI focuses exclusively on innovation output and monitors a reduced set of dimensions to support policy - makers in establishing new or reinforced actions to remove bottlenecks that prevent innovators from translating ideas into products and services that can be succes sful on the market s. Its measurement framework was introduced in the 2013 Communication and Staff Working Document (European Commission, 2013) and further refined in 2014, 2016, 2017 and 2023. This report presents the latest results of the IOI. Compared t o the previous edition of the index, the present version uses the same framework for 6 indicators and four components, while it features updated data for most of the IOI component i ndicators. It covers 46 economies, featuring EU Member States and selected benchmark countries which include members of the EFTA, OECD and BRICS groups as well as additional emerging economies. The latest data used in this report refer to 2023 but for some indicators latest available data refer to 2022 or earlier (see Annex 8 for further details ). The empirical results of the 2024 IOI show that, despite the progress made during the last decade, the EU27 continues to underperform compared to its main economic partners such as the United States, South Korea, Japan, and the UK. The present IOI report features, for the first time, an extension of the IOI to the regional level for the 27 EU countries. This first and exploratory edition of the Regional Innovation Output Indicator (RIOI) builds on the national IOI framework an d uses established and novel datasets to assess the performance of EU NUTS -2 regions in terms of innovation outputs . It provides a snapshot of regional activity in terms of technological and non-technological innovation, regional contribution to value adde d in trade, employment, and innovative enterprises. This has the potential to complement the information provided in other regional benchmarking tools, such as the Regional Innovation Scoreboard (RIS), developed also by the European Commission (European Commission, 2023a). The RIOI covers 241 of the 242 EU NUTS -2 regions and the latest data used for its development refer to 2023 but for some indicators latest available data refer to 202 2 or earlier (see Annex 9). The empirical results for the RIOI confirm the innovation divide captured at national level in the IOI. In addition, they document regional disparities in terms of innovation performance between high -performing Central and Northern European regions on the one hand, and below -average performing regio ns in Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean on the other hand. The report is structured as follows. The next section presents the structure of the IOI. Section 3 discusses country performance in terms of the composite score, both when comparing EU Member States with one another and when comparing the EU as a whole wit h other benchmark countries, and in relation to each IOI component. Section 4 presents the structure and empirical results of the RIOI for 241 EU NUTS -2 regions. Section 5 includes a short description of contextual indicators to help understand performance differences across countries. Section 6 concludes. The description of the dataset and the methods applied to compute the aggregate measures, alongside the description of a series of robustness and sensitivity tests of the IOI and the RIOI to the methodological choices are provided in Annex 1 and Annex 2 for the IOI and in Annex 5 and Annex 6 for the RIOI.

The IP component aims to capture a country’s technological and non -technological innovation outputs. It consists of the arithmetic average of two indicators: the number of patent applications filed under the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT_POP) and the number of trademark classes in total resident trademark applications 1 (TRA_POP), each denominated by population (in millions). The first indicator ( PCT_POP) relies on patent data. It is well -known that patent data have a number of drawbacks and may not fully capture the whole set of innovation activities. N ot all inventions , for instanc e, are patentable since they may not meet the patentability criteria ( Dernis et al., 2001; Choi et al., 2007) or some organisations may prefer other mechanisms to protect their technologies (e.g., secrecy) (OECD, 2009). However, the use of patent informati on as a proxy of innovation output is quite established in the innovation literature (Maurseth & Verspagen, 2002; Barzotto et al., 2019; Corradini et al., 2021 ) and they remain one of the most reliable indicators of innovating activity . The requirements of novelty, industrial applicability , and non-obviousness to deem an invention patentable have indeed been argued to be strongly related to innovative activity. The limitations of patent data as a measure of innovation outputs are particularly evident in se ctors with a low propensity to patent, such as services (Scherer, 1983; Arundel and Kabla, 1998; Brouwer and Kleinknecht, 1999). As of the 2023 framework revision, the IP component includes a second indicator, which is based on trademark data ( TRA_POP). In formation on trademarks offers another viewpoint on the multifaceted phenomenon of innovation, in particular one that is less focused on the technological side. Trademarks have been shown to convey information on non -technological innovations (Mendonça et al., 2004; Millot 2009), such as innovation in services or marketing innovation, as well as to better capture innovation in small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) than traditional innovation indicators (Mendonça et al., 2004).

6 The second component (KIABI) measures the number of persons employed in knowledge -intensive business industries as a share of total employment. It aims t o capture the structural orientation of the business economy towards knowledge -intensive activities. This component has remained unchanged in t he latest revision of the IOI and it features in the index’s previous editions (Vertesy and Damioli, 2020; Bello et al., 2023; Bello et al., 2024). The third component, TECH_CAP, aims to capture the contribution of domestic technology capacity to the country’s exports in knowledge -intensive manufacturing and service sectors. It is composed as the arithmetic average of two indicators: d omestic value added content of medium -high and high -tech manufacturing exports as a share of total manufacturing exports (GOOD_VA), and d omestic v alue added content of knowledge-intensive service exports as a share of total service expo rts (SERV_VA). The TECH_CAP indicator makes use of trade in value added data 2, which measures flows related to the value that is added by a country in the production of any good or service that is exported (OECD, 2013). Compared to similar metrics used in previous editions of the IOI, which make use of data on gross exports, 3 the use of value added data is able to differentiate between exports that reflect domestic technology capabilities and those that rely more on foreign inputs, such as those that are the result of mere imported components assembling. In addition, in a world of highly interconnected global value chains traditional measures of trade based on gross exports face certain limitations as the y record flows of good and services every time they cross borders and can lead to double counting and misguided policy decisions (OECD, 2013). The use of value -added data avoids this. A higher value in TECH_CAP indicates a lower foreign content of the country’s exports in these industries. The fourth and last component of the IOI is ENT, which is the share of innovation -active firms on the total number of firms. This indicator captures both product and business process innovations (i.e., process, marketing and organisation innovation), as well as other innovation activities by firms (e.g., R&D or training) which have not yet resulted in a product or a business process innovation. It also reflects innovation activity across less knowledge-intensive economic sectors of a country, which are not captured by the KIABI or the TECH_CAP component.

The European Commission’s FIGARO-REG Input-Output Tables Input-Output tables and their multiregional extensions have been a cornerstone of economic analysis and modelling, traditionally applied at the national level. These analyses are particularly valuable for evaluating the effects of economic shocks, inter -industry relationships, an d, more recently, for quantifying the value added content of international trade and of final demand of countries. By leveraging suitable tools, these analyses can be modelled in a way that not only enhances the theoretical understanding of the economies’ performance but also provides practical, applied insights, ultimately enriching our knowledge to improve economic decision-making. However, the escalating complexity of global trade, together with the increasing concentration of economic activities within countries, poses significant challenges in evaluating economic, social, and environmental performance accurately. This challenge is particularly pronounced when analysing the economic flows within regions of a country, where the intricacies of global trade and the uneven distribution of economic activities can distort our understanding of the metrics of economic performance. As global trade volumes and complexities continue to rise, and economic activities become more dispersed within countries, the assessment of economic, social, and environmental performance at the subnational level becomes increasingly nuanced, highlighting the need for more refined and regional approaches to evaluation. To address this challenge, the Joint Research Centre (JRC) has devel oped a comprehensive multiregional input-output table, known as FIGARO -REG (García-Rodríguez et al., 2023), building on the FIGARO intercountry Input-Output tables (Rueda -Cantuche & Rémond -Tiedrez, 2019) published by Eurostat as official statistics since 2021. FIGARO-REG is a detailed database that is consistent with FIGARO, using 2017 as a benchmark year, and provides a more granular understanding of the economic landscape of each specific economic area at the NUTS2 level. This database enables the observa tion of the consequences of economic linkages, weaknesses, dependencies, and potential improvements in economic performance, while also offering a complete view of bilateral trade flows. FIGARO -REG comprises 240 NUTS2 regions from the European Union (EU), 48 regions from Norway and the United Kingdom together, and 16 countries plus a rest of the world region. It also provides an unprecedented level of detail with 56 industries, allowing for a nuanced view of economies beyond national borders in the EU. This database enables researchers and policymakers to conduct economic analysis at the subnational level, gaining a deeper understanding of the unique characteristics of each region and developing targeted policies to support economic growth and development. F urthermore, FIGARO-REG facilitates the analysis of both intraregional and interregional effects, providing a powerful tool for informing evidence-based decision-making. CARMEN (Computable Analysis of the Regional Multipliers of the European Economy) is the modular economic model approach used to exploit the potentialities of FIGARO -REG. It encompasses several modules to perform specific and customised economic analysis. The main value added of CARMEN is that it allows for a deep dive analysis of economic li nkages, taking advantage of the granularity of FIGARO -REG at territorial and industrial level analysing economic impacts, evaluating short -term economic effects of external shocks, delivering detailed outcomes of the composition of the regional value added or obtaining global value chains indicators. Several studies have been conducted utilising the CARMEN framework, each with different objectives and focuses, all sharing a common emphasis on subnational analysis within the EU. These analyses have explored a range of topics, including the value -added composition of the European automotive industry cluster (European Commission, 2024e), the direct and indirect employment impacts of the coal transition (European Commission, 2025a), and the vulnerability of the EU regions to non -EU protectionist trade policies (European Commission, 2025b). These studies demonstrate the versatility and applicability of the CARMEN framework in examining complex economic issues at the subnational level, providing valuable insights f or policymakers and stakeholders. The CARMEN framework also shows its capacity analysing global value chains (GVCs) at the regional level, where the production of goods and services often involves complex networks of inputs from multiple countries worldwide. The elaboration of regional indicators capturing the intricate flows of goods and services within the GVCs overcome the limitation of the national ones, providing a more nuanced and accurate understanding of international trade by accounting for the val ue added generated in each territorial unit due to the consumption of goods and services worldwide. By doing so, these GVC indicators offer a more detailed and precise picture of commercial relationships between regions, enabling the analysis of global pro duction distribution, networks, and supply chains. This, in turn, can provide valuable insights into the organisation and dynamics of GVCs, ultimately informing policy decisions and business strategies that seek to optimise regional participation in global trade.

This may underscore the existence of spill-over effects, which could be interpreted as strong regional innovation output in a particular region influencing neighbouring regions positively. Similarly, a spatial cluster of low values emerges in the Iberian Peninsula, southern Italy, Greece, Bulgaria, Romania, Hungary, Czech Republic, Poland, and the Baltic States. According to recent qualitative research results, a region’s geographical location is linked to the existence o f barriers to innovation activity (European Commission, 2024f). This subsection presents the results of a spatial autocorrelation analysis capturing the spatial dependence among regions in the RIOI and its component indicators. It aims to examine in a robust manner whether and for which regions proximity plays a role in their innovation output performance (i.e., whether there is a tendency for regions with similar scores to be located close to each other and vice versa), how “far” does spatial dependence st retch, and whether exceptions exist, namely “outlying” regions performing poorly in a broader territory of innovation output prosperity or exceling –in a relative manner- in a neighbourhood of poor performers. Spatial interaction among regions and the pote ntial presence of regional spill -overs in competitiveness can be evaluated using Exploratory Spatial Data Analysis (ESDA) (Annoni & Kozovska, 2011 ). The main objective of ESDA is to identify the structure of the spatial correlation that best describes the data, focusing specifically on spatial autocorrelation and spatial heterogeneity. 18 To examine whether the RIOI scores exhibit spatial autocorrelation, this sub -section adopts the global Moran's I index (Anselin, 1995) employing a distance -based spatial w eight matrix with the number of nearest neighbours set to 10. 19 This

23 index takes values from -1 (maximum negative autocorrelation) to 1 (maximum positive autocorrelation) and indicates the extent to which observations are spatially grouped (positive spatia l autocorrelation), dispersed (negative spatial autocorrelation), or distributed randomly across space. The values of the global Moran's I index for the RIOI is 0.50, a figure suggesting a clear indication that innovation output is positively spatially aut ocorrelated across regions. This result is statistically significant at the 1% level (z-score is equal to 18.93). Positive correlation exists also at the level of the individual indicators of the RIOI, albeit its level varies (the corresponding global Mora n's I values range between 0.17 and 0.66). Spatial clustering is stronger for KIA_REG, BUS_INN, and PRD_INN, and weaker -although still statistically significant- for EUTM_POP and SERV_IVA. These results confirm the claim that there is indeed spatial dependency across regions in terms of innovation output. Figure 14. Clustering of high and low regional innovation output in the EU.

28 5 Conclusions This report presents the 202 4 edition of the Innovation Output Indicator (IOI) , an output-oriented measure of innovation performance at the country and EU levels which assesses countries’ capacity to derive economic benefits from innovat ion. It tracks the extent to which innovative ideas reach the market, create knowledge - intensive jobs, and increase country technological capability. Chapter 3 presents the latest figures for the composite index and its underlying indicators for 46 countries, including the 27 EU Member States, EU candidate states, EFTA and selected BRICS, OECD and emerging economies. According to the IOI, Sweden, Ireland, and Germany are the best -performing EU Member States in 2023 and are followed by Finland and Luxembourg. Sweden outperforms the other EU countries in terms of IP applications, Ireland does so in te rms of employment in knowledge intensive industries, while Germany outperforms the other EU Member States in terms of its domestic value added content of its knowledge - intensive manufacturing exports . Conversely, Romania, Latvia and Bulgaria recorded the lowest performance among EU member states. The EU 27 leads Canada, Australia, and China, but it continues to lag behind its main economic partners, namely the United States, South Korea and Japan. These results are mainly due to its relatively weaker performance compared to international partners in terms of the share of knowledge - intensive employment in business industries, patent applications, and the share of domestic value added in knowledge-intensive services firms. The EU27's performance has improved ov er time, albeit at a slower pace than its main economic partners. This suggests that the United States, South Korea, and Japan are increasing their lead over the EU27, whereas China is rapidly narrowing the gap. Chapter 4 of this report extends, for the fi rst time, the IOI to the regional level of the 27 EU Member States. The newly developed Regional Innovation Output Indicator (RIOI) builds on the national IOI framework and uses established and novel datasets to assess the performance of 241 EU NUTS -2 regi ons in terms of innovation output. Its go al is to provide a snapshot of regional activity in terms of technological and non - technological in novation, regional contribution to value added in trade, employment, and innovative enterprises. According to the RIOI scores, the best performing regions form a cluster stretching from Eastern France, the Netherlands, Belgium, and German regions, to Denmark, Sweden and Finland. By contrast, clusters of lower -performing regions form in the Mediterranean countries and Eastern Europe, with noteworthy exceptions of certain important regions exceling in neighbourhoods of weak performance. The RIOI results are complemented by a spatial autocorrelation analysis that confirms the exist ence of regional disparities in terms of innovation performance in the EU and the formation of clusters of high-performing Central and Northern European regions on the one hand, and below-average performing regions in Eastern Europe and the Mediterranean on the other hand.

Comparison with earlier IOI reports It must be stressed that comparisons with results from previous I OI reports are not possible. Although the methodology in the current report is the same as in the previous two IOI reports, results for the same year, e.g. 2021 in the IOI 2023 report (Bello et al., 2024) and 2021 in this year’s report, are different due t o: (i) updated data being published ; (ii) b y adding new data at the end of the time series for each indicator and removing data at the start of the time series, the highest and lowest scores used for calculating normalised scores across all countries and all years for an indicator can change, directly impacting these normalised scores, and (iii) the rolling 11 -year period affects the imputation of missing data, with some countries that in earlier reports were reporting data for an indicator n ot doing so in later reports, and hence, imputations are used instead. 41 Annex 2. Robustness of IOI ranks and validation of results Uncertainty analysis Methodological choices are part of constructing a composite indicator. Developers make these choices using expert opinion, statistical theory or other practical considerations. However, there often exist equally valid alternatives to the choices being made for a specific indicator. In this sense, methodological choices introduce some uncertainty, as users may ask whether rankings are robust to these alternative specifications. The ranking of any composite indicator should not be heavily dependent (i.e., the re should not be much uncertainty) to a particular methodology chosen during its development. This section investigates to what extent modelling choices influences the ranking of countries in the IOI. Index validation analysis A composite indicator summarize s a multifaceted phenomenon that is not directly measurable by a single indicator or variable . As such, constructing composite indicators requires the use of metrics of observable phenomena that are part of or closely related to the composite indicator’s target concept. Given that the target concept is unobservable, it is useful to validate the resulting measurement by comparing the composite indicator to other measures o f the same or similar concepts. A strong relationship between independently created measures of highly similar concepts provides evidence that they do indeed measure the same underlying phenomenon in a precise way. In this section, the latest edition of the IOI 2024 is plotted against the Global Innovation Index (GII), developed by WIPO and based on 80 indicators (WIPO, 2024), as well as the Summary Innovation Index (SII) in the European innovation scoreboard developed by the European Commission (European Commission, 2024b). The GII includes an Input and an Output sub -pillar. This section plots the IOI against both the GII and its Output sub-pillar. Each plot shows the subsample of countries that appear in both indexes and normalised versions of the indices by dividing the scores of each index with the maximum score obtained in each index . In the top left of each graph, readers can find the Pearson correlation coefficient between the two indices. Overall, there is a strong correlation between the two measures of innovation output, indicating that both measure a similar phenomenon. For instance, Israel scores highly and is ranked 7th in the “knowledge and technology outputs” dimensio n of the GII output sub -index, which is close to its IOI rank (3rd). The RIOI methodology The RIOI 2024 dataset and index development The dataset for the first, exploratory edition of the R IOI for 2024 includes 241 observations covering the 99.6% (241/242) of the NUTS level 2 regions of the 27 European Union Member States and the 27 EU MS as a single economic zone (EU27) for benchmarking reasons. Note that this includes five Member States ( Cyprus, Estonia, Luxembourg, Latvia, and Malta) which are also classified as NUTS level 2 regions. The RIOI estimates for these five countries are not comparable to their IOI estimates shown in Section 2. T his is because there are differences in the IOI and the RIOI in terms of some of the indicators used, the observation sample (which affects indicator minima and maxima that are used in the normalisation process), and the method used for imputing missing da ta (which was not used to impute missing data for these five countries but affected the data of other NUTS-2 regions). The dataset includes seven indicators: PCT_POP, EUTM_POP, KIABI, GOOD_IVA, SERV_IVA, BUS_INN, and PRD_INN. Data availability: The first edition of the RIOI is developed for one year, using the last available data for each of the indicators. That is, if data for 2023 were not available for a particular indicator, data from a previous year were used. Please refer to Annex 9 or Table 11 which include information on the year in which data for each the seven indicators refer. The data availability threshold for inclusion of regions in the RIOI was set at 65%, in accordance wit h JRC-OECD (2008) guidelines. This meant that regions with data missing for three or more indicators would be excluded from the sample. This was the case for 1 NUTS -2 region (FRY5 - Mayotte, see Table 11), which is thus not included in the RIOI. For regions for which no last known values are available for one or two indicators , the imputation was done by means of the algorithm used to develop the European Commission’s RIS (see European Commission, 2023 b), which imputes missing values for NUTS -2 regions with values of previous years (if available, using their ratio with the value of the parent NUTS -1 region) or with values of the parent NUTS -1 region, if regional NUTS-2 data are not available for the previous years. The JRC imputed the PRD_INN and BUS_INN indicators for 47 NUTS-2 regions (all regions mentioned in Table 11 except the 12 regions of the Netherlands and FRY5) with the corresponding values of their parent NUTS-1 region for the same year (2020) , using the RIS algorithm . Robustness of RIOI ranks Uncertainty analysis This section is similar to Annex 2 and investigates the extent to which the m odelling choices made during the development of the RIOI have an e ffect on the regions’ RIOI ranking. The uncertainty analysis systematically changes four different modelling assumptions: the normalisation, imputation, the weights and aggregation. The alternatives considered in the uncertainty analysis of the RIOI are the same as those used for the national IOI and are shown in Table 5. Mean imputation is used as the alternative method to the RIS algorithm used in the nominal RIOI, t he standardization of indicators with a mean of five and a standard deviation of 1.5 is used as the alternative normalisation method to min -max normalisation, the geometric mean is used as the alternative for aggregating the four RIOI pillars, while indicator and pillar weights are randomly varied by 20% from their original value. The combinations (original and alternative) of the normalisation, imputation and aggregation steps result in eight different versions of the RIOI. Four hundred alternatives are randomly drawn from these, and each is computed 100 times using a randomly selected set of weights from a distribution of weights with random perturbation of up to 20% of their original value. This results in 4000 versions of the RIOI. Figure 34 plots for each N UTS-2 region the median rank, the 5 th and the 95 th percentile rank across the 4000 vers ions, while Table 14 summarises the deviations between nominal and median ranks. The ranks of the RIOI are generally robust against changes in methodolog ical assumptions. In particular, for 60% of the NUTS-2 regions median rank up to five positions a way from the nominal ranking (see Table 14). It should be noted that the a bsolute impact –in terms of changes in ranks - of changes in methodology is naturally expected to increase with sample size, as a given (absolute) change in a region’s RIOI might cause larger changes in its ranking compared to, e.g. the national IOI where t he number of the assessed units is considerably smaller. In this sense, with a sample of 241 NUTS-2 regions, rank deviations up to 5 positions are viewed as highlighting high stability and deviations up to 10 positions as highlighting satisfactory stability. From Table 14 we see that for the vast majority of regions (83%), rank deviations from nominal rank are up to 10 positions. What is more, as can be seen from Figure 34, the rankings of the top and bottom-ranked regions are extremely stable: The average deviation of the median simulated rank from the nominal rank is 2.51 positio ns for the top -40 ranked regions and 1.98 positions for the bottom -40 ranked regions, suggesting that the RIOI can identify the top and the bottom 16% of regions in terms of innovation outputs with extreme precision. There are 24 regions (10%) for which the median rank is more than 15 positions away from the nominal rank. These are all located around the middle of the RIOI distribution (their ranks range from 89 th to 194th), where rank changes are in general more frequent even for smaller changes in absolu te RIOI values due. In addition, for 67% (16/24) of these regions concern PT30, and French, Dutch, and Belgian regions, for which the PRD_INN and BUS_INN indicators are not available . Thus, t he likely cause of th e higher deviation of their median and nominal ranks is the imputation process, in which the default modelling choice (the RIS algorithm) takes into account the parent region’s performance (which will unavoidably be the same for all NUTS -2 regions sharing the same parent), while the alternative mean imputation considers the own region’s performance in the other five indicators of the RIOI. On the other hand, the remaining eight regions are Greek. These are ranked considerably better in terms of the two ENT indicators compared to the other five indica tors of the RIOI, suggesting that their indicator profile is quite unbalanced. Thus, their rank deviations are likely caused by the aggregation process, where the alternative aggregation formula is the geometric aggregation which penalises unbalanced profiles, in combination with weights perturbation. The confidence interval (CI) provides another indication of the uncertainty in the ranking due to modelling choices. The confidence interval reflects whether modelling alternatives result in a wide variety of rankings. In the uncertainty analysis, confidence intervals are computed as the 5 th and 95 th percentile of the observed distribution of ranks across simulations. The CI indicates highly stable rankings for the 15.8% of regions , which have a CI range of zero to ten. An additional 67% of regions h ave a CI range between 11 and 40 positions, indicating some uncertainty, but still allowing accurate inference. Lastly, the CI range is larger than 40 positions for the remaining 17% of regions , indicating a lager uncertainty in their ranking . The majority of these regions (29/42) include the French, Dutch, and Belgian regions, for which the PRD_INN and BUS_INN indicators are not available, and Greek regions which have quite unbalanced indicator profiles as discussed earlier.
