Summary
Issue
This study examines scenario-based approaches to structuring U.S.–European Union (EU) and
member state artificial intelligence (AI) cooperation amid intensifying U.S.-China rivalry, at a
moment when AI leadership is becoming central to economic competitiveness, global standard-setting,
and the maintenance of democratic governance (as articulated in Winning the Race: America’s AI
Action Plan and the 2025 National Security Strategy of the United States of America).
1
The case for recalibration does not rest on the absence of transatlantic institutional architecture.
Government mechanisms, research, and private-sector partnerships are already substantial, although
some have gone dormant. It rests on a scope mismatch between what that architecture delivers and
what the competitive moment requires. The existing toolkit enables bilateral dialogue, research
collaboration, venture capital for the most visible EU firms, and market access with reasonable
competence. What it has not produced is a strategic partnership for AI. For the purposes of this
analysis, I define a strategic AI partnership as a formal, durable collaboration that aligns resources,
rules, and incentives across the AI development and deployment life cycle, enabling each partner to
gain capabilities or leverage that it could not efficiently secure on its own while providing a degree of
insulation from short-term political or commercial volatility.
The existing architecture has not accounted for the possibility that the strategic value of EU and
member state offerings may differ as AI develops. A strategic partnership designed without that
contingency thinking risks being well calibrated for one future and poorly positioned for several
others, leaving allied capabilities underdeveloped in domains that may prove consequential to the
democratic bloc’s collective position. To inform this assessment, I analyze four archetypal scenarios—
Concentrated Dominance, Commoditized Frontier, Dependent Specialization, and Collaborative
Specialization—representing the structural pathways along which AI development could unfold,
defined by two variables: (1) the ultimate locus of AI value creation and (2) the degree of advanced
model openness.
By mapping EU and member state capabilities against these potential futures, I propose a
conditional framework for partnership, one that moves away from asking how deeply the United
States should engage with such allies toward asking under what conditions different forms of
engagement generate the greatest returns for U.S. strategic interests. The framework encompasses five
cooperation efforts. Two foundational efforts—an evaluation, testing, and standards interoperability
stack and lithography resilience efforts—generate returns across all trajectories. The remaining three
scenario-contingent efforts are those whose priorities are calibrated against the signposts identified
during the course of this analysis.

Key Findings
• According to the scenario logic, two cooperation efforts are likely to generate returns across all four
AI trajectories analyzed and should be pursued now. These are establishing a permanent joint
infrastructure for evaluating AI systems and maintaining interoperable safety standards and a
coordinated framework to sustain the research and development (R&D), supply chain
integrity, and operational security of the allied lithography base.
• Three additional efforts should be calibrated against observable signposts indicating which of two
variables driving AI futures may be materializing: the benchmark gap between proprietary and
open-source models and the margin differentials between general-purpose and specialized AI
providers. These efforts involve structuring U.S. private investment to extend compute access
to EU startups, small- to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), and research institutions;
deepening cooperation on the privacy-preserving technologies that enable trustworthy AI
deployment in regulated sectors; and building financing and distribution networks that help
specialized EU firms commercialize. Such a split gives policymakers a defensible sequencing
logic: Act on the two foundational efforts now; build institutional readiness for the three
scenario-contingent efforts.
• The EU AI Act’s effects run counter to the prevailing narrative. The often-cited compliance
burden is driven by the cumulative effects of the General Data Protection Regulation, sectoral
rules, and national policies rather than by the Act itself. To the extent that global AI
regulation continues converging toward EU-style risk-classification frameworks, the firms
bearing the highest compliance costs are also those best positioned to export compliant
solutions, because the conformity infrastructure they have built can be redeployed into new
markets at a fraction of the cost that competitors must incur to build one from scratch. The
Act should therefore be understood as a conditional first-mover dynamic for trustworthy
deployment, with implications for how U.S. firms should think about EU partners as
compliance bridges into regulated markets adopting equivalent frameworks.
• U.S. private capital is reshaping EU member states’ AI infrastructure, but its allocation patterns
may foreclose broader access outcomes that the alliance should seek to secure. Hyperscaler-anchored
capacity is being absorbed before it reaches the market, often concentrating compute among
large enterprise customers and underserving SMEs, startups, and research institutions, where
specialized innovation also occurs.
• Export controls on ASML’s sales to China are necessary, but they impose revenue pressure on the
R&D pipeline that the U.S. AI hardware base benefits from. Coordinated long-horizon R&D
funding commitments to sustain high numerical aperture extreme ultraviolet lithography
innovation across project cycles are largely absent; no public evidence of a pre-agreed U.S.
emergency allocation arrangement commits ASML output during a supply crisis; and the
publicly available record does not document an ASML-specific allied framework for joint
threat intelligence and incident response, although visible coordination has a deterrence and
alliance cohesion value that arrangements beyond public view cannot fully substitute for.
Together, these vulnerabilities weaken the resilience of one of the most consequential global
hardware chokepoints.
