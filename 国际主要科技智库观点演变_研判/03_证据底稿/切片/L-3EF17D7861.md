Executive Summary
Turkiye’s defence-industrial relationships with key
European partners have changed over time. In the
1980s Turkiye pursued joint ventures with foreign firms
to procure equipment and develop defence-industrial
capability. Although the most significant of these were
the agreements with US firms to set up F-16 fighter-
aircraft assembly in Turkiye, many projects were pur-
sued with European companies, including for aircraft,
guided weapons and communications equipment.
From 2004, Turkiye changed its approach to focus more
on acquiring locally developed equipment. Many of
these platforms were either designed with assistance
from foreign firms or were fitted with their subsystems,
although over the past two decades Turkiye has been
seeking to replace the latter with its own systems.
Over the last four decades, and in some cases for even
longer, French, Italian, Dutch, Spanish and British compa-
nies have been important in supporting Turkish defence-
industrial objectives. Today, however, some of those
companies are less involved than they were previously, due
to a series of political disagreements between Ankara and
their respective national governments. The main causes of
discord have been Turkiye’s military operations in Syria
since 2016, particularly the 2019 Operation Peace Spring,
and its acquisition of the Russian Almaz-Antey S-400 air-
defence system in 2017. Due to these political differences,
France’s and the Netherlands’ levels of defence-industrial
engagement with Turkiye are now at historic low points.
By contrast, although Italy, Spain and the United Kingdom
share many of these concerns and have at times limited
defence exports to Turkiye, they largely seek to engage
with Turkiye as an important NATO ally that has a key role
in security matters in Europe, the Middle East and Africa.
This includes seeking defence-industrial opportunities,
such as the design support for Turkiye’s Anadolu amphibi-
ous assault ship and Kaan fighter-aircraft programmes pro-
vided by Spanish company Navantia and the UK’s BAE
Systems, respectively.
Meanwhile, Turkiye’s defence-industrial relation-
ships with Eastern European states such as Poland and
Romania are in their early stages, with Turkiye the sup-
plier of defence equipment rather than the customer.
Although these countries share many policy concerns
with their allies in other parts of Europe, the threat they
face from Russia, which they regard as existential, has
pushed them towards greater engagement with Turkiye
in defence and security.
The countries analysed in this report have assessed
Turkiye’s defence industry to have strong capabili-
ties, particularly in aerospace and electronics. Turkish
equipment is now often combat proven, of good qual-
ity, low cost, and sold with few or no end-user restric-
tions. This makes Turkish products competitive on the
export market against traditional suppliers. While some
European companies now consider Turkish firms to be
potential competitors in certain markets, other compa-
nies – particularly from Italy, Spain and the UK – see
opportunities to collaborate and achieve joint success
in export markets where Turkiye has sold equipment
recently, such as Africa, Central Asia, the Middle East
and Southeast Asia.
Turkiye’s participation in the A400M transport-aircraft
project is the country’s most significant involvement in a
European multinational programme to date. However, it
is unlikely that future engagement with Turkiye will take
place in a similar multinational format. Instead, efforts
will probably proceed in a bilateral manner with individ-
ual countries supporting Turkish projects or collaborat-
ing with its industry to export to third countries, or with
Turkiye beginning to make further export inroads with
other European states. While many of the EU member
states examined in this report are keen to pursue these
avenues, Ankara may have to navigate a situation where
EU-supported projects or initiatives designed to encour-
age joint procurement by members will have the effect of
discouraging collaboration with Turkiye. Other avenues
outside the bilateral format, such as the Organisation for
Joint Armament Cooperation (OCCAR) and the NATO
Support and Procurement Agency (NSPA), could repre-
sent more viable options for Turkiye.
