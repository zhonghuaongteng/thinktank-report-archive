Abstract The semiconductor value chain is highly complex, with globally distributed production processes and tightly interconnected segments. This paper identifies the k ey segments, its actors, inputs, and datasets , offering a comprehensive view of the semiconductor ecosystem. It examines each segment to identify bottlenecks and vulnerabilities. The analysis of cross- country linkages within the semiconductor value chain reveals a high concentration of critical inputs in specific regions , the specialisation of economies in certain segments and growing trade dependencies . Policymakers should consider actions to mitigate vulnerabilities and enhance the resilience of the semiconductor value chain, including through efforts to diversify supply chains and through sharing of information on market and policy developments. Strengthened international collaboration and knowledge sharing will be essential to address vulnerabili ties and manag e potential supply chain disruptions.

Executive summary The semiconductor value chain is highly complex, characterised by globally distributed production processes and numerous interlinked segments. Comprehensive understanding and effective risk monitoring and management of this value chain are crucial but requ ire addressing significant data gaps. This paper provides evidence on dependencies and vulnerabilities in the semiconductor value chain to help inform policymakers in considering policies to strengthen its resilience. Working together with stakeholders to diversify supply sources and enhancing international collaboration as well as knowledge sharing can help reduce trade dependencies and manage potential supply chain disruptions. More specifically, this paper provides a first mapping of the semiconductor va lue chain, focusing on two essential aspects: i) the structure of the semiconductor value chain and ii) its main vulnerabilities and bottlenecks. It identifies key segments, actors, and inputs, and offers a detailed analysis of how the different components and processes within the value chain interact, as part of the complex interdependencies that characterise the industry. UN Comtrade data used in this paper show that the number of trade dependencies for semiconductors is particularly high, having more than doubled from 2012-14 to 2020-22. Dependencies for raw materials and manufacturing equipment are also high and have increased considerably in the same period. The supply of borates, silicon wafers and various inputs for wafer fabrication is highly concentrated, with several Asian producers playing key roles. Detailed analysis of bilateral trade flows shows that , for numerous products, many economies increasingly rely on the People’s Republic of China (hereafter “China”), with limited alternative suppliers. This reliance creates dependencies not only for semiconductors themselves but also for several inputs along the value chain. Building on OECD Inter-Country Input-Output data, this paper provides a more comprehensive analysis of the concentration of intermediate inputs flows , differentiating between final and intermediate goods (e.g. packaged and unpackaged semiconductors) and identifies whether the production is intended for domestic use or export. Data show that about half of t he intermediate inputs used globally in the semiconductor industry are components sourced from within the industry itself, highlighting a high degree of intra-industry dependency. In parallel, approximately one-third of intermediate outputs are sold to oth er semiconductor firms for further processing . This dynamic highlights the layered and iterative nature of semiconductor production. In other words, the industry is deeply interconnected: firms rely on one another not just for components, but also for successive processing stages —sending parts back and forth as they are gradually assembled, refined, or integrated into final semiconductor products. Additionally, over half of all semiconductor intermediate outputs go to China, highlighting both the concentration of production and the vulnerabilities in the event of supply chain disruptions. Enhancing the resilience of the semiconductor value chain by mitigating vulnerabilities is an important topic for policymakers. The OECD supports these efforts by analysing different segments of the value chain, by providing policymakers with data to help identify bottlenecks, monitor market imbalances and manage disruptions in the value chain , and by supporting international dialogue and exchange on best policy practices.

During its inaugural year in 2023, the OECD Semiconductor Informal Exchange Network (hereafter the Network) discussed the importance of understanding and monitoring the semiconduc tor value chain, as well as managing value chain risks. The Network highlighted data challenges and affirmed the need for a comprehensive understanding of the semiconductor value chain in detail . To support this objective, the Network developed a taxonomy of semiconductor products and production facilities (OECD, 2024[1]) as a first step, with the longer-term goal of identifying and measuring key upstream and downstream activities in the semiconductor value chain.1 Mapping the semiconductor value chain provides critical insights into the interdependencies between production segments and the flow of inputs and outputs throughout the manufacturing process. This understanding is essential for addressing data gaps, ident ifying vulnerabilities, and informing policy decisions, in line with the objectives of the Network (OECD, 2023[2]). This paper explores two key aspects of the semiconductor value chain: The first examines its structure to better understand the different segments, linkages, inputs and outputs. A thorough understanding requires first identifying the inputs (and their sources) and outputs (and their destinations) at different segments of the semiconductor manufacturing process. This understanding also helps identify other industries beyond semiconductors where the resilience of the whole ecosystem, i.e. the network of firms, workforce, processes, and sectors involved in or dependent on semiconductor production, could be strengthened. The second aspect focuses on identifying bottlenecks and vulnerabilities in the semiconductor value chain. A deeper understanding of these vulnerabilities is crucial for policymakers to manage risks and respond effectively to potential disruptions. The industry may also benefit from better information about vulnerabilities further upstream in the value chain (i.e., beyond the first tier of suppliers). This entails important data challenges. The analysis in this paper builds on the previous identification of key segments and their interdependencies, leveraging available data sources. While detailed, granular data are available for upstream and front -end manufacturing, data availability is comparatively limited for back-end manufacturing, as well as for Research and Development (R&D) and Design. The analysis builds on highly disaggregated UN Com trade data to quantify input -output relationships within the value chain, which are further complemented by OECD Inter-Country Input-Output (ICIO) data to derive key information such as intermediate consumption. There are nevertheless important data aggregation challenges that limit the analysis , notably for semiconductor inputs. Further work will investigate each segment in more detail. Definitions and key segments The semiconductor value chain is extremely complex and relies on a vast network of different types of actors with varying degrees of interaction. As shown in Figure 2.1, the semiconductor value chain can be segmented into three major process steps: design, wafer fabrication and assembly, test and packaging (ATP). Each of these process steps depends on distinct inputs, such as wafers (i.e. a thin slice of a semiconductor material, usually silicon, on which electronic circuits are created), equipment and materials, from often highly specialised suppliers. Both the process steps and their suppliers are heavily reliant on R&D, resulting in the semiconductor industry having among the highest R&D margins across industries (Bloom et al., 2020[3]). Because of these distinct differences between process steps, semiconductor companies have become more specialised, typically focusing on only one of the steps. This specialisation has contributed to the growing complexity and interdepen dence of the value chain over the last three decades. Below is a brief overview of the different business models that emerged and their relation to the three process steps. • Fabless companies design and sell chips. They outsource front - and back-end manufacturing to foundries (i.e. manufacturing facilities producing chips for other companies) and Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test (OSAT) companies, respectively. Fabless companies have among the highest R&D margins in the semiconductor industry, ofte n spending 25% and more of their revenue on R&D (Kleinhans, 2021 [4]). Most of the fabless chip design companies are headquartered in the United States, such as Nvidia, Qualcomm and AMD. • System companies design chips for their own products. They differ from fabless companies by selling systems (cars, smartphones, or services) rather than individual chips. Like fabless companies, they rely on foundries and OSATs for manufacturing. Examples of syst em companies with substantial chip design capabilities are Apple, Tesla, Google and Alibaba. • Semiconductor Intellectual Property ( IP) vendors design and sell functional blocks (also referred to as “IP cores”) that are used by chip designers to shorten time to market and lower chip design costs. Arm, Cadence, Synopsys and Imagination Technologies are among the largest IP vendors (Esteve, 2024[5]). • (Pure-Play) Foundries operate wafer fabrication plants (fabs) to provide contract wafer fabrication services to companies that design chips (fabless, Integrated Device Manufacturers (IDM) and system companies). They closely collaborate with IP vendors and Electronic Design Automation (EDA) suppliers (see below) to offer a “rich” IP value chain to their customers. • Integrated Device Manufacturers (IDM) design, produce and sell chips. Traditionally, IDMs handled all three pro cess steps – design, front - and back -end manufacturing – internally. Historically, this was the prevalent business model in the semiconductor industry. However, over the last two decades, IDMs have increasingly outsourced parts of their production, followi ng the so-called “fab-lite” business model. Today, the “fab-lite” IDM model is prevalent , where IDMs decide on a product -by-product basis whether front - and/or back-end manufacturing is done in - house or outsourced . Some IDMs, such as Intel and Samsung, also offer foundry services for contract manufacturing. Unlike Pure-Play Foundries, IDM-Foundries use some or all of their fabs for contract manufacturing for external customers and internal production of its own chi ps. • Outsourced Semiconductor Assembly and Test (OSAT) companies offer contract back -end manufacturing services to external customers. It is important to understand that the level of vertical integration (across process steps) does not just vary at the company-level but already at the product level, illustrated by the following three examples — see (OECD, 2024[1]) for an overview of product types and manufacturing process technologies. Intel, like many other IDMs, has g radually shifted toward a fab -lite business model, outsourcing some elements of both front - and back-end manufacturing to foundries and OSATs (Mujtaba, 2024 [6]). At the same time, the company is expandi ng its foundry business to offer contract manufacturing to external customers for both front-end and back-end manufacturing. Thus, Intel could be considered a fab-lite IDM, an IDM-Foundry and an OSAT. Infineon, Texas Instruments, Renesas and STMicroelectro nics are examples of fab -lite IDMs in the analog, power, mixed -signal and microcontroller markets. While they may carry out front - and back-end manufacturing of a power semiconductor in-house, these same process steps could be entirely outsourced for microcontrollers. • IP cores are pre-designed, reusable functional blocks used in chip designs. They are crucial to reducing development time, ensuring reliability, and meeting the stringent performance and power requirements of modern chips. Developing a high -quality IP block, such as a Central Processing Unit (CPU) core or a SerDes interface, requires years of expertise and domain-specific knowledge. Furthermore, (upfront) licensing fees and royalty structures (often paid on a per -unit basis) create ongoing financial and contractual dependencies between chip designer and IP vendor. • EDA tools are specialised software used by engineers to bring together semiconductor designs using IP cores and custom designs. It allows them to design, simulate and verify the design. Modern chip design processes involve more than 40 individual steps and most chip designers “mix and match” EDA tools to select the best -in-class tool per step (Nenni, 2022 [13]). The costs of designing a chip vary widely depending on the feature size of the process node, the complexity of the design as well as license costs for EDA tools and IP cores (Shilov, 2023[14]). • PDKs ensure that semiconductor designs align with the capabilities of the foundry where the chip will be taped out . IDMs and foundries develop PDKs for their process nodes that contain all the design rules of a node, such as minimum distances between functional elements. W hile the IDM uses the PDK only internally, foundries provide the PDKs to their customers. The EDA software then uses the PDK’s design rules to ensure that the design created can be manufactured on this process node. Foundries regularly improve their PDKs a nd there is often close collaboration between EDA tool vendors, such as Cadence and Synopsys, and foundries. Innovation in semiconductor design starts through novel designs of chips and IP cores. R&D is particularly crucial for optimising and overcoming the design challenges of creating the most efficient semiconductors within the current physical limitations of the fabrication process, while considering the planned end use (logic, memory, etc.). R&D plays a critical role in all steps of semiconductors production. Innovations in electronics, material science, and miniaturisation, among others, can significantly impact the entire value chain. This can, for example, manifest in improved designs, manu facturing processes or equipment for both state-of-the-art and next-generation semiconductor technologies . The fabrication process itself is an important area of ongoing R&D in companies and across the value chain (e.g., through institutions such as Interuniversity Microelectronics Centre in Leuven, Belgium). Therefore, many key technology partners and input providers throughout the semiconductor value chain invest heavily in R&D. Traditionally IDMs, such as NXP, Renesas and Texas Instruments, as well as fabless companies, such as AMD, Broadcom and MediaTek, were designing chips. While this remains the case, system companies, such as Alibaba, Apple and Google, increasingly invest in their internal chip design (Ma, 2022[15]). As a result, it is challenging to map the global chip design landscape and compare chip design capacities of different economies solely based on data from IDMs and fabless companies. Wafer production Wafers can consist of various materials, such as silicon, silicon carbide (SiC), gallium nitride and diamond. However, silicon is by far the dominant wafer material and the backbone of today’s semiconductor manufacturing, especially for logic and memory chips. In 2023, more than 250 million (8 -inch equivalent) silicon wafers were shipped (SEMI, 2024[28]), compared to an estimated 1.7 million SiC wafers (irrespective of diameter) (Huang, 2023[29]). the goal remains the same: to achieve high purity, uniformity, and in the long-term an ever-increasing wafer diameter (mks, 2024[30]). Fabs typically obtain raw wafers from specialised providers. In order to deliver a raw wafer, the specialised production facility has to produce a cylinder (referred to as a boule or ingot) of pure silicon (or another material). Wafer thin discs are then cut from the cylinder with the help of special machine tools. The level of semiconductivity can then be altered across different parts of the wafer by using ion implanters to embed “dopants”. The latter step can also be performed in the fab as part of chips fabrication. Key inputs to this step include raw semiconducting materials, typically silicon, which are usually refined in a separate facility, as well as furnaces and machine tools for forming and slicing the ingot, implanters and dopant substances for treating the wafers. Landscape More than 50% of the global silicon wafer market are supplied by two Japanese companies, Shin-Etsu and SUMCO, with other significant pla yers including GlobalWafers from Chinese Taipei and Siltronic from Germany (Siltronic, 2024[31]). However, the landscape for other types of wafers, such as silicon -carbide, gallium nitride, sapphire or diamond wafers is fundamentally different. While silicon wafers will continue to dominate, especially for logic and memory chip production, alternative wafer materials provide superior physical characteristics for power and radio frequency chip s. The production process and corresponding supply chain for these wafers substantially differs from that of silicon wafers. Material inputs Polycrystalline silicon, or polysilicon, is ultra refined metallurgical grade silicon created from quartz. The quartz undergoes multiple purification steps to remove as many impurities as possible, resulting in silicon rods. These rods are key inputs for wafer fabrication, as they allow for the creation of an ingot which will eventually be machined into wafers. The process is very similar for germanium wafers. Other semiconductor materials can be used as substrates, including, but not limited to, silicon carbide, gallium arsenide, aluminium gallium phosphide, and mercury cadmium telluride. Each requires the eponymous elements in high purity. Nevertheless, the vast majority of semiconductor production currently uses silicon wafers. Although the production of most of these raw materials is largely concentrated in China, many of these elements are present across the globe. While semiconductor fabrication itself is important, it relies on a wide array of inputs both in equipment and materials. Many of these inputs are specific to semiconductor fabrication and are highly specialised. A prime example are Extreme Ultraviolet (EUV) lithography machines produced by the Dutch company ASML. Modern semiconductor production depends on various lithography tools, such as wet and dry deep ultraviolet lithography tools. For the most advanced production processes at 5nm and below, several layers of a chip can only be produced with EUV equipment. Nonetheless, “older” deep ultraviolet (DUV) lithography equipment is still required for the other layers (Kleinhans and Lee, 2024 [33]). Furthermore, for photomasks production and advanced packaging, other types of lithography equipment, such as X -ray lithography and electron-beam lithography, are essential. This step of the semiconductor manufacturing process requires the most inputs in terms of equipment and materials, with wafers requiring different specialised tools and inputs as they are processed. Throughout its journey in the fab, a wafer is constantly moved and monitored, requiring specific process controlling and wafer handling equipment. Landscape It is important to understand that wafer fabrication is highly diversified, and market analys es need to consider d ifferences in node density, process technology and business models, at the very least. For instance, a modern power semiconductor cannot be built on a process node for high -performance processors or on one for memory chips. Furthermore, some chip types are mainly built in an IDM or a foundry business model. Commodity memory, such as DRAM and NAND, is solely built by IDMs, including Samsung, SK Hynix, Micron, Kioxia and YMTC. These IDMs are also among the companies with the highest wafer capacity, as competition in commodity memory is driven by economies of scale. In contrast, most of today’s smartphone System on Chip (SoC) and Artificial Intelligence (AI) accelerators are designed by fabless and system companies and then manufactured by foundries. Material inputs Photomasks: The material and functioning of the photomask depend on the type (or generation) of lithography tool. They allow transferring the pattern onto chips using photolithography. While DUV lithography tools use transmissive masks, often made o f glass substrates which are coated with opaque materials (typically chromium), EUV lithography uses reflective masks consisting of multiple layers. Photomask production is either done by the fab internally or by merchant mask shops. It starts with a mask blank that is then processed to create the pattern of each layer of the chip. According to the Center for Security and Emerging Technology (2021[10]), the production of transmissive masks (DUV lith ography) is not particularly concentrated, and the raw material components are only moderately concentrated, even if chromium exports are concentrated, South Africa being the largest exporter (US Geological Survey, 2024[34]) . Photoresists: these are chemicals that are deposited on the wafers that undergo a chemical reaction when exposed to the light passing through the photomask. This reaction lays the groundwork for etching, which is performed on areas where the photoresist has dissolved. Each generation of lithography uses photoresists that are specifically tuned to the wavelength of the light source. The shorter the wavelength, the more advanced and specialised the resist chemistry m ust be to meet the increased demands of finer feature sizes. Photoresists are organic materials typically made up of resin, sensitiser, and solvent. Japan is the dominant player in the photoresist market (Center for Security and Emerging Technology, 2021[10]).

Wet processing chemicals : these chemicals are used for different purposes and process steps. For example, wet processing chemicals for etching are used after lithography to transform solid mater ial into liquid to etch the chip layout. Wet processing chemicals are also used during the various cleaning steps to remove residues. Examples of wet processing chemicals are sulfuric acid, isopropyl alcohol, ammonium hydroxide, phosphoric acid, nitric acid, hydrochloric acid, and acetic acid. Some of these require ultra-high levels of purity and therefore are only produced by a limited number of plants. Specialty gases : gases are used throughout the semiconductor fabrication process as dopants, dry etchants, in the dry etching process or for protection from atmospheric exposure. These include common gases like nitrogen, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide, electronics specialty gases, such as nitrogen trifluoride, tungsten hexafluoride, hydrogen chloride, ammonia, disilane, germane, high purity carbon dioxide, and nitrous oxide; or noble gases including neon, argon, argon, helium and krypton. The level of concentration of production of these gases varies. Some are widely available while others are produced by only a few specialised companies (SIA, 2021[35]). Chemical mechanical planarisation (CMP) slurries: a uniform surface for lithography requires wafers to be planarised using a polishing pad and a slurry, which contains abrasives and chemicals. These include rare earths such as cerium. The production of these slurries and pads is concentrated in Japan (Center for Security and Emerging Technology, 2021[10]). Sputtering targets: these are used for Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD) to apply a thin film through the sputtering method which consists of ejecting material from the target on to the wafer. The most commonly used sputtering targets in semiconductor manufacturing are made of aluminium, titanium, tantalum and copper. The choice of target material depends on the type of chip and desired properties. Japan and the United States are the main producers of targets for the industry (Stanford Advanced Materials, 2023[36]). Tools and equipment A large variety of chipmaking equipment is needed in modern wafer fabrication. In addition to equipment for the major process steps – lithography, etching, deposition –, equipment is needed for cleaning, CMP, process automation and metrology and inspection. To understand the landscape, competitive positions and dependencies, these broad categories need to be further broken down. For example, wafer fabrication depends on several different types of deposition equipment in different process steps but also on the type of chip that is built. While Applied Materials is leading the market for Physical Vapor Deposition (PVD) equipment (Castellano, 2023 [37]), ASM and Tokyo Electron are the market leaders in Atomic Layer Deposition (ALD) (Fund, 2024 [38]). A similar situation exists in other chipmaking equipment categories, such as etching, bonding and cleaning equipm ent, where technology leadership depends on the specific subcategory. Throughout this process the chips are rigorously tested to ensure that there are no defects and that they are functioning properly. The chips can be mounted onto a PCB which is shipped to a different assembly facility while in some cases the circuit board is assembled into the final product in the same facility. Collectively these process steps are called ATP or back-end manufacturing. There is a n ever -increasing variety of packaging technologies, and chip designers typically choose a specific packaging technology based on performance requirements, cost constraints, form factor and reliability. The most common categories of packaging technologies are wire bonding, flip chip, wafer-level packaging, system-in-package (SiP) and 3D packaging (Burkacky, Kim and Yeom, 2023 [43]). Packaging technologies are steadily evolving and IDMs, foundries and OSAT s offer increasingly specialised packaging approaches targeted for specific applications, such as high-performance computing, automotive and mobile chips (Vardaman, 2021[44]). For example, advanced pack aging techniques such as 2.5D and 3D packaging are increasingly popular, particularly for mobile applications and high -performance computing. These techniques involve stacking multiple dies or integrating different functions onto a single package, improving performance and reducing the space needed (VerWey, 2022[45]). Advanced packaging also makes mapping the ATP landscape increasingly complex since Pure -Play foundries, IDM-foundries and (traditional) OSATs now offer advanced packaging processes. The reason is that advanced packaging depends on more sophisticated equipment, better cleanrooms and in general a more controlled environment, blurring the line between front - and back -end manufacturing (Patel, 2022[46]).

Trade dependencies To assess bilateral trade dependencies, this paper follows the approach d eveloped by Kowalski and Legendre (2023[54]). Three conditions need to be met to consider an OECD Member country i dependent for its supply of good k. First, gross imports of good k by economy i need to be overall highly concentrated (HHI >0.4). Second, the imports from a partner economy account for a high share (>10%) of i’s total imports of good k. Third, other OECD Member countries’ import share for good k in economy i is less than 20%. To avoid labelling an economy with a high domestic production as dependent, two additional conditions apply. The ratio of exports to imports of good k in economy i must be below the 90th percentile of the distribution and imports from economy i of good k need to amount to USD 1 million or more. However, these analyses provide only a partial view of the ecosystem value chain and its main risks for several reasons. First, concentration of trade flows does not take into account other aspects that are key to determine supply chain risks, such as the criticality of the input for the production process and the availability of substitutes. Second, trade data provide an incomplete view of the supply chain. For instance, some goods are counted twice (e.g. a chip fabricated in country X, packaged in country Y and used in country Z will appear twice in the same category), wh ereas production for domestic use is not taken into account. Third, the granularity of the data is not sufficient to accurately capture the specifics of the semiconductor industry. For instance, although the HS classification covers over 5 thousand goods, some categories are too broad, bundling several types of non-substitutable chips, or a wide range of machines, not all of which are relevant for the semiconductor value chain. This paper examines the structure of the semiconductor value chain and helps identify bottlenecks and vulnerabilities. It offers a detailed analysis of how the different components and processes within the value chain are interconnected, revealing the comp lex interdependencies that characterise the industry. This report highlights the concentration of critical inputs in particular regions and the specialisation of certain economies in specific inputs. The analysis shows that the concentration of production and bilateral trade dependencies have been increasing since 2012. The supply of borates is highly concentrated, with Türkiye as a key supplier. Similarly, the supply of silicon wafers and various inputs for wafer fabrication is highly concentrated with Japan and other Asian producers such as Korea and Chinese Taipei playing key r oles. Detailed analysis of bilateral trade flows shows that , for numerous products, many economies increasingly rely on China, with limited alternative suppliers. This reliance creates dependencies not only for semiconductors themselves but also for several inputs along the value chain. The semiconductor industry is uniquely self -reliant: OECD ICIO data show that about half of the intermediate inputs used globally in the semiconductor industry are components sourced from within the industry itself. In parallel, approximately one-third of intermediate outputs are sold to other semiconductor firms for further processing. This dynamic highlights the layered and iterative nature of semiconductor production. In other words, the industry is deeply interconnected: firms rely on one another not just for components, but also for successive processing stages —sending parts back and forth as they are gradually assembled, refined, or integrated into final semiconductor products. Additionally, the level of concentration in downstream intermediate materials flows (particularly to China) highlights vulnerabilities to potential supply chain disruptions. The concentration of key semiconductor inputs and the growing trade dependencies highlight the need for strategies to diver sify supply sources and reduce vulnerabilities. Efforts to diversify supply sources can help decrease trade dependencies, while further enhancing international collaboration and knowledge sharing will be key to address vulnerabilities and manage potential supply chain disruptions. The findings from these analyses can be complemented by production data analyses, as well as future research focusing on two key areas. First, a more detailed mapping of different segments of the value chain could further enhance understanding of interdependencies. Second, incorporating perspectives from semiconductor-using industries could further refi ne the mapping, offering a more targeted view of the functioning of the value chain. Together, these efforts would provide deeper insights into both the upstream and downstream elements of the value chain, contributing to a more comprehensive and extensive analysis. The OECD supports these efforts by analysing different segments of the value chain , by providing policymakers with data to help identify bottlenecks, monitor market imbalances, and manage disruptions in the value chain, and by supporting international dialogue and exchange on best policy practices .

Efforts to map the semiconductor value chain This subsection provides an overview of recent and ongoing efforts by governments and public sector organisations to map and monitor the semiconductor value chain. This overview is by no means exhaustive and is currently limited to publicly available and more widely publi cised initiatives (Hess and Kleinhans, 2022[56]). Efforts outlined below have differing objectives, coverage, and frequency. They generally have a focus on the respective economy but include both one -time reports and briefings to ongoing monitoring efforts. United States In 2021, the White House published its 100-Day Reviews on ‘Building Resilient Supply Chains, Revitalizing American Manufacturing, and Fostering Broad -Based Growth’ (The White House, 2021 [57]). One of the supply chains identified was ‘Semiconductor Manufacturing and Advanced Packaging’ for which the Department of Commerce led the review. This review starts by mapping the value chain across five segments: “ (1) design; (2) fabrication; (3) assembly, test, and packaging (ATP) and advanced packaging; (4) materials; and (5) manufacturing equipment”. It takes a US centric view to understand the performance of the national industry within each of these segments. The review identifies eight risks across the semiconductor value chain: “ (1) fragile supply chains; (2) malicious supply chain disruptions; (3) use of obsolete and generations -old semiconductors and related challenges for continued profitability of companies in the supply chain; (4) custo mer concentration and geopolitical factors; (5) electronics production network effects; (6) human capital gaps; (7) intellectual property (IP) theft; and (8) challenges in capturing the benefits of innovation and aligning private and public interests.” On that basis, the review offers a number of policy recommendations centred around promoting investment in, collaboration with, and the strengthening of the industry at a national (and international) level, as well as international engagement towards supply chain resilience. The US Department of Energy published a similar report in 2022 (US Department of Energy, 2022 [58]), although with an additional focus on semiconductors for power electronics. Their findings match those of the Department of Commerce, whose review is referred to on multiple occasions, and find similar patterns and vulnerabilities in the power electronics semiconductors. In July 2022, the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) published a report titled “Semiconductor Supply Chain: Policy Considerations from Selected Experts for Reducing Risks and Mitigating Shortages” (Government Accountability Office, 2022[59]), following increasing interest in semiconductor policymaking from both the US Senate and the House of Representatives. The report is less focused on mapping the supply chain and rather looks to identify key risks and relevant policy levers through research and expert interviews.

The recommendations from this report fall under five categories, with one dedicated to supply chain strengthening. The key themes in this section are supply chain security, supply chain monitor ing and transparency, secure access to critical materials, building supply chain resiliency, prioritising chip supply based on industry needs, and increasing and clarifying the demand signal. Building supply chain resilience was identified as one of the mo st important areas. The suggested levers include mandated resilien ce standards, targeting of key risks, as well as promoting agility. Among the various recommendations for value chain and supply chain mapping, some experts interviewed suggested using "high-level organisational information" rather than requiring the disclosure of proprietary information from private entities. Others included forming a team dedicated to understanding and monitoring the supply chain. In 2023, the Congressional Research Servic e published a report on semiconductors and the semiconductor industry (Congressional Research Service, 2023 [60]) which provides a broader briefing on semiconductors and highlights the global nature of their production. The repor t also highlights the state of the US industry across the different segments of the value chain as well as planned investments. In 2023, the United States Department of Commerce published a report assessing the status of the microelectronics industrial base in the United States (U.S. Department of Commerce, 2023[61]). This report highlights the United States microelectronics industry’s leadership but also its challenges. Key areas in the semiconductor value chain such as assembly and packing, along with materials supply, heavily depend on foreign sources. Workforce shortages, foreign competition, and high manufacturing costs are major obstacles, despite strong intellectual property protection and research capabilities. Among the recent US policy mechanisms implemented is the Semiconductor Alert Mechanism (SAM) 1 which gives stakeholders the opportunity to signal disruptions to the semiconductor value chain. European Union The third pillar of the European Chips Act (European Union, 2023[62]) seeks to establish a mechanism for EU member states to coordinate on semiconductor value chain monitoring, as well as anticipating demand and supply shocks. In 2022, the European Commissi on Joint Research Centre (JRC) published a technical report titled: ‘The position of the EU in the semiconductor value chain: evidence on trade, foreign acquisitions, and ownership’ (Ciani and Nardo, 2022 [63]). The report maps the global semiconductor value chain and looks at the EU's position within it. The analysis is split into four main segments, describing: • The EU's position in terms of its trade balance using product level data; • The global linkages between semiconductor value chain actors using firm-level supply chain data; • The investment and Mergers and Acquisitions (M&A) activity involving firms in the EU using firm - level data; • The role of EU-owned firms in each segment of the supply chain using turnover data. The report finds that the value chain is dominated by a few economies, but that given the complexity and high degree of specialisation , no single actor has dominant position over the entire chain . Therefore, no economy is truly independent when considering the enti re value chain. The majority of insights focus on the EU's position, finding a significant role for its companies in most segments of the value chain, with the exception of semiconductor fabrication.

In 2023, the JRC published a paper outlining how it applied the Supply Chain Alert Notification (SCAN) methodology (Bonnet and Ciani, 2023[52]) proposed by the office of the Chief Economist of the European Commission's DG GROW to the Semiconductor value cha in. To identify supply chain risks , the methodology uses a number of structural indicators such as concentration of imports and possible substitution, and high -frequency indicators to capture fluctuations in import prices and quantities. By monitoring these factors, the goal is to identify potential structural vulnerabilities and track imbalances in near-real time. In the case of semiconductors , the JRC found that among the 74 semiconductor value chain relevant products they examined, ten were considered to be at medium to high -risk of import disruption in 2021. This was primarily driven by factors such as high concentration and low substitutability. According to SCAN, six of these products are of particular concern for the EU in the last quarter of 2022 due to increasing prices and decreasing quantities. These are "i) Azidies, Silicides; ii) Photographic plates and film in the flat for monochrome photography; iii) Sheets and plates of polarising material; iv) Fans of a kind used principally for cooling micro processors; v) Diodes; vi) Electronic integrated circuits as dynamic random access memories." The JRC's paper then attempts to evaluate the importance of these products to their respective value chain segments. The JRC's paper assesses the SCAN methodolog y in its application to the semiconductor value chain. It notes that while it can identify structural vulnerabilities there are some confounding factors. The product level view does not allow to isolate the semiconductor value chain specifically. This is particularly important in the case of raw materials which play an important role in other value chains. Additionally, inventory is not accounted for in the methodology and can lead to delays in the impact of disruption s in the monitored data. Building on the JRC’s work, t he DG GROW Chief Economist unit has proposed a new methodology for strategic dependency monitoring (Arjona, Connell García and Herghelegiu, 2023 [64]), which can identify the potential for diversification for specific products, and those particular cases in which the EU needs to develop internal capacity in order to diversify. Finally, the EU has also set up a warning mechanism for firms experiencing supply chain disruptions called the Semiconductor Alert System.2 US-EU Trade and Technology Council The May 2022 Trade and Technology Council (TTC) Statement on Semiconductors outlined a common understanding between the U nited States and the European Union (Trade and Technology Counc il, 2022[65]). Both parties recognised the importance given to securing the supply chain by their respective Chips Acts. While the 2021 supply chain disruption s in semiconductor s can be traced back to an exceptional confluence of factors, it underlines the importance of information sharing and monitoring. While the EU and US initiatives outlined above mostly use trade data, in its statement the TTC emphasises the important role th at industry can play in monitoring efforts. A commitment is made to working with industry on transparency regarding semiconductor demand to better inform public initiatives. The sensitivity of business confidential information is acknowledged. The parties propose the implementation of a common "early warning and monitoring mechanism", on which further information is not yet available.

Other jurisdictions Many jurisdictions have commissioned analys es of their respective semiconductor industries to assess capacities and capabilities. Such reports tend to acknowledge the complexity of the global value chains involved but do not dedicate significant sections to mapping and monitoring efforts. In the United Kingdom (UK), t he UK's Business, Energy and Industrial S trategy Committee published a report on 'The semiconductor industry in the UK' in November 2022 (Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy Committee, 2022[66]). This report describes the extent of the semiconductor industry in the United Kingdom, where it has strengths and where it is lagging, and its vulnerabilities as part of the global value chain. The main strengths outlined are the UK's design, intellectual property, and advanced material chips. On the other hand, it does not have end-to-end production capabilities and is dependent on concentrated suppliers for many inputs and fabrication. The report also issues a set of policy recommendations for the development of semiconductors around focusing on existing strengths, facili tating the development of semiconductor manufacturing in the country, and bridging the gap with the EU and the United States in terms of monitoring and mitigation efforts. In 2020 , the University of Sydney Nano Institute published a review of the Australi an Semiconductor Industry on behalf of the Office of the New South Wales Chief Scientist & Engineer (Nano Institute, 2020[67]). The goal of this report is to assess the Australian semiconductor industry, its scale, the reasons for its small size, the potential advantages of Australia and, accordingly, the levers that might be used to develop it. One of the areas of improvement suggested by this paper is the development of the semiconductor industry through the value chain lens as a way of promoting industry success in the long term, along with improvements in skill, capital access, research, and market information . The Network team would welcome documentation from all members about their respective efforts to map and monitor the semiconductor value chain and/or value chain. This would contribute to the shared knowledge of the network and help all participants improve their own initiatives.

Annex C. Inter-Country Input-Output (ICIO) tables Inter-Country Input -Output (ICIO) tables are the s tatistical infrastructure developed by the OECD to increase understanding of Global Value Chains (GVCs) , by mapping flows of production, consumption , investment and flows of international trade, broken down by economic activity and economy. ICIO tables reflect the division of labour between economies and industries in the production of goods and services and allow for insights into major current dynamics underlying regional and global value chains, such as GVCs resilience (especially after the COVID -19 pandemic), greenhouse gas emissions embodied in internationally traded goods, and trade dependencies (OECD, 2024[68]). Nonetheless, for analysing the semiconductor global value chain, the ICIO tables are too aggregated, therefore, an additional step is required to obtain more granular insights. Haramboure et al. (2023[51]) developed the semiconductor-augmented ICIO tables, which are based on relevant information from five domestic input-output tables ( Korea, Japan, Malaysia, Chinese Taipei and the United States) and from bilateral trade data ( Bilateral Trade in Goods by Industry and End -use Category ) that are sufficiently granular to identify semiconductors (Harmonised Systems headings 8541 and 8542). To split the ISIC division 26 ( manufacture of computer, electronic and optical products ) into two subindustries (the semiconductor industry and ICT and other electronics ), technical coefficients are estimated to determine the proportion of each subindustry in domestic and foreign uses for each producing and purchasing economy. The set of technical input -output coefficients provides enough granularity to analyse the semiconductor industry and focuses on sectoral output and value -added contributions from design, front-end, and back-end manufacturing rather than tracking the intricate details of all upstream inputs such as manufacturing equipment sourcing. The five economies listed above represent around 75% of the global semiconductor value added in 2018 and their domestic technical coefficients are directly available. For the other economies, technical coefficients are imputed from an economy with a similar semiconductor industry according to expert judgement. One caveat of this analysis is that gross -fixed capital formation is not considered under this approach. Moreover, the study period only extends up to 2018 and therefore does not reflect the disruptive impact of the COVID -19 pandemic or the geopolitical strategic decisions made to increase the resilience of the semiconductor GVCs. Nonetheless, technical coefficients remained rather stable over time, reflecting consistent conditions and highlight the usefulness of the results, even in light of later disruptions.
