1 Abstract This report provides an ecosystem analysis of cultural and creative industry (CCI) sector with regard to the adoption of new technologies and as case studies additive manufacturing and advanced materials as these two technologies are becoming consolidated in prototyping and manufacturing in many industrial sectors, but are still in their infancy in the CCI. The introduction of these two technologies, however, is the base of emerging new business models as showcased by a number of promising startups. We identified 430 CCI-startups in Europe that operate innovative products or services and that were further analysed via an online survey to which 83 companies participated. In addition, we discuss the role innovation ecosystems for the CCI development in Europe taking four ecosystems as example, namely the ‘Finnish Additive Manufacturing Ecosystem’ in Helsinki, the ‘Design Farm’ in Berlin, the ‘Sustainable and Tech Fashion’ hub in Amsterdam and “Portuguese footwear cluster” in the northern region of Portugal. Our study shows that green and digital transformations make additive manufacturing and advanced materials technologies quite worth looking into for CCI firms. The survey demonstrates a high degree of business dynamism of those companies that have fully embraced additive manufacturing and advanced materials as their operational basis. Furthermore we show that an effective ecosystem are key for deploying novel technologies to the sector, mostly composed by small and very small enterprises.

3 Executive summary Policy context. Cultural and Creative Industries (CCI) are an essential part for the continued development of societies and important part of Europe’s economy. CCIs are knowledge- intensive and largely based on individual creativity and talent, generating growth and create jobs. But CCIs do not escape from the techno-societal or circular economy megatrend: they have to master the green and digital transition, too. In this respect, a number of EU cities and regions did already start the transition and have been very successful at tapping into the potential of CCIs to boost their socio-economic development. However, there are still too many places in Europe, which have not yet been making most of their potential. Many industrial sectors and value chains will have to go through a very rapid green and digital transformation in the next years towards a sustainable model of inclusive growth, embracing an improved environmental footprint along all steps of the value chain. Within this industrial context, investments in strategic value chains and new forms of collaboration with industry will be essential for ensuring Europe’s strategic autonomy and technological leadership. Likewise, in order to support the implementation of European value chains across EU territories, it is necessary to understand the innovation capacity in particular places, the dynamics of their actors and whether & how effective working relationships can be established across Europe. Technological Considerations. Additive manufacturing (AddManu) is increasing finding its place in the creative industries as it allows them to move from custom production towards mass-customisation, to design products with previously unattainable geometry, to reduce the manufacturing complexity, to improve material efficiency and to simplify the supply-chain. We present two dozens of startups that show how AddManu is finding its way for interiors, particularly furniture and lightning and for fashion, the application areas are clothing, footware, and jewellery. In a survey we carried out we find that AddManu is mostly employed in design stage, and that conceptual design is the process step expected to benefit most in the mid-term. In this study we analyse also CCI startups that employ advanced materials (AdvMat) in their manufacturing process, such as advanced composites, textiles & fibres, coatings, gels & foams, high-performance polymers, light alloys or nanomaterials. We exemplify our results using a dozen of startups for which advanced material constitute the backbone of their businesses. For many years the introduction of advanced materials was on grounds of technical performance and costs. More recently, social and environmental motivations prevail. Trends. Responsible consumption patterns and sustainability considerations come increasingly into the fore. In fashion, clients are increasingly demanding sustainable "cradle to grave" solutions, assuring the recovery and recycling dynamics set from the start of the design process. Such concepts can go beyond mere recycling, such as using fabrics that can be easily be dissolved, transformed or re-polymerised into new semi-finished products. Another aspect influencing the choice of materials is the enhanced sensitivity towards animal protection and more people practising veganism, calling for new natural fibres and materials. Examples include (i) vegan textiles made of mushroom skin and mushroom roots, (ii) naturally dyed sports shoes grown with microbes, which lose more than 70% of their mass one week of after composting or (iii) wool fibre created through biodegradable fibres.

6 Ecosystems. Innovative CCI startups seldom appear in isolation, rather they emerge as a spin-off of previously existing industries or in environments with a high density of creative talents. Several countries have realized the importance of nurturing the CCI ecosystem. Examples of geographical hubs that pursuit contribute to strengthening an innovation driven ecosystem include the DesignFarmBerlin in Berlin (focusing on smart textile wearable technologies), the Finnish Additive Manufacturing Ecosystem in Vaasa (3D printed furniture and biocomposites for 3D printing), FAMEST in Madeira (sustainability and circular economy applied to biomechanics and sensed foodware), Porto Global Hub (a design factory for applied research and industrial collaboration), ClickNL in Eindhoven (diverse activities for local creative industries) or ‘REG X’ in Vejle in Southern Denmark, (providing IT services and design support for user experience and user interaction). Policy Considerations. CCI ecosystems contribute to economic, societal and environmental policy challenges. It is claimed that they may contribute fighting against the depopulation of rural areas, avoiding migration to urban areas. We observe, however, that most innovative CCI startups are located in traditional innovation hotspots like Berlin or Amsterdam. It is in these metropolitan areas where an ecosystem exist where talented people and technology wise can meet and find the infrastructure, capacities and funds to advance promising projects. In rural areas where the density of talented people is lower, the importance of organizations promoting measures to tighten networks and support innovation are more critical. Examples of organization whose aim is to integrate specific local knowledge with the opportunities deriving from new technologies include FAMEST in Madeira, FAME in Vaasa or REG X in Vejle. Whether in metropolitan or rural areas, there seems to be need to support startups with hands-on practical training on technological, and particular digital, support for design, modelling and manufacturing. Key conclusion. As whole, CCI industries still have a long way to go to master the green and digital transition. This study, however, shows that there are hundreds of innovative firms and startups in Europe that have already mastered this transition. They make use of state-of- the art technology (digital tools, additive manufacturing, use of advanced materials, etc.) in their respective business process steps that allow them to master societal and environmental demands (sustainable production, animal-free materials, recycled materials, etc.), while staying economically robust. The support of a functioning local, placed-based innovation ecosystem is key to promote and help companies which are still in the transition phase. The case studies presented in this study show that such ecosystems can emerge in different geographical environments taking into account the specificities and needs of the regions.

The introduction of AddManu in creative industries will impact value chains, business models and specific activities such as product design and prototyping. These changes require people ready to work with these technologies and that are open to changes in production processes. AddManu in the creative industries, although not new at all, is still considered an emerging technology because it is still not widely diffused and has not yet reached a maturity level across the sector. While not yet consolidated, we observe a fast adoption of AddManu technologies is due to a miniaturization of machines, to an ever greater computing capacity, to an important expansion of the range of materials used and to the reduction of cost. In some cases this acceleration is propelled by the expiration of the first registered patents. In essence, AddManu is a new way of turning data into physical things, which enables creative industries to.  To move from custom production towards mass-customisation;  To design products with previously unattainable geometry;  To reduce the manufacturing complexity;  To gain material efficiency; and  To simplify the supply-chain. The main advantages of these technologies are the high precision, the possibility of using different materials (available for metals, ceramics, plastics and biomaterials), and the ability to obtain impossible prototypes using conventional means. AddManu is applied mostly in creative industries where the following parameters influence decisions about technologies and production processes:  High added value of customisation;  Small batch size;  Design complexity;  Importance of low weight;  Drastic assembly reduction.

Therefore, AddManu is increasingly being applied in the creative industries. In addition, there are broader, ‘horizontal’ applications of AddManu to produce components for prototypes and spare parts. Especially for these applications, the technological developments and the cost effectiveness and price of AddManu (mostly for metal) are important drivers. Other drivers (or enablers) are the extent to which AddManu can deliver on parameters such as reducing weight and simplifying assembly lines. In the future, artificial intelligence will also play an important role in both design and production, complementing the existing Finnish experience. Artificial intelligence applications already exist that support quality assurance in the AM process and Finnish research facilities are contributing to this development. All these attributes support the idea that Finland is one of the main AM hubs in Europe. The seemingly remote geographic location is not an obstacle due to the high value per kg ratio in the AM parts, unlike traditional low value added manufacturing where the cost of logistics is significant. The goal of FAME is to increase the use of additive manufacturing technologies in the Finnish machine manufacturing industry and, consequently, to fuel innovation scenarios in highly strategic sectors such as product design, starting with furniture. The actors of the FAME ecosystem could, in the coming years, contribute significantly to the European market of products designed and manufactured in AM and of production systems, also thanks to elements such as low barriers to collaboration. This ecosystem will be based on the development and acquisition of new skills, aimed at creating a portfolio of systemic offers from which the world of creative companies could draw significantly, increase their AM use rate and share experiences, information, data, physical equipment and facilities. Innovative firms in this ecosystem include Kalevala; Hernesaari; UPM; Prenta; Materflow.

The industry has two major production centers: one in Felgueiras and Guimarães; and the other one in Santa Maria da Feira, São João da Madeira and Oliveira de Azeméis. Both hubs are approximately at 50/60 kilometers from Porto. A third, smaller, center is located at Benedita, at 90 kilometers from Lisbon. Some facts give us an idea of the size and importance of the two larger footware clusters for Portugal in general, and the northern region, in particular: the footwear sector accounts for 6,7% of the jobs and 2,8% of the business volume in Portugal. The figures increase to 12% and 6.8%, respectively for the northern region. The footwear industry is, therefore, one of the main economic foundations of the northern Portugal. The geographical concentration of companies facilitates the relations between firms specializing in the manufacture of footwear, components and leather goods, creating good conditions for formal and informal business networks, and it enables firms to benefit from economies of agglomeration. In addition to the business fabric, some of the sector's main infrastructures, such as the Portuguese Footwear Technology Center14 and the Design and Footwear Academy (CFPIC),15 are present in both centers mentioned above. According to the 2019 Statistical Report by the Portuguese Footwear, Components, Leather Goods Manufacturers’ Association APICCAPS,16 the Portuguese footwear cluster is formed by 1877 companies, of which 48.3% of which are micro-enterprises and 39.5% are small. Only 11.7% of firms employ between 50 and 249 workers (medium-sized businesses) and 0.6% more than 250 (nine footwear manufacturing companies, one in components and one in leather goods). The foregoing data leads to the conclusion that the average size of firms is very different in the three industries that make up the cluster. A footwear manufacturing company does in fact employ an average of 26.4 workers, a number that decreases to 20.5 in the components industry and 15.6 in the leather goods industry. The sector seems to be characterized by a high capacity of flexibility and quick response, where even small enterprises demonstrate being able to respond to a very selective demand with a high quality of production. This achievement is possible thanks to the adoption of advanced technological solutions for their production processes. In terms of innovation
