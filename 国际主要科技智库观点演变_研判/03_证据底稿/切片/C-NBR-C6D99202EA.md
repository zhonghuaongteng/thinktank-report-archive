The KFTC’s enforcement record compounds
concerns about the Online Platform Fairness Bill
because the legislation would expand the number of
day-to-day business judgments that can be reframed
as unfair. U.S. firms already view KFTC enforcement
as selective and heavy-handed. Prior research
published by the National Bureau of Asian Research
documented that U.S. firms’ central concern is not
simply what South Korea regulates but how the
KFTC enforces regulations, especially given the lack
of due process and avenues for legal redress. 23 Firms
consistently described a compliance environment
marked by a low threshold for opening investigations,
limited transparency about the underlying theory
of harm, aggressive investigative tactics (including
raids), and unusually broad information demands
and data seizures—paired with the frequent use (or
threat) of criminal referral as an enforcement lever.
These are not abstract process critiques; they are
precisely the kinds of features that convert an already
broad fairness mandate into a highly uncertain and
high-cost regulatory risk for U.S. firms operating in
fast-moving digital markets .
The Impact of the Bill on U.S.-ROK
Trade and Innovation in South Korea
The Online Platform Fairness Bill risks becoming
another major irritant in U.S.-ROK digital trade by
layering broad prohibitions on “unfair trade practices”
onto cross-border platform activity and then vesting
primary enforcement in a regulator that U.S. firms view
as unusually aggressive and procedurally imbalanced.
Some U.S. firms already see South Korea as a high-risk
compliance market, leading them to delay new product
entry and launches. This bill, if enacted, is likely to
reinforce this perspective.24
In trade terms, the Online Platform Fairness Bill
operates like a digital nontariff barrier. Even where
the statute is facially neutral, uncertainty, coupled
with discretionary application of broad conduct
rules, creates asymmetric compliance and litigation
risks that are likely to fall disproportionately on large
U.S. platforms. There is an uncertainty premium
as platforms cannot reliably predict what conduct
will be deemed unfair, how compliance will be
measured, or how penalties will be calculated until
after presidential decrees and enforcement cases. That
uncertainty increases legal exposure to complaints,
investigations, and corrective orders, which in turn
drives precautionary compliance (rewriting and
executing merchant terms and conditions, redesigning
integrations, and expanding internal review) and
could deter the rollout of new features or business
models in Korea. This leads to disproportionately high
fixed compliance costs and expected enforcement
risks for foreign suppliers at the center of cross-border
commerce. The result is a market-access constraint “in
effect,” even without an explicit nationality-based rule.
This is exactly the kind of regulatory friction that trade
policy treats as a nontariff barrier.
