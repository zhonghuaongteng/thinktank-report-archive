Abstract This report presents a patent landscape analysis investigating the innovation trends within the industrial biotechnology (IB) sector from 2015 to 2020. The study's primary objective is to identify the geographical hotspots of innovation, the key players, and the role of different types of organizations in driving technological advancements in IB. By employing a methodology that includes data retrieval through the Technology Innovation Monitoring (TIM) tool and careful selection of keywords and Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) terms, the report categorizes patents across five technological areas pertinent to IB. The geographical scope of the analysis encompasses major global players as well as the European Union, providing a broad view of the innovation landscape. The report also introduces an online dashboard to facilitate further analysis and exploration of the data. This study serves as a resource for policymakers, industry stakeholders, and researchers, offering insights that can inform strategic planning and decision-making in the evolving field of industrial biotechnology. China follows with 14%, showing both high growth and a substantial share, highlighting its growing role in global innovation.

• Asian countries, particularly Israel, China, and Singapore, show rapid growth in patent filings, with a compound annual growth rate (CAGRs) above 27%, underscoring Asia's increasing focus on industrial biotechnology and challenging traditional leaders like the USA and Ja- pan.

• European Union patenting data reveals a significant disparity among member states. While several countries demonstrate strong innovation and growth in industrial biotechnology, the overall EU performance is moderate compared to the USA and leading Asian nations. This is largely because the strong performance of leading innovators within the EU is counterbal- anced by the slower growth or decline observed in other member economies. This disparity raises important policy considerations regarding whether to prioritize support for existing centers of excellence or to implement broader strategies aimed at levelling up innovation capacity across all member states.

• When normalized by gross domestic product (Purchasing Power Parity) (PPP-GDP), smaller countries like South Korea, Singapore, and Belgium show high patent intensities, indicating efficient innovation systems but the overall EU patent intensity remain low compared to other global leaders.

• The USA leads in all technological sectors analysed in terms of total number of patents. Specific countries excel in certain fields when normalizing the number of patents on PPP- GDP: for instance Sweden in Bioprocess Engineering, Germany in Molecular Diagnostics and Robotics, South Korea in Computational Biology, the UK in Molecular Diagnostics.

• The USA maintains a dominant position across all five technology fields, with Revealed Technology Advantage (RTA) values above 1, indicating a high level of specialization. While China shows rapid growth in patent filings, its RTA suggests it does not specialize strongly in industrial biotechnology. Individual countries like Belgium, Germany, and France display strong specialization in various fields.

• There is a variation in the sources of patents, with universities and research centers leading in some countries (e.g., Singapore, Israel), and private companies in others (e.g., Netherlands, Belgium). • The USA and China are at the forefront of jointly owned patents between public and private institutions. The USA is strongly involved in international collaborations, mainly with the UK and Germany, while EU countries have most international collaborations within the bloc.

1 Introduction Biotechnology and biomanufacturing are increasingly recognized as vital sectors to address societal and environmental challenges, enhance competitiveness, and modernize the economy. In its recent communication related to the “Building the future with Nature” initiative1, the European Commission highlights the key role of the Biotechnology and Biomanufacturing sectors in advancing the European Green Deal objectives by providing sustainable solutions for climate change mitigation and adaptation; fostering the sustainable use of natural resources; enhancing food supply and security; and preserving human health. Biotechnology is also identified as a critical technology from an economic security perspective and prioritized in the Strategic Technologies for Europe Platform (STEP) 2 regulation. The industrial biotechnology (IB) sector, which uses biological systems to produce goods and services, is undergoing a transformation driven by the convergence and maturation of emerging and enabling technologies such as synthetic biology, artificial intelligence (AI), microfluidics, computational biology, and gene editing. These technologies, individually and collectively, have the potential to enable the development of novel products and markets by improving production process efficiency and reducing costs. Understanding the innovation ecosystem surrounding these enabling technologies and their application in IB is therefore critical to informing policy making and strategic planning to strengthen the EU's biotechnology and biomanufacturing competitiveness, and to realize the full potential of these sectors for a greener and more resilient economy. This report aims to address the following goals: • Mapping the geographical hotspots of innovation in emerging technologies relevant to IB, providing insights into the global distribution of research and development activities. • Pinpointing key players, including companies, universities, and research institutions, which are driving innovation in IB. • Exploring the role of distinct types of organizations, such as universities, corporations, and public-private collaborations in fostering innovation in enabling technologies applied to IB. This report will explore the current state and future trajectory of the IB sector, with a particular focus on the role of emerging technologies. The findings will be of interest to policymakers, industry stakeholders, and researchers looking to understand the dynamics of innovation in this rapidly evolving field. An online dashboard accompanies this publication to facilitate user analysis and exploration of the underlying data used in the creation of this report.3

2.1 Scope of the study & definitions Industrial biotechnology (white biotechnology): This field involves the application of biological principles and processes to develop sustainable solutions for industrial manufacturing and production, including the creation of bio-based products, chemicals, and energy. It uses microorganisms, enzymes, and genetic engineering techniques to optimize industrial processes, minimizing environmental impact and enhancing efficiency. This definition focuses on the process (how a product is made) and not on the end product itself. Emerging (bio)technologies: Em erging biotechnologies and enabling technologies (robotics, AI, microfluidics…) that apply to IB. Examples of such technologies include synthetic biology, engineered biology, gene editing, metabolic engineering, directed evolution, microfluidics applied to the selection of organisms, cell-free biotechnology, nanobiotechnology, cellular agriculture, machine/deep learning, bioinformatics, and data analysis applied to bioprocess and bioengineering. Geographical scope: The geographical scope of the study encompasses the European Union (EU) and other global leaders in IB, such as Switzerland, South Korea, Japan, China, the UK4, the USA, Singapore, and Israel. Period: 2015-2020. Due to the significant time lag between patent application filing and granting, which varies across patent offices and can take several years, patent data beyond 2020 may be incomplete. Therefore, to ensure data accuracy and completeness, this analysis considers only granted patents up to 2020. The starting point of 2015 was selected based on key technological advancements that have contributed to shaping the IB sector. Notably, the CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology, first described in 2012, rapidly gained prominence and demonstrated widespread applicability in 2013 and 2014. This period also marked significant progress in other foundational biotechnologies, such as advancements in genome sequencing towards the 'thousand- dollar human genome', and later, AI-assisted biological predictions gained traction in 2018 with the breakthrough of Google’s AlphaFold. By starting in 2015, this analysis captures the synergistic effects of innovations in gene editing, artificial intelligence (notably machine and deep learning), synthetic biology, and other enabling technologies. Technological areas of interest: We categorized emerging biotechnologies and enabling technologies into major technological domains to highlight countries’ specialization. The technological domains were defined to cover as much as possible the technologies of interest while minimizing overlap between them. Their definition is based on previous works refined upon expert consultation5,6. The

They include sequencing technologies, bioimaging techniques, and diagnostic assays. 2.2 Data retrieval Patent data retrieval: Patent data was retrieved using the TIM (Technology Innovation Monitoring) tool developed and managed by the JRC - European Commission7. We identified for each of the 5 technological

domains of interest relevant keywords and Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) codes. Different approaches were used for their identification; we started from a list of “benchmark” companies recognized as specialized and innovators in the field of industrial biotechnology and related enabling technologies (Annex 1). Using natural language algorithms available in the TIM Tool, relevant keywords were extracted from the titles and abstracts of patents retrieved from these companies. We also identified recurrent CPC codes related to these companies’ patent portfolio. We then refined this list via expert consultations and by consulting the existing literature, which also helped us define the sub-sectors covered in this study8. The resulting list of keywords and CPC codes associated to each of the technological areas are available in Annex 2. For this study, we retrieved IP5 patent families in the field of IB filed between 2015 and 2020. IP5 patent families are defined as sets of patent applications protecting the same invention, filed in multiple intellectual property (IP) offices. At least one of these filings must be in an office belonging to the IP5 consortium, which includes the European Patent Office (EPO), the Japan Patent Office (JPO), the Korean Intellectual Property Office (KIPO), the State Intellectual Property Office of China (CNIPA), and the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). Using IP5 patent families ensures higher patent quality and comparability across countries, as these patents represent significant inventions with broader market potential. This approach reduces biases from differing national patenting practices and enhances global innovation insights. For this study, the assessment of national performance in industrial biotechnology is based on a full count of IP5 patent families, assigned to countries according to the applicant's country. This method ensures cross-country comparability by accounting for high-quality patents with international market relevance, Patents were then retrieved using the TIM Analytics tool for each of the 5 technological domains selected by using a dedicated query. For each domain, we selected patents whose title or abstract contain at least one relevant keyword AND one of the CPC codes we identified. GDP data retrieval for normalization: We considered throughout the study the total patent count per country, and we normalized this data against each country’s Purchasing-Power-Parity-adjusted Gross Domestic Product (PPP-GDP) in current international USD. For all countries except China and Taiwan, this data was retrieved from the World Bank via the UNData website (accessed in July 2024) 9. For China and Taiwan, this data was retrieved from the International Monetary Fund (IMF)10. The value considered for the normalization is the average GDP over the observation period.

3 Results 3.1 Trends in patenting activities The European Union faces a competitive global innovation landscape. While the United States remains the dominant force in overall patent granted from 2015-2020, the pace of innovation is now set by others, challenging Europe's strategic standing. This period is characterized by explosive growth rates outside the EU. Israel leads with a 30.3% Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) in patent filings, followed closely by China (30.1%) and Singapore (27.2%) (Figure 1). Crucially for Europe, China's expansion (its patent output effectively multiplied 3.75 times over these six years) saw it overtake the EU's total patents within this period. This rapid growth, combined with China's already substantial 14.0% global patent share, signals a shift in global innovation leadership. South Korea and Australia also demonstrate robust expansion with CAGRs between 17-23%. Within Europe, the picture is mixed. While some member states such as Italy, Belgium or Germany show moderate or strong growth, the European Union bloc as a whole records a modest 4.8% CAGR. This signals a slower pace of progress compared to leading global players for the bloc as a whole, and highlights a strong variation in the specialization in the IB field of the bloc’s economies. Indeed, major economies like France (-0.6% CAGR) and Sweden saw patent activity decline, mirroring downturns in Japan (-5.8%) and Taiwan. Despite its own declining growth, the USA still contributed a dominant 49.2% of all global patents. However, China's rapid emergence is reshaping the global patent map. While smaller economies like Israel and Singapore contribute less than 1% to the global total, their rapid growth from a relatively small base showcases powerful national innovation ecosystems. This highlights a critical challenge for the EU: its moderate growth and internal disparities risk ceding ground to more dynamic innovation hubs. To secure its future competitiveness and strategic autonomy, Europe must confront these trends, foster a more integrated and ambitious innovation ecosystem, and ensure its patent output matches its economic potential.

Bioprocess Engineering In Bioprocess Engineering, Sweden emerges as a global leader in patent intensity, boasting more than twice the patents per unit of GDP compared to second-ranked Belgium. This efficiency is driven by companies such as GE Healthcare Biosciences, Cytiva, and Sartorius, complemented by robust government initiatives like the Testa Center. Within Europe, Germany distinguishes itself through its substantial total patent count, propelled by industry giants including Sartorius, Merck, Boehringer, and Eppendorf. Despite these individual successes, the European Union as a collective performs moderately low on the global stage in bioprocess engineering. This paradox is explained by a disparity among member states: while countries like Sweden, Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Germany, and the Netherlands

stand out as strong innovation hubs and top global ranks, the overall EU average is significantly diluted by the comparatively lower patenting activity from other major economies, such as France, Italy, or Poland. This structural imbalance highlights that the EU's innovation landscape in this domain is characterized by pockets of excellence coexisting with regions that are lagging. Addressing this calls for a dual policy approach: either strategically reinforcing the leading innovation ecosystems to maximize their global impact, or implementing targeted investment and capacity-building initiatives in underperforming member states to foster a more balanced and robust Union-wide innovation front. Computational Biology South Korea leads globally in both patent counts and intensity in computational biology due notably to the activity of the Korea Advanced Institute for Science and Technology (KAIST) and companies like GC Genome Corp, Oncocross, HEM Pharma, and SeeGene. In Europe, the Netherlands and Germany take leading roles. The Netherlands’ contributions are driven by companies like Koninklijke Philips N.V., while Germany’s patents stem from entities such as BASF SE, Curetis GmbH, KWS SAAT, Roche Diagnostics, and Siemens Healthcare. Molecular Diagnostics Switzerland leads in patents per unit of GDP in molecular diagnostics, outperforming the USA. Germany, however, leads in Europe for total patent counts, supported by companies like Roche Diagnostics, Qiagen GmbH, Bosch GmbH, Siemens Healthcare, and Miltenyi Biotech. Globally, the UK ranks third in total patents, thanks to its strong gene sequencing ecosystem. Key contributors include Illumina Cambridge and Oxford Nanopore Technologies, both active in DNA sequencing technology. The UK’s long history of innovation in this field, from the discovery of DNA’s structure at the University of Cambridge to Sanger sequencing, has fostered a robust genomics sector. Cyprus also ranks highly in patent intensity, largely due to NIPD Genetics, a leader in genetic testing solutions. Robotics & High-Throughput Automation Switzerland ranks first globally in patent intensity in robotics and high-throughput automation, led by companies such as Tecan Trading, Hamilton Bonaduz, and Hoffman-La Roche. Germany leads in Europe for total patent counts, with contributions from companies like Bosch GmbH, Eppendorf, Brand GmbH, and the Fraunhofer Institute. These organizations are notably active in advancing automated liquid handling and lab automation technologies. Similarly as for other sectors, while Europe as a whole is performing low on the global stage, a few countries top the ranks such as Belgium, Denmark, Luxemburg or Finland indicating a strong innovation disparity within the bloc. Synthetic Biology In synthetic biology, Switzerland leads globally in patent intensity, followed by Denmark and the USA. Germany and France are Europe’s top contributors in total patent counts. Germany’s biggest innovators in this field in our study are KWS SAAT, BASF SE, the Fraunhofer and Max Planck Institutes, and BioNTech. France’s efforts are driven by research organizations such as INSERM and CNRS, along with private companies like Cellectis and Total Energies.

3.3 Revealed Technological Advantage We measured the Revealed Technological Advantage (RTA) of the EU and its largest global competitors in the field of industrial biotechnology (Figure 3). The RTA indicates the extent to which a country specializes in a particular technology sector relative to other countries, hereby “revealing” where a country has a stronger technological capability based on its patent activity in a specific area compared to global averages. We calculate the RTA index by comparing a country’s share of IP5 patents in a specific technology to its overall share of IP5 patents across all technologies. This ratio is then normalized by the global share of IP5 patents in the same technology, ensuring that a country’s specialization is assessed in a global context rather than being limited to a specific set of economies. An RTA value greater than 1 indicates that a country has a higher relative concentration of patents in a given technology compared to the global average, signifying a revealed technological advantage. Significant disparities exist in the balance of patenting activity between the public and private sectors across countries. In France and Belgium, innovation is predominantly driven by public sector institutions, which account for 60% of patents, with private companies contributing the remaining 40%. Conversely, countries like the UK, Germany, and Switzerland demonstrate a stronger reliance on private-sector innovation, with over 80% of patents originating from companies. Singapore, despite being a leading innovator, exhibits a unique profile where only 25% of patents have companies as assignees, while 75% are linked to publicly funded organizations. This contrasts sharply with countries such as Denmark, the Netherlands, Germany, the UK, the USA, and Switzerland, where private companies dominate patent filings. These differences reflect heterogeneity in the structure of national innovation ecosystems. Countries like Singapore, Belgium, and France rely more heavily on public-sector contributions to innovation in this specific domain, whereas others, such as Germany, Switzerland, and the USA, are more dependent on private-sector activity. This suggests that both public and private investments play roles in driving innovation in industrial biotechnology (IB), although the balance varies by country.

3.5 Technology transfer and collaborations Analysing public-private co-owned patents, those listing at least one applicant from the public sector (e.g., universities, research centres, hospitals) alongside at least one private-sector applicant (e.g., companies), provides insights into trends in knowledge and technology transfer (Figure 6).

Co-ownership and public-private partnerships An examination of co-owned patent data provides insights into the collaborative dynamics of national innovation systems. This analysis highlights significant variations in partnership structures. Spain exhibits the highest proportion of public-private co-owned patents (45%), signalling a robust emphasis on academia-industry collaboration. France (34.9%) and Italy (29.2%) also demonstrate substantial engagement in this model. Conversely, Switzerland, Sweden, Finland, and Austria register no instances of public-private patent co-ownership within the covered timeframe. This suggests that innovation in these economies predominantly occurs within distinct sectorial boundaries. Beyond the public-private dimension, a broader analysis of multi-entity patent co-ownership reveals extensive collaborative networks. France (40%) and Italy (29.2%) maintain high rates, indicative of well-established collaborative infrastructures that may encompass diverse entity types or multiple contributors within a single sector. Smaller economies such as Belgium (19.8%) and the United Kingdom (19.1%) also exhibit elevated rates of multi-ownership collaboration, attesting to dynamic innovation ecosystems despite comparatively lower absolute patent counts. 4.1 Insights from the patent landscape 4.1.1 Geographical trends and economic impact The United States' dominance in the IB patent landscape is consistent with its long-standing leadership in biotechnological innovation and substantial investment in research and development. The surge in patent filings from China, surpassing Japan and the EU, signals that its long-term investments in technological capabilities and commitment to becoming a leader in high-tech industries is starting to deliver. South Korea's ascension is indicative of its strategic investment in emerging technologies, particularly in the fields of computational biology and high-throughput automation. It is noteworthy that when patent counts are normalized by PPP-GDP, smaller countries like South Korea, Singapore, and Belgium exhibit high patent intensities, suggesting efficient innovation systems that generate a significant number of patents relative to their economic size. This shows the importance of targeted investment strategies in supporting an environment conducive to innovation, regardless of a country's size. The EU's lower patent intensity compared to other global leaders points to potential areas for policy intervention to stimulate innovation. The varied distribution of patent affiliations across countries reflects different national policies and cultural attitudes towards innovation. In countries where public institutions hold a significant share of patents, such as France, Singapore and Belgium (Figure 5), there may be more robust support for fundamental research and an emphasis on knowledge generation within the academic sector. In countries with such patenting dynamics, it underlines the importance of developing or maintaining a well-functioning technology-transfer capability to transfer academic-based research into innovation. The European Union is known to generate strong research and innovation, but weaker in transferring it to the industry, often for the benefit of other countries 14. In contrast, the high proportion of private company-affiliated patents in countries like Denmark and the Netherlands suggests a business environment that is conducive to commercial innovation and a strong presence of mature biotechnology industries, and their dynamism within the European Union may serve as a case study for other countries. The "Professor Privilege" in Sweden exemplifies how legal frameworks can influence the source of innovation, encouraging direct commercialization by researchers.

4.1.2 Technology specialization and patent productivity Despite the European Union's moderate overall performance in key technological areas like bioprocess engineering, the success of individual member states highlights a strategic path forward. For instance, Sweden's leadership in bioprocess engineering demonstrates the power of national specialization, fuelled by historical strengths, targeted funding, and key industry players such as GE Healthcare Biosciences and the Testa Center. This pattern of specialized excellence within certain EU countries, contrasting with the Union's overall lower global standing due to internal disparities, suggests a strategic direction. Rather than striving for universal excellence across all technologies in every member state, an effective EU policy might involve identifying and nurturing distinct technological strengths within individual member states. This approach, coupled with robust promotion of cross-border cooperation, can build complementary innovation ecosystems across the Union, allowing the EU to leverage its diverse expertise to collectively enhance its global competitiveness in these critical sectors.

4.1.3 Collaboration and knowledge transfer The success of the USA, France, and China in fostering public-private partnerships, as reflected in their significant number of co-owned patents (Figure 6 and 7), highlights the critical role of cross- sector collaboration in driving innovation. The USA, in particular, has successfully positioned itself as a global leader in international co-affiliations, with key partnerships with the EU, Switzerland, and China. Within the EU, Germany and Sweden account for the majority of co-affiliated patents with the USA, demonstrating their leadership in facilitating global research partnerships. In contrast, countries like Switzerland and Sweden exhibit high levels of internal collaboration (patents with multiple co-affiliates within either the public or private sector) but limited public- private partnerships. This suggests that while these countries have robust innovation ecosystems, opportunities for bridging academic research with industry may remain underexplored. For the EU overall, the low percentage of public-private patents underscores the need for stronger mechanisms to facilitate collaboration and transfer knowledge into industrial applications. 4.2 Countries-specific particularities We found a few global leaders that stood out in our analysis, particularly in regards with their innovation landscape related to the size of their economy or with their dynamic growth over the observed period. The dynamism and growth of these specific countries in the field of industrial biotechnologies warrant advanced case-studies to highlight past and current policy measures dedicated to this industrial sector. We propose below a brief overview of the policy-related reasons that might have influenced these countries’ strength in this area, but further, comprehensive, investigations beyond this patent landscape analysis are required to fully understand how specific policies have fostered a dynamic innovation environment in these countries. 4.2.1 South Korea South Korea emerged as a global leader in many aspects of IB, and in particular in computational biology (Figure 2 and 3), as highlighted in our patent landscape analysis. South Korea is a global leader in innovation according to the Innovation Scoreboard 2024 published by the European

Commission, ranking first among the global competitors of the EU overall15. This success is attributed to both systemic factors and targeted biotech policies. Since 2006, South Korea's "Bio- Vision 2016" plan has set specific biotech and bioeconomy goals, with a later focus on bioindustries and pharmaceuticals16. The 2012 "Strategy for promotion of industrial biotechnology" and the 2013 "3rd Basic Plan for Science and Technology" further prioritized biotech innovation, fostering technology transfer, cross-sector alliances, and biobased standards. The Ministry of Science and ICT's Bio-Venture Support Program backs life sciences startups17, complemented by R&D tax credits, regulatory streamlining, and IP protection to attract investment. In 2023, the Green Bio Act was introduced to boost biotech in agriculture through development plans, research institutes, startup support, and "green bio" data promotion18. Its strong reliance on public institutions and government-backed initiatives (Figure 5) emphasizes the critical role of centralized planning in its innovation ecosystem. 4.4 Limits of the study • This report is descriptive and serves as foundation for future studies. • Relying solely on patent data to gauge innovation may not provide a complete picture. Not all innovations are patented, and patent counts do not necessarily reflect the commercial viability or impact of the technologies. Additionally, the complexity of patent classifications may lead to under- or over-representation of certain technologies. Moreover, organizations have different strategies for protecting their intellectual property, with some favouring trade secrets or rapid market entry over patent filings. This study does not account for such strategic variations, which can influence the number and type of patents filed. • The analysis is confined to patent data between 2015 and 2020. Trends or innovations emerging after this period have not been captured, omitting recent developments that could influence the current state of the sector. • The study focuses on emerging technological fields within industrial biotechnology, such as synthetic biology and AI. Other relevant technologies that did not fall within the defined scope may also play a critical role in the sector's transformation but were not analysed here. • The technologies are found using a set of keywords and patent CPC terms. The addition or absence of certain terms can influence the outcome. • There is a time lag between technological innovation and patent filing, as well as between patent filing and publication, meaning that the study may not fully reflect the most innova- tive advancements at the time of analysis. • While the study examines co-owned patents, the depth of these collaborations and their im- pact on innovation could not be fully assessed as the nature of the relationships between co-owners and the quality of their interactions are not reflected in patent data alone. • While the study highlights the role of public-private collaborations, it does not analyse the effectiveness of these collaborations in driving innovation or their long-term sustainability.

5 Conclusion This patent landscape analysis examined innovation trends within the industrial biotechnology (IB) sector from 2015 to 2020, identifying geographical hotspots, key players, and organizational roles. Our findings confirm the United States and Switzerland as a dominant forces in IB and highlights the rapid ascent of Asian countries, particularly China, which has shown remarkable growth in patent filings, surpassing the EU and Japan. While the European Union demonstrates overall growth, its pace is more moderate compared to the US and key Asian nations. This can be attributed, in part, to the fact that while several smaller European countries exhibit strong patent intensity and efficient innovation systems, the EU's overall performance is influenced by larger economies that may not specialize as heavily in specific IB areas. Nevertheless, the analysis of Revealed Technological Advantage (RTA) reveals the USA's broad technological advantage across IB, while countries like Switzerland and the UK show specialization in specific high-value areas like Molecular Diagnostics and Synthetic Biology. The EU demonstrates specialization primarily in Bioprocess Engineering. In conclusion, this patent landscape analysis of industrial biotechnology between 2015 and 2020 underscores the dynamic nature of the field. While the USA maintains a leading position, the rapid growth in Asia coupled with the specialized strengths of various European nations highlights a shifting global innovation landscape. Understanding these geographical trends, technological specializations, and collaboration patterns is crucial for policymakers and industry stakeholders aiming to support innovation and ensure continued advancements in this sector. Future studies could explore deeper into specific technological sub-sectors and the impact of evolving regulatory frameworks.
