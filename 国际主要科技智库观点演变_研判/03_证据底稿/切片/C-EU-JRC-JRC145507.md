2 Abstract This report presents the latest edition of the Innovation Output Indicator (IOI) for 2025, a composite indicator published by the European Commission since 2013 to offer an output -focused metric of innovation performance at both national and European Union (EU) levels. The IOI measures countries’ capacity to derive economic benefits from innovation by tracking the extent to which innovative ideas reach the market, create knowledge -intensive jobs, and increase country technological capability. It was developed in response to a 2011 Europe an Council request to enhance the monitoring of innovation progress. It also serves as a flagship indicator for assessing the progress of the underlying objectives of the EU Framework Programmes for Research and Innovation and contributes to the European Commission's Directorate-General for Research and Innovation (DG RTD) assessment of innovation performance in the EU, complementing the European Innovation Scoreboard. The report presents the most recent results for the IOI composite indicator and its under lying indicators for 46 countries, including EU Member States (MS) and selected EFTA, OECD and emerging economies. According to the IOI, Sweden, Ireland and Germany are the top-performing EU countries, followed by Denmark and Luxembourg. Sweden outperforms the other EU countries in patent applications, while Ireland and Luxemburg excel in employment within knowledge-intensive industries. Both Ireland and Germany achieve the highest performance in the domestic value-added content of their knowledge-intensive manufacturing exports, whereas Denmark records the highest domestic contribution to the value of its knowledge-intensive service industry exports. At the lower end of the ranking, Romania, Bulgaria, Slovakia, and Latvia exhibit the lowest performance among EU countries. Overall, most of the EU MSs (23 out of 27) improved their IOI performance between 2014 and 2024, with Lithuania and Estonia witnessing the most significant improvements during the period. The EU27 outperforms Canada, Australia, and China in the IOI, but it remains behind the United States, South Korea and Japan. Although the EU27's performance has improved between 2014 and 2024 , this progress has occurred at a pace similar to or slower than that of some of its key partners. South Korea and Japan are either maintaining or widening their lead over the EU27, while China is narrowing the gap with the EU27. By contrast, the United States show signs of slower growth as the EU27 advances more rapidly. The main IOI analysis is complemented by an exp loration of innovation systems across MSs. This includes the collection and analysis of contextual indicators related to countries’ economic structure and their performance in entrepreneurship and other dimensions of innovation. Additionally, a validation analysis of the IOI is conducted to assess its relationship with indicators reflecting inputs to the innovation process, framework conditions for robust innovation systems and its congruence with existing composite indicator frameworks measuring innovation at the country level. Overall, the analysis demonstrates a strong positive relationship between innovation performance, economic structure, and macroeconomic outcomes across EU countries. Top IOI performers such as Sweden, Germany, and Ireland report high GDP per capita growth, substantial R&D investment and a significant share of employment in innovation -active enterprises, while reflecting distinct economic structures. In contrast, low-performing countries like Bulgaria and Romania demonstrate improvements in both economic growth and IOI scores but continue to face limitations due to lower R&D intensity and a reliance on low-tech manufacturing. The results of the IOI validation analysis demonstrate a strong positive correlation between the IOI and a range of indicators capturing innovation inputs and supportive framework conditions, as well as the rankings from other composite indicators monitori ng innovation performance. While these overlaps confirm the IOI’s alignment with established innovation metrics, the distinct differences in

3 rankings across frameworks underscore the added value of the IOI within the broader landscape of innovation monitoring tools.

5 1. Introduction The Innovation Output Indicator (IOI) is a composite indicator designed to support policy makers by providing an output-oriented metric of innovation performance at both national and European Union (EU) levels. The IOI was developed by the European Commission in response to the European Council’s request to develop a “single integrated indicator to allow a better monitoring of progress in innovation” (European Council, 2011 and 2012). It captures a country’s ability to translate innovation into economic ben efits by examining how effectively innovative ideas reach the market, generate knowledge-intensive jobs, and enhance technological capabilities. By focusing exclusively on innovation outputs, the IOI monitors a streamlined set of dimensions of innovation, enabling policymakers to design or reinforce targeted actions to address innovation barriers and spur innovation performance. The measurement framework of the IOI was introduced in the 2013 Communication and Staff Working Document (European Commission, 20 13) and has been further refined in subsequent updates in 2014, 2016, 2017 and 2023. These refinements ensure the indicator remains aligned with the EU’s evolving policy priorities. The evidence provided by the IOI complements other benchmarking tools, such as the European Innovation Scoreboard (European Commission, 2025a), another tool produced by the European Commission, and it informs the biennial Science, Research and Innovation Performance of the EU (SRIP) report, which is the Commission’s reference assessment of innovation performance of the European Union (European Commission, 2024b). In addition, the Commission adopted the IOIin the ex-post evaluation of Horizon 2020 to assess the progress of the program towards its objectives (European Commission, 2024a). This report presents the latest results of the IOI covering the period 2014 and 2024. The current version retains the same analytical framework as the previous edition, comprising six indicators and four components. It incorporates updated data for most of the IOI’s component indicators , covering 46 economies, including EU Member States (MS), the EU27 aggregate, and selected benchmark members of the EFTA, OECD and BRICS as wel l as additional emerging economies. The latest data used in this report refer to the year 2024; however, for certain indicators, the most recent available data refer to 2023 or earlier (see Annex 5 for further details). The main IOI analysis is complemented by an exploration of innovation systems across MS s. This includes the collection and analysis of contextual indicators related to countries’ economic structure and their performance in entrepreneurship and other dimensions of innovation. Additionally, a validation analysis of the IOI is conducted to assess its relationship with indicators reflecting inputs to the innovation process, framework conditions for robust innovation systems and its congruence with existing composite indicator frameworks measuring innovation at the country level. The report is structured as follows. The next section outlines the conceptual framework of the IOI, detailing its structure and components. Section 3 analyses IOI performance across EU MSs and the EU27 aggregate, comparing them to benchmark countries, and examines performance variations across the IOI’s four components. Section 4 provides a contextual analysis to deepen the understanding of IOI disparities among EU MSs, supported by validati on tests assessing the robustness of the indicator. Section 5 concludes the report, summarizing key findings and policy implications. For completeness, Annex 1 describes the dataset, methodological approaches, and procedures used to compute the IOI’s aggre gate measures. Annex 2 outlines the robustness and sensitivity analyses conducted to evaluate the impact of methodological choices during the IOI’s development.

Measuring country innovation performance: The IOI The Innovation Output Indicator 2025 uses the revised IOI structure adopted in 2023 and used in the previous two editions of the index (Bello et al., 2023; 2024; 2025).(see Annex 1). The framework includes a total of six indicators aggregated into four components, including intellectual property (IP), knowledge-intensive activities in business industries (KIABI), domestic technology capacity (TECH_CAP), and innovative-active firms (ENT). The IP component aims to capture a country’s technological and non-technological innovation outputs. It consists of the arithmetic average of two indicators: the number of patent applications filed under the Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT_POP) and the number of trademark classes in total resident trademark applications1 (TRA_POP), each denominated by population (in millions). The first indicator (PCT_POP) relies on patent data. It is well-known that patent data have a number of drawbacks and may not fully capture the whole set of innovation activities. Not all inventions, for instance, are patentable since they may not meet the patentability criteria (Dernis et al., 2001; Choi et al., 2007) or some organisations may prefer other mechanisms to protect their technologies (e.g., secrecy) (OECD, 2009). However, the use of patent information as a proxy of innovation output is well established in the innovation literature (Maurseth & Verspagen, 2002; Barzotto et al., 2019; Corradini et al., 2021) and they remain one of the most reliable indicators of innovating activity. The requirements of novelty, industrial applicability, and non -obviousness to deem an invention patentable have indeed been argued to be strongly related to innovative activity. Following the 2023 framework revision, the IP component was introduced as a new addition to the IOI, comprising both p atent data and a second indicator based on trademark data (TRA_POP). Information on trademarks offers another viewpoint on the multifaceted phenomenon of innovation, in particular one that is less focused on the technological side. Trademarks have been sho wn to convey information on non -technological innovations (Mendonça et al., 2004; Millot 2009), such as innovation in services or marketing innovation, while also being more closely linked to the market as they protect marketable products and support the commercialisation of innovation; moreover, they better capture innovative activity in small and medium -sized enterprises (SMEs) than traditional innovation indicators (Mendonça et al., 2004). The second component (KIABI) measures the number of persons employed in knowledge-intensive business industries as a share of total employment. It aims to capture the structural orientation of the business economy towards knowledge-intensive activities. This component has remained unchanged in the latest revision of the IOI, and it features in the index’s previous editions (Vertesy and Damioli, 2020; Bello et al., 202 4; Bello et al., 2025). The third component, TECH_CAP, aims to capture the contribution of domestic technology capacity to the country’s exports in knowledge-intensive manufacturing and service sectors. It is composed as the arithmetic average of two indicators: domestic value -added content of medium -high and high- tech manufacturing exports as a share of total manufacturing exports (GOOD_VA), an d domestic value-added content of knowledge -intensive service exports as a share of total service exports (SERV_VA). The TECH_CAP indicator makes use of trade in value added data2, which measures flows related to the value that is added by a country in the production of any good or service that is exported (OECD, 2013). Compared to similar metrics used in previous editions of the IOI, which make use of data on gross exports, 3 the use of value -added data can differentiate between exports that reflect domestic technology capabilities and those that rely more on foreign inputs, such as those that are the result of mere imported components assembling. In addition, in a world of highly interconnected global value chains, traditional measures of trade based on gross exports face certain limitations as they record flows of good and services every time they cross borders and can lead to double counting and misguided policy decisions (OECD, 2013). The use of value-added data addresses this issue as a higher value in TECH_CAP indicates a lower foreign content of the country’s exports in these industries. The fourth and last component of the IOI is ENT, which is the share of innovation-active firms in the total number of firms. This indicator captures both product and business process innovations (i.e., process, marketing and organisation innovation), as well as other innovation activities by firms (e.g., R&D or training) which have not yet resulted in a product or a business process innovation. Validation analysis As a composite indicator, the IOI summarizes a latent concept that cannot be directly measurable by a single metric. The validity of the IOI as a measure of innovation can be assessed by examining its concordance with alternative measures of national innovation performance or comparable constructs for the same countries, as well as other unidimensional indicators that resemble or are closely related to innovation output, such as inputs to innovation and framework conditions known to facilitate the process of innovation. This subsection summarises the results of such a validation analysis (see Annex 2 for a more detailed discussion). The IOI is positively associated with of two other summary measures of innovation at the national level, namely the 2025 Summary Index of the EIS (European Commission, 2025a) and the WIPO Global Innovation Index (WIPO, 2025) and their sub-components (see Table 7 and Figure 15, Figure 16 Fast-growth is defined by an annual average employment growth greater than 10% over a three-year period.

21 16, and Figure 17 in Annex 2). The fact that leader and laggards in the IOI are similarly performing in the EIS and GII s upports the consistency of the IOI , indicating that it indeed measures a similar phenomenon to these metrics. However, notable differences are observed in the relative performance of countries across the t hree measures (see Table 7 in Annex 2 ), suggesting that their close association does not indicate information redundancy. On the contrary, they suggest that the IOI provides a distinct perspective on country innovation performance relative to the EIS and GII. On the other hand, the IOI and its four components are adequately correlated with a series of variables representing inputs to innovation , such as R&D expenditure and GDP per capita , and the quality of innovation -enabling framework conditions within countries, such as the share of higher education graduates, ICT specialists, and cloud computing adoption within the country (see Table 8 in Annex 2 ). This provides additional evidence on the validity of the IOI, in that countries investing more in innovation and facilitating a more innovation-friendly e nvironment tend to also perform better in terms of the innovation outputs captured by the IOI.

22 5. Conclusions This report presents the 2025 edition of the Innovation Output Indicator (IOI), an output -oriented measure of innovation performance at the country and EU levels. The IOI assesses countries’ capacity to derive economic benefits from innovation by tracking the extent to which innovative ideas reach the market, create knowledge-intensive jobs, and increase country technological capability. According to the IOI17, Sweden, Ireland, and Germany are the best -performing EU MSs in 2024 and are followed by Denmark and Luxembourg. Sweden outperforms the other EU countries in terms of patent applications; Ireland does so in terms of employment in knowledge intensive industries and also, together with Germany, outperform the other EU MSs in terms of their domestic value-added content of their knowledge-intensive manufacturing exports. Luxembourg tops the ranking of trademark applications and shares of employment in knowledge-intensive activities, while Denmark excels in the value of knowledge-intensive service exports added from domestic industries and patent applications. The EU27 leads international partners such as Canada, Australia, and China, but it continues to lag behind the United States, South Korea and Japan. These results are mainly due to the EU27’s relatively weaker performance in terms of the share of knowledge-intensive employment in business industries, patent and trademark applications, and the share of innovation -active enterprises. South Korea and Japan are either maintaining or widening their lead over the EU27, while China is narrowing the gap with the EU27. By contrast, the United States show signs of slower growth as the EU27 advances more rapidly. Section 4 compares the IOI with other innovation -related indicators and frameworks at the country level to validate its relationship with innovation inputs, framework conditions, and broader contextual measures. Higher innovation output in the EU is systematically linked to higher income levels, sustained economic growth, and greater R&D investment. Conversely, countries with lower IOI performance are typically characterised by lower R&D intensity and a reliance on low -tech manufacturing. The IOI reveals a robust positive association between national performance in the IOI and indicators of innovation inputs and enabling conditions. The IOI methodology The IOI 2025 dataset and index development18 The IOI 2025 dataset includes 50619 country-year observations covering the 27 European Union MSs, EFTA countries (Switzerland, Iceland, Norway), EU candidates (North Macedonia, Serbia, Türkiye), selected OECD countries (Australia, Canada, Chile, Israel, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, New Zeeland, United Kingdom, United States of America) and two BRICS members (Brazil and China) over the time period 2014 until 2024. It also includes an observation for the 27 EU MS as a single economic zone (EU27). The dataset includes six indicators: PCT_POP, TRA_POP, KIABI, GOOD_VA, SERV_VA and ENT. JRC conducted data collection for additional countries. The threshold for inclusion of a country in the IOI has been set to 65% of the indicators having at least one non- missing data point, as per the JRC guidelines (JRC/OECD, 2008). That is, countries are included in the IOI calculations if they have at least one non-missing data point for four out of the six indicators. Countries that did not meet this criterion for which data were collected (Albania, Armenia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia, Moldova, Montenegro) are included in the indicator Figures in Section 3 whenever data for those countries are available. Comparison with earlier IOI reports It must be stressed that comparisons with results from previous IOI reports are not possible. Although the conceptual framework and the methodology in the current report is the same as in the previous three IOI reports, results for the same year, e.g. 2021 in the IOI 2024 report (Bello et al., 2025) and 2021 in this year’s report, are different due to: (i) updated data being published; (ii) by adding new data at the end of the time series for each indicator and removing data at the start of the time series, the highest and lowest scores used for calculating normalised scores across all countries and all years for an indicator can change, directly impacting the normalised scores, and (iii) the rolling 11-year period affects the imputation of missing data, with some countries that in earlier reports were reporting data for an indicator not doing so in later reports, and hence, imputations are used instead. Robustness of IOI ranks and validation of results Uncertainty analysis Methodological choices are an unavoidable part of constructing a composite indicator. Developers make these choices using expert opinion, statistical theory or other practical considerations. However, there often exist equally valid alternatives to the choices being made to develop a composite indicator. In this sense, methodological choices introduce some uncertainty, as users may ask whether rankings are robust to these alternative specifications. The ranking of any composite indicator should not be heavily dependent (i.e., there should not be much uncertainty) to a particular methodology chosen during its development. This section investigates to what extent modelling choices influence the ranking of countries in the IOI.

Index validation analysis As a composite indicator, the IOI summarizes a latent concept that cannot be directly measurable by a single metric. Its construction requires the use of metrics of observable phenomena (such as the patent and trademark data analysed in the previous section) that are part of or closely related to its target concept. Therefore, it is important to validate the resulting measurement by examining the concordance of IOI scores with two groups of variables: (i) alternative measures of national innovation performance or comparable constructs for the same countries, and (ii) metrics of observable phenomena that resemble or are closely related to innovation output, such as inputs to innovation or framework conditions known to facilitate the process of innovation. A strong relationship between the IOI and such metrics would provide further evidence that the IOI adequately approximates the innovation output performance of the assessed countries.
