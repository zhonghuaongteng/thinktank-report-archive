Summary
• Different categories of federal R&D show different associations with U.S. productivity
growth.
- A one-standard-deviation increase in the share of basic research is associated with
cumulative labor productivity gains of about 2.1 percentage points over ten years.
- A comparable increase in the development R&D share is associated with cumulative
declines of about 2.2 percentage points.
- Applied research and R&D plant investments are associated with positive but less
precisely estimated effects of 1.7 and 1.4 percentage points.
• The productivity effects of basic research operate primarily through total factor
productivity (roughly three-quarters of the cumulative effect). By contrast, for R&D
plant, capital deepening is the only positive channel that is statistically significant. The
mechanisms for applied research effects are split between the two.
• Holding fixed total research and development spending, our illustrative portfolio
optimization framework, which treats R&D categories as productivity investments,
results in Pareto-optimal compositions that allocate roughly three times the historical
share to basic research (47 percent vs. 17 percent) and reduce by a factor of three the
historical share on development research (18 percent vs 59 percent). We note that this
framework solely optimizes for productivity, thus not accounting for other potential
research objectives (e.g., military competitiveness).

Substantively, four years corresponds to a single presidential term, which represents a natural institutional cycle for federal R&D priority setting. Incoming administrations typically revisit R&D budget priorities as part of a broader policy agenda, and the federal budgeting process introduces additional implementation lags that extend the influence of any given administration’s priorities across its full term. A four-year lag structure thus allows the forecasting equations to condition on a complete cycle of political priority setting, helping to absorb the predicable component of compositional change driven by shifts in administrations.
