'Widening' indicator Leveraging the potential for inclusive European research and innovation

The EU's Horizon Europe framework programme promotes European innovation. The current Horizon Europe programme uses 'Widening' measures to target Member States , support their capacity to innovate, close gaps between EU countries and move towards performance convergence. This study provides a framework and associated indicator options for establishing a systemic 'Widening 2.0' methodology which could prove instrumental in discussing post-Horizon Europe 'w idening' support measures. It is based on assess ment of research and innovation indicators of targeted 'widening' countries, looking at the economic and research impact that Horizon Europe may have had between 2018 and 2025.

IV Executive summary Despite broad consensus that science, research, and innovation are vital for Europe 's future prosperity and security, the EU 's innovation system is failing to deliver. The gap with the U nited States is deepening and China has overtaken the EU -27, alongside weak convergence among Member States. Evidence shows that this lag stems less from public research and innovation ( R&I) funding than from weak business sector innovation, with Europe struggling to translate strong science into globally successful firms – an increasingly serious weakness in a fast-moving, artificial intelligence ( AI)-driven and geopolitically competitive innovation landscape. While boosting EU innovation capacity is now a central policy priority and debates focus on increasing public funding, a key unresolved challenge remains how to balance excellence- based funding w ith broader participation, given persistent disparities in research and development ( R&D) and innovation performance across Member States. The 'Widening participation and spreading excellence actions' introduced in the previous framework programmes have been supporting Member States in improving their capacities to adopt and adapt new technologies. These initiatives aimed to strengthen research institutions by facilitating networking, partnerships with leading institutions, and recruitment of skilled sta ff. Although it is premature to evaluate the full impact of these 'Widening measures', recent assessments show that these are likely to deliver some early evidence of success stories. However, there seem to be persistent barriers for participation of 'Widening' countries and impacts are highly heterogeneous, often depending on complementary national efforts. Genuine sustainable change tends to depend on R&I investment and reforms at national level, challenging the attraction of international researchers, and long-term sustainability through effects generated from research results. The EU's next framework programme for research and innovation will continue to support 'Widening' measures aimed at reducing R&D performance gap s between Member States, ( European Commission communication of 4 July 2025). According to the draft proposal, the 'Widening' programme should focus on delivering ' concrete measures ' for building the R&D capacity of countries that have been lagging in terms of participation in Horizon Europe. More emphasis will be placed on incentivi sing them to catch up, e.g. by mon itoring and recogni sing efforts in their commitment to using national strategies to benefit from additional EU support, tailoring access to 'Widening' funding schemes on this commitment and by using the European Semester process for monitoring. It could also involve a larger share of structural funds invested in R&I . Consideration is also given to splitting eligible countries into categories, depending on their position in their advance towards catching up. The overall aim of this study is to provide a framework for establishing a systemic ' Widening 2.0' methodology, which will be instrumental when discussing ' Widening' support measures once the Horizon Europe framework programme ends. It discusses new or improved existing metrics which can be used for assessing which Member States to select for which support measures, considering both the needs, but also the capacity for effectively 'catching up'. Overall, the proposed framework allows for sound policy option s when discussing new R&I support programme structures for 'Widening'. The report proposes options for possible different choices, in which case their respective strengths and weaknesses are discussed. The study used the latest 2025 version of a broad tool for assessing a country's innovation capacity - the European Innovation Scoreboard (EIS) developed by the European Commission 's Joint Research Centre (JRC). The EIS variables were used as indicators for the various dimensions composing a country's margin for innovation capacity building, complemented, where relevant, with other sources. The dimensions for ' Absorptive capacity ', 'Access to technology ', and ' Creative capacity' (multiple dimensions) allowed the classification of 'Widening' countries into five categories of 'Potential for Innovation Capacity' (PIC).

V Two principles guided the underlying analysis. First, as the analysis has shown the importance of 'systemic' performance, countries need to perform well on all the prerequisites for innovation capacity building. Second, when defining benchmark levels for scores, the study considered the position of countries on their innovation development path, i.e. scores will be compared with the overall 'Widening' benchmark. The analysis illustrates that addressing new 'Widening' measures to unleash the innovation capacity of lagging countries to catch up is a complex challenge that requires more than simply addressing participation in EU f ramework programmes. It requires considering the heterogeneity among 'Widening' countries in their position and potential for innovation capacity building. Continued 'Widening' support could use the proposed PIC classification to tailor 'Widening' instruments according to identified Member St ate bottlenecks and weak spots. Hence, the focus could be not only on supporting participation in framework programmes in general, but on supporting participation in those programme s/pillars, tackling a country 's weaknesses, and which measures have the best scope for improving a country's PIC.

The background to the 'Widening' EU policy scene There is no lack of reports these days arguing how important science, research and innovation are to take Europe into a prosperous, clean, safe and healthy future for its citizens. 'It is investment in research and innovation that will ensure Europe's (and its Member States') future security, strength and freedom and thus Europe's ability to successfully navigate in turbulent times' (Draghi (2024)1). Yet, the EU's innovation system is currently not in shape to deliver. The evidence showing how big the gap has become between EU's research and innovation system with the US and China is not new (e.g. Veugelers (2024 )2), but the Draghi (2024) report proved to be a loud wake- up call. The innovation gap relative to the US is 'Widening' rather than shrinking, and China has managed to overtake the EU27. Together with the lacking EU catching up worldwide, there has been very slow convergence intra EU27, hampering EU's overall innovation performance. A deeper dive into the evidence reminds us that the major source of EU's persistent lagging behind and failure to catch up with the US is not so much in its public R&I funding, but in its business sector R&I. While Europe may have nurtured pockets of great science, it typically succeeds less in turning them into innovative corporate successes, compared to the US, and more recently China. This has been a long -diagnosed challenge for the EU, which policy does not seem able to address. This missing dynamic business innovation has become even more critical in the current fast changing and highly competitive global AI powered innovation environment. In addition, the global innovation scene is increasingly being characteri sed by a race between US and China for technology sovereignty and dominance, protected by domestic fences. Powering EU's innovation capacity has become centr al in current EU policy priority discussions. Reports of various stakeholders preparing their position on the upcoming new EU budget for Research, FP10 (e.g. the Heitor (2024) 3 report), complemented the momentum. Most of the policy discussion calls for an increase in public funding, although there is no consensus between Member States on whether and how to support R&D through EU-level instruments or through coordination of instruments at the level of Member States. Considering evidence indicating weak or no convergence in R&D and innovative performance across Member States , the question is how to reconcile broader participation in the public and private R&D&I ecosystems in the EU, with excellence-based public and private selection. Differences in R&D&I performance create a divide that affects participation in public programme s such as the Framework Programmes (FPs) where funding is awarded based on excellence. Lower participation further limits underperforming countries' ability to improve their research and innovation systems. The ''Widening' Participation and Spreading Excellence actions' introduced in the current FP Horizon Europe (HEU), is targeted at supporting Member States in improving their capacities to adopt and adapt new technologies. These initiatives aimed to strengthen research institutions by facilitating networking, partnerships with leading institutions, and recruitment of skilled staff. Although it is premature to evaluate the full impact of these ' 'Widening' measures', several recent studies, e.g. by the EC itself and the European Court of Auditors (ECOA) have assessed the changes brought by the initiative (ECOA, 2022)4. 3 2. This report: Developing a framework for a 'Widening' methodology Evaluating and monitoring of 'Widening' measures requires an effects-based approach entailing ex- ante and ex-post micro and macro assessments of long - and short -run impacts of specific instruments and of the FP portfolio in general. To what extent does ''Widening' Participation and Spreading Excellence' unleash the innovation capacity of lagging Member States? The relevant key performance indicator to use is the contribution of the portfolio of instruments to nurture the next scientific and technology ideas that will boost business innovation and growth. In the light of the ongoing discussions on the role of ' 'Widening' measures' in the next FP10 (2028- 2034) or its alternatives (e.g. European Competitiveness Fund )6, it is important to reassess and update existing metrics defining the distribution of these measures in the R&I support programme after HEU. The overall aim of this study is to provide a framework for establishing a systemic ' 'Widening' 2.0' methodology, which will be instrumental when discussing ''Widening'' support measures after HEU. It will discuss new or improved existing metrics (''Widening' 2.0' indicator) used for assessing which Member States to select for which ' 'Widening'' support measures, considering both the need for ''Widening'' support, but also the capacity for effectively 'catching up'. The framework for such a systemic ' 'Widening' 2.0' methodology will focus on indicators that are relevant, measurable, achievable, and time-bound: • The methodology should be multidimensional, building on various metrics involving Science, Research, Technology, Innovation, Business Structure, Productivity. • The methodology will be relevant for an ex-ante assessment of strength and weaknesses in R&D&I of Member States as well as for an assessment of the capacity for catching -up through 'Widening' (openness, absorptive capacity, creative capacity). Consequently, metrics should go beyond R&I investment level as % of GDP (total, public, private) to also include metrics such as number of people and skills trained for R&D&I, diffusion/connectedness measures such as metrics of researchers' mobility and cooperation. • The framework and associated indicator options will consider the dynamic dimension of metrics that are used to assess initial positions, as well as trends and patterns of growth/catching up. • The framework should consider (trends in) Horizon participation metrics. • The framework will critically assess its composite dimensions, with appropriate differentiation of dimensions when needed. Overall, the proposed framework will allow for sound policy options when discussing new R&I support programme structures for ''Widening''. The report will propose options for possible different choices, in which case their respective strengths and weaknesses will be discussed. The proposed framework will also be useful for future bigger in-depth studies for evaluating R&D&I capacity development across Member States and time.

Trends in EU science, technology and innovation performance and cohesion The innovation capacity of nations measures their ability to generate new ideas and to translate them into economic growth and prosperity (Furman et al, 2002) 7. Because of differences in initial conditions and because of differences in how EU countries have sought to create innovation-based growth, we can expect substantial differences between European countries in terms of innovation capacity. We would however expect that the process of EU integration would allow lagging countries to catch up faster, pushing convergence within the EU in terms of innovation capacity, along with economic convergence. A broad tool for assessing a country 's innovation capacity is the European Innovation Scoreboard (EIS) developed by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC). 8 The EIS uses multiple indicators (32 in recent editions), but the overall score for each country is represented by Summary Innovation Indicator (SII). This section will use the latest 2025 version of SII scores to trace trends in EU-27's overall position in innovation capacity, as well as trends in the cohesion within the EU-27 on this indicator. Overall EU Science, Technology and Innovation (STI) performance, as measured by the trends in its SII score relative to the rest of the world, is showing, in line with Draghi report et al, how EU is losing in relative position in innovative performance. 7 4. The 'Widening' programme in Horizon Europe With its dedicated 'Widening' participation and spreading excellence' programme in its Framework Programme, the Commission has the objective to address the cohesion within the EU on innovative performance, by 'Widening' the participation in the Framework Programme of lagging countries and to improve their capacities in research and innovation, as well as to support the reforms of national policies, within an overall objective of strengthening the European Research Area. Countries which are targeted ' 'Widening' countries', are less performing in terms of research and innovation with respect to the average EU 27. Within the Horizon Europe regulation 'Widening' countries are: Bulgaria (BG), Croatia (HR), Cyprus (CY), Czech Republic (CZ), Estonia (EE), Greece (EL), Hungary (HU), Latvia (LV), Lithuania (LT), Malta (MT), Poland (PL), Portugal (PT), Romania (RO), Slovakia (SK), Slovenia (SI) and all associated countries with equivalent characteristics in terms of R&I performance and the Outermost Regions. The actions within this ' 'Widening'' objective are mainly Coordination and Support Actions, i.e. funding schemes sustaining activities that contribute to the objectives of Horizon Europe Programme, normally excluding research and innovation activities. All organisations eligible in Horizon Europe can participate in the 'Widening' actions but only organisations based in 'Widening' countries can participate as coordinators. Its main types of actions are: • Teaming: action of support to the creation of new centres of excellence or the upgrade of existing ones, located in 'Widening' countries, building on partnerships at European level. At least two parties must be involved in the action, a legal entity established in a 'Widening' country operating as coordinator and, a counterparty, a university or research organisation, internationally recognised for excellence in research and innovation, established in another EU Member State or Associated Country. • Twinning: supports joint research projects, new or ongoing, aimed at strengthening scientific excellence and innovation capacity in a defined research area as well as the scientific quality of the partners involved in the collaborations. Under this action, a research institution located in a 'Widening' country act as coordinator in a partnership with at least two other internationally leading institutions/bodies located in other Member States or Associated Countries. • Pathways to Synergies: This action supports beneficiaries of regional funding to increase cross-border collaborations and reduce access barriers to different funding systems. • Hop-on Facility; This action aims to facilitate 'Widening' countries' research institutions joining already ongoing Horizon R&I projects. • ERA Fellowships: ERA Fellowships are open to researchers moving to the EU from a third country or moving within the EU to a 'Widening' country. All the 15 EU27 'Widening' Member States have scores below EU average on S II on average in the period 2018-2025, confirming their selection as 'Widening country'. Most of them have made some progress in reducing their innovation gap (see Figure 3 below). This holds most notably for Estonia which has made most progress and even managed to score above the EU27 average since 2021. Portugal was the best performing 'Widening' country in 2018, but has struggled to reduce its remaining gap since, testimony of the challenge of building a long- term consistent catching up process.

The countries which are below 75% of the EU27 SII score (NB: a benchmark considered as a 'Widening' criterium in the next framework programme, cf. supra) are since 2022: HU, SK, HR, LV, PL, BG and RO. In 2018, also CZ, LI, EL scored below 75%, but they have gradually made sufficient progress on SII to no longer be below 75%. While HU and PL have made some progress on reducing their gap, they nevertheless remain at below 75%. All this further illustrates the considerable heterogeneity in innovative performance within the current group of 15 'Widening' countries and the heterogeneity in catching up performance. Drivers for innovation capacity and for capacity for catching up: A short review of the literature What can we learn from the literature in terms of heterogeneity in innovative capacity and convergence in innovative capacity? (see also Veugelers & Schweiger (2016), Veugelers (2010) a.o.). An important dimension explaining the heterogeneity across countries in their innovation capacity, is differences in levels of initial development and a country's initial position relative to the technology frontier (Verspagen (1991)12; Aghion & Howitt (1998)13). For countries at still early stages of development, far from the technology frontier, the contribution of technology to growth runs through the country 's ability to effectively absorb new technologies (World Bank (2008)14; Lall (2002)15). There are two key ingredients explaining differences across countries in effective technology take- up. The first is access to foreign technology . This requires openness through trade, foreign direct investment (FDI) and other forms of international cooperation. Second, access to (foreign) knowledge needs to be combined with a sufficiently developed 'absorptive capacity ' (Cohen and Levinthal ( 1989) 16) or ' social capability ' (Abramovitz (1986)17), in order to deliver growth. This absorptive capacity depends on many factors, including the extent to which a country: has a technologically -literate workforce and a highly-skilled elite; promotes a pro-investment climate; permits the creation and expansion of firms using higher -technology processes; permit s access to capital; and has adequate public -sector institutions to promote the take-up of critical technologies when private demand or market forces prove inadequate (World Bank (2008)). For countries at higher levels of development, closer to the technology frontier, another factor that explains heterogeneity in innovation capacity comes into play, namely divergence in countries' own indigenous innovative capacity (Hoekman et al (2006) 18). First, a country's own R&D complements the take -up of existing technology because it is a component of absorptive capacity. Foreign technologies frequently need modifi cation so that they are suitable for domestic circumstances (Acemoğlu et al. (2006)19). But, at higher levels of development, a country 's own R&D can increasingly substitute adoption of existing technologies, allowing the generation of new technologies, pushing the technology frontier. At this stage, countries require technological know- how, reflected in public and private R&D resources. They also need to be able to incentivise or reward innovation (e. g. Furman et al (2002) 20). Indicators for assessing the potential of countries to catch up on innovation capacity Following the literature (see sections above), the conditions influencing innovative capacity building can be divided into those affecting access to technology, those affecting firms ' capacity to adopt and fully understand existing technologies, and those affecting the ability to create innovations new to markets. The importance of each of them will depend on differences in the position of a country on its path to development.21 Access to technology depends on a country 's economic openness, the availability and use of ICT infrastructure and the extent to which openness (to FDI and trade) facilitates the transfer of technology. Absorptive capacity is underpinned by the quality of secondary and undergraduate education and the effectiveness of on-the-job training. Creative capacity is further underpinned by favorable incentives to engage in building innovative capacity . Business environment conditions for innovation include broader business environment conditions (or general framework conditions). Such conditions affect the operations and decisions of all firms in the economy, including firms that innovate. Business environment conditions include the quality of institutions and the functioning of product, labour and financial markets. 48 11. 'Widening' index options Based on Tables 12, 13 and 14, several options for 'Widening' countries support in the next FP could be considered. For all three options and regardless number of selected countries, it is very important to note that future 'Widening' measures should not only on support participation in FP in general, but specifically target those programmes/pillars tackling a country 's weaknesses with the best scope for PIC improvement. 'Widening' index option 1: Strict scenario The first scenario omits the top five best-performing 'Widening' countries. Using an algorithm to select 'Widening' countries with potential for innovative capacity building according to the criteria that: they do not have more than one bottleneck, have no more than two weak spots and do not accumulate one bottleneck with one other weak spot; the following countries would pass: Czechia, Estonia, Malta, Portugal and Slovenia. Among these, Slovenia has no weak spots; Estonia has only one weak spot (on the trade components under 'effects', not on productivity). These countries can be gradually phased out of the ' Widening' support, on the condition that they will sustain their innovation capacity building growth trend and that they will address any persisting weak points. 'Widening' index option 2: Moderate scenario The second scenario loosens the above selection criteria. Using a more conservative algorithm to select 'Widening' countries with potential for innovative capacity that do not have a bottleneck, Malta would drop out of the top group , as it has a bottleneck on f inance. Only Czechia, Estonia, Portugal and Slovenia. would therefore remain in the best-performing group. As in Option 1, these countries can be gradually phased out of 'Widening' support. 'Widening' index option 3: Inclusive scenario The third scenario would retain all 'Widening' countries within the 'Widening' support. All 'Widening' countries still score below the EU -27 average, when cross -referencing their PIC ranking with the success rate of their HEU funding applications, confirming their status as 'Widening'. 49 12. Concluding recommendations Based on our analysis of the evidence on the innovation development and HEU involvement of 'Widening' countries, we propose the following recommendations, very much in line with the draft proposal on (European Commission, 4 July 2025 communication). • Call for differentiating among 'Widening' along their position in the innovation capacity development path and depending on their capacity to further develop (see PIC). • This differentiating should be translated into 'Widening' instruments tailored to these subcategories. • 'Widening' instruments could even be tailored made for individual countries depending on their weak spots (eg international connectivity; quality of HEI institutes, digital skills…). • Focus not only on supporting participation in FP in general, but on supporting participation in those programmes/pillars tackling a country's weak spots and which have the best scope for improving a country's PICs. • Select and evaluate 'Widening' programmes and participation in FP into effectiveness on PIC. • Select and evaluate 'Widening' programmes and participation in FP on leveraging these into national public and private capacity building. • Remove bottlenecks for 'Widening' countries (esp ecially those in 3 % deficit) to activate national funding for interventions that are PIC cleared. • Remove bottlenecks for using other EU funding ( e.g. Structural Funds) for funding interventions that are PIC cleared. • Do regular country profiling on PIC (eg see OECD Innovation System profiling of countries). • Monitor, evaluate and remedy 'Widening' countries usage of national and EU instruments, using e.g. the Semester toolbox.

50 13. Caveats and suggestions for further analysis The methodology to classify countries into 'Widening' status and within 'Widening' status into their PIC status relies on EU-JRC Innovation Scoreboard data, which are tested and widely used indicators in innovation policy circles. Nevertheless, indicators could be improved and added to better reflect the factors that shape innovation capacity building. • E.g. on linkages, patent and publication citation information could be used to finetune the link between science and technology. • E.g. on innovation active enterprises, more information on new and young firms, including science-based start-ups could measure deeper the scope for business dynamism. • E.g. on international connectedness, more information on international mobility of researchers and entrepreneurs to capture brain circulation, brain drain, brain gain. The proposed methodology composes factors by using multiple indicators as input, and thus reduces the sensitivity of the classification outcome to individual factors and outliers, increasing the robustness of the classification. Also, the systemic nature of innovation capacity building, requiring a simultaneous presence of prerequisites, lead to a rather robust classification, as performance correlates across factors. Bottom performers score typically low on all indicators, top performers high. Nevertheless, robustness checks with various scenarios of different weights to individual components within factors and to different weights of factors in the overall PIC score, should be on the radar, if only to show the sensitivity of the results to each component. More analysis is needed to match the size and format of 'Widening' support to the various PIC categories. More analysis is needed to evaluate how 'Widening' support contributes to • improvements in PIC scores for recipients, • reduce gaps between 'Widening' countries and the EU27 average, • improve EU27 cohesion and to improve EU27's overall innovation capacity.
