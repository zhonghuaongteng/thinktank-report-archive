Quantum mechanics is based on the following principles, which are also key to how quantum technologies work:244 — Superposition: In quantum physics, superpo- sition refers to the superimposition of several quantum states. This means that a quantum system is not initially in a clearly defined state without external influence but rather occupies several possible states simultaneously and the results of physical processes can only ever be predicted in the form of probability.245 — Decoherence: Superposition states of a quantum object only exist if the object is completely isolated from its environment. Even the smallest environmental influences cause the superposition to dissolve and thus a specific state to be realized. However, environmental influences are unavoidable in any kind of measurement. — Entanglement: Entanglement creates so- called ‘quantum correlations’ between particles. As soon as the state of one of two entangled quantum objects is measured in a suitable way and thus determined, the state of the other is also determined, even if they are far apart. 23 mm von oben 15 mm von rechts

It involves encoding the key for encrypting and decrypting messages in quantum states and exchanging it via specially set up communication channels. The actual message can be transmitted via conven- tional communication channels. A vivid metaphor for QKD is the exchange of a briefcase containing sensitive information between two diplomats, the contents of which would be destroyed if an unauthorized person tried to access the docu- ments. In contrast to traditional cryptographic methods, which use mathematical algorithms to generate keys, QKD is based on principles of quantum mechanics (cf. box B 2-1). A fundamen- tal concept here is the superposition between two states. It changes measurably as soon as an attempt is made to intercept the information about the state of the quantum system during transmission between sender and recipient. This principle makes QKD a physically secure method for exchanging information. However, the actual security always depends on the specific hardware implementation. Among the established technologies in quantum sensing, magnetic field sensors are particularly rel- evant in terms of market volume. They play a key role in medical diagnostics, for example in measur- ing the weak magnetic fields of the heart or brain using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). In the form of atomic clocks that send precise time sig - nals for positioning, quantum sensors also provide the basis for today’s satellite-based navigation (e. g. GPS). Furthermore, quantum-based technologies are used in semiconductor production. New Quantum Sensors Enable High-Precision Measurement Particularly in fields of application with extremely high precision requirements, new quantum sensors can play an increasingly important role. For exam - ple, the possibilities for using existing technologies can be significantly expanded by new magnetic field sensors, as they enable more precise measurements or no longer require complex cooling. In addition, the development of optical atomic clocks, which are 100 times more accurate than established atomic clocks, can in future make satellite-based position- ing more precise to within a few centimetres or even millimetres. Moreover, novel magnetic field sensors can circumvent the susceptibility of satel - lite-based systems to interference, thus enabling fully autonomous navigation. Quantum methods with entangled photons promise advances in medi- cal diagnostics, e. g. in the early detection of cancer (cf. box B 2-1). Methods such as ghost imaging en- able high-resolution images to be obtained with low illumination, for example for imaging at the cellular level. These methods minimize radiation exposure and allow detailed, long-lasting measurements of light-sensitive cells without damaging them.275 Quantum sensors are usually capital goods that are traded on B2B markets and promise excellent value creation potential for applications in downstream markets. The development of new technologies must therefore be closely aligned with the respective user needs. It is expected that advances in quantum sensing will come from continuous improvements rather than radical technological leaps. Particular challenges currently exist in improving user-friend- liness, miniaturization and robustness. Quantum technologies, in particular quan - tum computing and quantum communication, have been a key part of this strategy for 15 years, with the goal of achieving a leading role in important technologies by 2035. As the largest research organization in the world, reporting directly to the State Council, the CAS coordinates major initiatives such as satellite projects and the development of quantum commu- nication infrastructures. In 2017, a ‘National Lab - oratory for Quantum Information’ was also estab - lished, which, according to expert reports, has been provided with substantial funding.281 In 2018, the EU launched the Quantum Flagship Initiative, a ten-year plan to promote quantum technologies under the leadership of the European Commission. The focus is on coordinating national programmes, networking key players and promot - ing strategic partnerships. 282 Within the European Commission, the Directorate-General for Commu - nications Networks, Content and Technology (DG CONNECT) has been responsible for digital strate- gies and the Directorate-General for Research and Innovation (DG RTD) for the funding of projects via Horizon Europe. Technology Transfer Included in All Strategies In the USA, public-private partnerships are accel - erating technology transfer, i. e. the transfer of sci- entific knowledge into economic application. The European Investment Bank (EIB) is also providing capital for start-ups and SMEs to finance projects in the field of quantum technologies. Quantum Computing Associated with Security Risks Predictions as to when a universally applicable quan- tum computer will be available for practical appli - cations vary considerably.302 Nevertheless, experts agree that future quantum computers will achieve a level of computing power that will enable them to break current encryption standards in communi - cation and data security. This constitutes a signifi - cant risk for the security architectures in military, political and civil communication. Adjustments to the security standards are therefore indispensable to ensure data and communication security in the long term, especially for critical infrastructures. The state has a pivotal role to play here. The risk of so-called ‘harvest now, decrypt later’ attacks is particularly critical. In such attacks, sen - sitive data is intercepted, stored for years or de- cades and later decrypted with sufficient computing power. In view of the long transition period until the full introduction of new encryption methods, and the fact that these attacks already represent a significant security risk today, the need for action here is urgent. In addition to the security risks arising from the prospective computing power of quantum comput- ers, risks are foreseeable that could arise from uni- lateral dependencies in the provision of hardware.
