This first Strategic Research and Innovation Agenda (SRIA) for Earth Observation sets out a comprehensive framework for Copernicus' research and technological development. It outlines key priorities for the Horizon Europe Work Programmes for 2025 -2027 and beyond into the next Multi-annual Financial Framework, ensuring that Copernicus and its products and services remain state-of-the-art. Copernicus, a key component of the EU Space Programme, provides Earth observation data through satellites, contributing missions, and in-situ data sources. This wealth of information is transformed into actionable services across six thematic areas: land, ocean, atmosphere, climate change, emergency, and security. The SRIA aims to ensure the continuity and evolution of these services. The SRIA o utlines how Earth Observation (EO) is integral to the EU Policy goals, such as in the implementation of the European Green Deal. The SRIA will contribute to guiding Copernicus' evolution, emphasizing agile, resilient data streams, digital transformation, and expanded services. Enhancing policy uptake and downstream adoption is crucial, ensuring Copernicus meets evolving user needs and drives economic growth, societal well -being, and security in Europe. The report emphasizes the need for comprehensive support for Copernicus' services, ensuring they stay relevant and beneficial for European and global policy goals. The SRIA addresses the individual R&I needs of each service and their components. The SRIA also addresses horizontal elements and priorities to create synergies and efficiencies across services. Key initiatives include Thematic Hubs, which centralize access, enhance services, and target specific cross-cutting applications. Computing and data infrastructures, Digital Twins, and AI/ML also play pivotal and growing roles in advancing Earth observation. Coordination across entities, EU policies, and global organizations fosters seamless integration, maximizing Copernicus' impact. The strategy emphasizes the evolution of services and technologies, parti cularly through research, data integration, and user -centric developments. Collaborative efforts with European and global partners further strengthen Copernicus, aligning it with broader policy goals and ensuring effective implementation across diverse sectors and communities. The SRIA concludes by emphasizing three dimensions that need to be addressed in its implementing. These are the Earth observation research and innovation activities themselves, the delivery and evolution of sustained Earth Observation services and the underlying policy needs for data and information derived from Earth observation. To monitor the successful implementation of the strategy a key focus should be on the interfaces and connections between the different dimensions identified. This then allows to identify specific metrics that can be followed.

This report outlines the new Strategic Research and Innovation Agenda (SRIA) for Earth observations enabling the Copernicus evolution for the remaining part of the M ultiannual Financial Framework (MFF) and beyond. It represents a major update of the roadmap developed back in 2020 for the current MFF and was justified by growing user requirements, new technologies, evolving policies at EU and global level and a changing global geopolitical context. The Copernicus evolution for the period 2021 -2028 focuses on continued and more agile observations, service uplifting to take advantage of the digital transformation (artificial intelligence and machine learning, cloud and edge computing, etc.), more federated data infrastructures to enable data analytics, and promoting downstream market uptake of Copernicus products. At this mid -term juncture, users require increased resilience and secure earth observations, enhanced knowledge provision, interactivity and decision-making tools, interoperability and AI-readiness, which can enable mass adoption and on-demand offer. The SRIA provides a longer -term perspective for Research and Innovation to inform the Copernicus evolution but also to justify and leverage investments in Earth observation. It complements or supplements other assessments such as those developed by Entrusted Entities and agencies to look at the broader potential of Earth observation beyond the strict EU Space Programme and its Copernicus component standpoint. Whilst Copernicus provides short -to-medium term research support at pre - operational level, the scope of the SRIA is to help shaping the long-term evolution of Copernicus and Earth observation across the European Un ion Research Programme. Introduction 1.1 Scope of the Strategic Research and Innovation Agenda The scope of the present document is to summarize and outline the Strategic Research and Innovation Agenda (SRIA) for Copernicus and Earth observations(1), the aim of which is to serve as a framework and reference for calls under the upcoming Horizon Europe(2) Work Programmes for period 2025-2027 and initial preparation for the subsequent EU Research and Innovation framework programme 2028-2035. It integrates the Strategic Research Agenda for the Copernicus Security Service (SRA-CSS) and represents an update of the Copernicus Service Evolution roadmap developed back in 2020. It addresses requirements of the Union Space Programme(3) to support the Union’s research and technological development activities carried out under Regulation (EU) 2021/695 [Horizon Europe]. 1.2 Copernicus and the EU Space Programme Copernicus is the Earth observation component of the EU Space programme, looking at our planet and its environment to benefit all European citizens, their wellbeing and security. The Copernicus backbone relies on a set of dedicated satellites (the current Sentinel family and upcoming Sentinel Expansion and Sentinel Next Generation missions), contributing missions (public and commercial satellites) , and in -situ (non - space) data. Copernicus services process, analyse, transform, and exploit the wealth of satellite and non-satellite (‘in- situ’) data into numerical models, products and applications towards timely and actionable information to help service providers, public authorities, and other international organisations improve European citizens' quality of life and beyond. These value -adding activities are streamlined through six the matic core services covering the land, ocean, atmosphere, climate change, emergency and security domains , the scope of which is defined in the Space Programme Regulation. The information services, as well as the data from which they are derived, are accessible on a full, free, and open basis by anyone. The technological innovation, maturity, operational commitment, as well as the free and open data policy of Copernicus have positioned the EU and Copernicus as major players in today's space-based activities.

1.3 EU Strategy for Space R&I During the next decade, the EU will consolidate its position as a global space actor able to access space, to act in space, to communicate and navigate in space and on Earth, and to monitor space and the Earth for the well-being and security of its Member States and citizens. To achieve this, the European Commission is working towards a future space ecosystem that is competitive, sustainable, resilient, and future -oriented. It should be underpinned by modern space flagships (Galileo, EGNOS, Copernicus, GOVSATCOM, SSA, and IRIS2) in -line with user requirements and demands. Space R&I has a considerable impact, not only on the growth of the space sector itself, but also on benefits to society in general . The spin-offs and cross fertilisation of R&I activities support the innovativeness of both the upstream and the downstream sector s. From a broader societal standpoint way beyond the space sector, there is substantial and quantitative evidence that space related research and development yields huge societal, economic, and environmental benefits for European citizens and end -users, estimated at 108.8 B EUR for the timeframe of 2017 – 20353 (a tenfold yield compared to the investment). An overall EU Strategy for Space R&I is needed taking a holistic approach towards an ambitious and comprehensive plan to support its Space Programme Flagships, and to actively drive the EU’s strategic autonomy, resilience and freedom of action in space and on Earth. Copernicus, in coherence with national and other inter-governmental satellite programmes, shall evolve in this context to provide state-of-the-art uninterrupted, high-quality, up-to-date and, free and open or where appropriate, secure, Earth observation data, products, applications and services to sustainably manage the environment we live in, in support of EU’s political agenda. 1.4 Horizon Europe and Earth Observation The vision of the EU Research and Innovation Programme Horizon Europe is, inter alia, to  tackle climate change,  achieve the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals,  boost the EU’s resilience, competitiveness, and growth,  facilitate collaboration and strengthen the impact of research and innovation in developing, supporting, and implementing EU policies while tackling global challenges. Earth observation (EO) plays an important role in addressing these goals by providing ev idence and scientific understanding of the issues at stake and monitoring tools for environmental policy implementation. This includes the European Green Deal, as well as support for smart decisions to all sorts of end-users. Building upon the first Strategic Plan covering the years 2021-2024, the second Horizon Europe Strategic Plan sets out the key strategic orientations for research and innovation investments over the period 2025- 2027, acting as a compass to stay on course in the green and digital transition, responding to new challenges and taking into account the lessons learned since the launch of Horizon Europe and from Horizon 2020, which are captured in the la st section of this document in the form of implementation recommendations.

2 Context 2.1 Research for a user-driven programme Copernicus as a user -driven programme, was established to support EU and Member States policy requirements. The Space Programme Regulation makes the distinction between “core users” and “other users”. The Core Users include Union institutions and bodies and European, national, or regional public bodies in the Union or Copernicus Participating States uptake of Earth Observation. The other users reflect all other users, including research and education organisations, commercial and private bodies, charities, non-governmental organisations and international organisations. A fundamental aspect of the Regulation is that the core user requirements should drive the evolution of the programme. As new/additional user requirements become available from core users, there is a need to analyse the implications for the evolution of the programme. This includes m aking an assessment of gaps and opportunities with respect to the existing operational programme elements and components, which need to be addressed by additional research and innovation to advance the state-of-the-art. An important consideration is to un derstand the cascade and feedback along the value chain. As new needs and requirements are expressed, we should assess the fitness for purpose of existing products and services to address these as follows:  firstly, an assessment on the implications of requirements on the evolution of Core Services;  secondly, if an identified gap within the service is dependent on missing products provided from the ground segment, this may have additional implications for the evolution of products and the ground segment;  finally, if the gap still remains at the level of the ground segment, implying missing observations, this may have longer -term consequences for the evolution of the space component of the programme. Each step in this cascade process may also require additional research and innovation to bridge the gap between the different stages of the value chain, which can be addressed both through the Framework Programme and potentially from institutional research programmes of Copernicus partners ( e.g. ESA, EUMETSAT). The user’s requirement process undertaken by research and operational space agencies is a critical scouting function for future application areas . In undertaking a systematic approach to assessing the fitness-for-purpose gap assessment and cascading implications for the evolution throughout the value chain will provide a robust framework to ensure the evolution of the programme and advancing the state- of-the-art of the EU Earth observation capabilities traceable back to emerging user and poli cy needs of core users. The user-driven pull of the Space Programme should also closely monitor new science and technology developments such as NewSpace capabilities, artificial intelligence, etc., to stimulate broader and state-of-the-art services. 2.2 Core user uptake and emerging EU Policy needs The current MFF has seen a fundamental change in the recognition of the value of Earth observation and Copernicus in supporting policy. The United Nations (UN) established bodies, multilateral agreements and frameworks aiming at addressing the above issues , such as the Sustainable Development Goals (SDG), the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), including national commitments under the Paris Agreement of 2015, the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, the WMO and its Global Framework for Climate Services, the Early Warning for all initiative, the UNESCO -IOC, the UN Decade of Ocean Science, the UN Ocean Conference , and other conventions such as the UN Convention on Biological Diversity, the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea the UN Convention to Combat Desertification , the UN Convention on Long -Range Transboundary Pollution, the Montreal Protocol on Substances that deplete the Ozone Layer, etc.

Those multilateral frameworks are greatly benefitting from Copernicus and other European observing systems and infrastructures (like ICOS, EuroArgo, etc.). The European EO sector can strongly support EU commitments towards this global agenda. In this context, it is important to note the need to provide products and services in a consistent manner so that they can be use d both in EU Policy directly a s well as in the global provision of data for our commitments to these international Conventions and Treaties. For example, the use of Copernicus both in support of the EU Biodiversity Strategy and in the context of the indicators for the Global Biodiversit y Framework. The Group on Earth Observation (GEO), a partnership of more than 100 national governments and more than 100 Participating Organizations , envisions a world where trusted Earth intelligence is universally accessible and empowers society to achieve a sustainable future. The term Earth Intelligence refers to integrated Earth and social science derived knowledge and insights that inform strategic decisions(6). GEO advocates open sharing of EO data and knowledge for effective measures addressing the global environmental challenges. As one of the four co-chairs of GEO, the European Commission is engaged in the governance of GEO and contributes mainly with the EU’s R&I framework programmes to GEO work programme activities, i.e. the GEO flagships, initiatives and pilot initiatives. References to GEO are added in the Horizon Europe Work Programme to ensure the effectiveness of this contribution. Moreover, DG JRC leads the initiatives “GEO Human Planet ” and “Global Wildfire Information System (GWIS) ”. Initiatives like these drive the international engagement and support the implementation of the multilateral agreements listed above. Copernicus, with its free and open data policy , is a major European contribution to GEO, which offers a unique platform for international valorisation of the EU’s investment in EO. EuroGEO, the European GEO component, brings together environmental and Earth observation researchers and innovators in Europe, and creates impact and visibility on the global landscape. Those linkages between Copernicus and EuroGEO should be further strengthen , for example via the entrusted entities, especially on R&D . The EuroGEO community reflects the broad range of stakeholders involved in support to Europe’s contributions. This include s the research community, member state agencies, the private sector , European institutions and Entrusted entities. EuroGEO implements a user-driven research and innovation agenda and develops EO applications th at support user communities addressing EU policies like the European Green Deal and GEO priorities . Supported by Horizon Europe, EuroGEO fosters demonstration, incubation, up -scaling, and replication. EuroGEO also coordinates the European contribution to the GEO global initiatives and priorities. EuroGEO thus supports the valorisation of Copernicus outputs by delivering user-tailored services in Europe, and also on an international scale, through the GEO initiatives.

2.4 End-to-end value chain and High-level SWOT analysis Copernicus is the Earth observation component of the EU space programme and is also responsible for the full value chain of the system with its core services and its upstream and downstream components, including space, in-situ and ground processing segments, as well as applications and services value adding activities to serve public/institutional users as well as the broader market uptake. A recent Expert Group report(7) pointed to the need to enhance system resilience and non -dependence, to address policy requirements, to foster the commercial sector contribution, and to upgrade the space and ground infrastructures. The strategic SWOT analysis on Copernicus flagged the following main points:  Strength: since it moved into operations, Copernicus became a world top provider of state-of the- art and traceable EO big, continuous, reliable, free and open data and products, applications and services, exploited by a wide use base (hundreds of thousands), part of which even relying critically on Copernicus for their daily and secured operations and policy needs; Copernicus further benefits from synergies (e.g. on Sentinel 4 and 5) with European meteorological and climate satellites (e.g. MTG, EPS-SG) which are planned for the next 20+ years;  Weakness: t he EO supply depends partially on non -EU assets (e.g., access to launchers or raw material). Slow products uptake and limited agility compared to competitors , from industry in particular. Lack of integration between components (e.g. computing, data , services, in -situ networks), also prevents the full and timely exploitation and downstream user uptake of EU’s investment in Sentinels which is still to be achieved;  Opportunities: the gradual integration of Copernicus and other European EO and big data (e.g. IoT, data ecosystems), EU computing assets (i.e., EuroHPC) and science excellence in adding value to EO (processing, analysis, modelling, mining, fusion, AI/ML) are instrumental to the Green Deal and can transform the Copernicus next generation product offer and user experience by leveraging the latest space and digital technologies;  Threats: large private corporations, in particular non-EU ones, are becoming aggressively active on the EO and Copernicus service portfolio landscape, with more fit-for-purpose offers, posing a threat to Copernicus at risk of losing its leadership and user base, and for products to become obsolete. This could even undermine the uptake at policy level. Resilience of EU supply chains is another serious threat to Copernicus operations and evolution. Some of the above issues are being addressed at higher level ( e.g., Critical Raw Material act, Access to Space), others can be the purpose of R&I efforts under the present strategy. To be most effective, a long- term R&I commitment on the Copernicus evolution is required to enable this transformation. The recent developments in the AI and machine learning landscape can influe nce the SWOT analysis by intensifying threats and unveiling new opportunities. The integration of dedicated AI and machine learning -driven activities would expedite the positive impacts. Europe has a leading advantage in the area of weather, climate and ocean modelling, thanks to its state- of-the art observing and modelling capacities. Several Copernicus services directly contribute to and benefits from these capacities (C3S, CAMS, CEMS, CMEMS). The AI/ML is both a threat and opportunity for Europe’s leading position in this area, and in related downstream sectors.

2.5 Copernicus evolution The policy and operational nature of the programme makes it de facto a mandatory focus for strong R&I. EU policies are evolving on a continuous basis, with growing expectation on the programme to deliver on policy uptake as flagged by the European Court of Auditors’ report 2021 which, whilst praising Copernicus for “Technologically, the EU has succeeded in becoming a global player in terms of space -based earth observation...”, also urged for the “uptake of its space services to fully capitalise on the significant public investment made”(8). This was further confirmed by the Deep Dive meetings with Commissioner Breton and his political priorities for the Copernicus and New Space to evolve as a digital infrastructure (9) supporting the Green Deal and the resilience of the EU so that the programme continues providing the best in class environmental, security and emergency services. It is also worth recalling that the evolution of Copernicus is also an explicit requirement in Horizon Europe which: “ shall ensure that the R&I needs of the Union Space Programme ... are identified and established as part of the [HE] Programme’s strategic planning”. Copernicus Research and Innova tion aims at ensuring the highest quality of the services meeting user requirements. The commensurate research and development is typically organized in three tiers:  Tier 1: R&D on timescales of a few months conducted in-house within the services addressing work on the operational system and its configuration funded by Copernicus;  Tier 2: R&D on timescales of up to 1 -2 years to prepare and test evolutions of the services. This work, funded by Copernicus, is largely outsourced to maximize uptake of mature research;  Tier 3: medium to long-term R&D on several years focusing on upgrades of the services to address EU and global policy priorities. This work is typically funded by the Framework Programme (currently Horizon Europe ), including but not only the ‘Cop ernicus Service Evolution Calls’, or national research. The present document is primarily concerned with Tier 3 research. Whilst Copernicus is a world-class Earth observation programme, it needs continuous evolution to remain so to address nowadays constan tly evolving environmental, emergency and security challenges by leveraging the latest science and technology. To support the Green Deal and Digital Transformation, the evolution of Copernicus is planned along four key priorities: 1. 2. 3 Service priorities 3.1 Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S) Vision and mission The mission of C3S is to support adaptation and mitigation policies of the European Union by providing consistent and authoritative information about past, present and future climate. The societal demand for operationally produced insights about the climate is on a steep rise. The service provides free and open access to climate data and tools based on the best available science. Responding to the evolving requirements of our core users, C3S helps them meet their goals in dealing with the impacts of climate change. Main portfolio and expected evolution The portfolio of C3S dwells on observed variables chosen according to the concept of Essential Climate Variable (ECVs) as defined by the Global Climate Observing System (GCOS). These variables are used to monitor how the climate has been changing over the last decades but also how it will change in the future. Aside the products directly based on satellite observations (including an increased role ‘in a climate sense’ of Copernicus Sentinel data), C3S also offers access to global and regional climate reanalyses which integrate the satellite data with our physical understanding of the climate system. These reanalysis products are a key entry in a huge variety of climate adaptation projects and activities. The portfolio is enriched by the access to the multi -system seasonal predictions, the CMIP climate projections and the regional projections of CORDEX, in particular for Europe. All these products and datasets are supplied through the Climate Data Store (CDS) in a way that facilitates the combination of datasets and the generation of new products. The evolution of the portfolio will include the development of a much - enhanced global reanalysis (ERA6) and possibly regional reanalyses, an enriched set of climate predictions (at decadal, seasonal and sub-seasonal timescales), and the operational update of the IPCC climate atlas including strategies for the operational provision of the underlying data, its quality assessment, and recommendations for its use, application and limitation . The evolution of the service offering wil l also include a near real time climate attribution service focusing on heat stress. Target policies, customers, users C3S serves a substantial number of policy domains encompassed by the European Green Deal. These include climate mitigation and adaptatio n (UNFCC Paris agreement, DG CLIMA, DG ENER, Mission on Adaptation, DG ENV, EEA, JRC, EUCRA), health (DG SANTE, Climate and Health Observatory), renewable energy (DG ENER, DG CLIMA, ENTSO-E, etc.). C3S is also establishing a National Collaboration Programme modelled on the example of CAMS to allow a good articulation and flow of information between the European and national levels for public institutions, fostering collaboration and co -design. The Climate Data Store (CDS) in June 2023 reached 200.000 registered users to whom it typically provides some ˜150 TB of quality-controlled climate data per day. Major developments are in progress to upgrade the CDS in the form of a new Climate and Atmosphere Data Store (CADS) system, which will be common with CAMS and will offer increased cloud /edge computing capacity and a much more user -friendly, flexible, and powerful set of tools for developing applications.

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threats Strengths: C3S provides access to a vast array of quality controlled and operationally produced climate data, that has no equivalent in the world. This extensive data collection allows for robust analysis and assessment of climate trends and changes, and it is one of the reasons why the service and its reanalyses ERA5 featured so prominently in the latest assessment report of the IPCC. The service delivers timely and up-to-date information on climate variables, ensuring that users have access to the most recent climate data available. It also offers long-term datasets, enabling the examination of climate patterns and changes over extended periods. Weaknesses: The field of climate science and data analysis is rapidly evolving, driven by technological advancements. Climate change is inherently a complex and politically charged issue. C3S operates within a policy landscape that may undergo changes due to shifting priorities ( e.g., claims related to extreme event attribution) or geopolitical factors (energy independence in a changing climate). Opportunities: the effectiveness of the programme relies on its ability to engage and communicate with diverse stakeholders effectively, something that consequently needs to be supported, maintained and whenever appropriate and possible expanded. Leveraging new technologies, adapting to new policy frameworks, navigating international collaborations, and addressing well potential political pressures will be key for C3S. Threats: failure to adapt and incorporate new technologies could hamper C3S’ ability to deliver state-of- the-art services in NRT. C3S will need to stay at the forefront of emerging technologies ( e.g., ML/AI) and methodologies to effectively process and analyse vast amounts of climate data.

3.6 Copernicus Security Service (CSS) Vision and mission The Copernicus Security Services (CSS) provide timely information to support the Union’s security, safety, law enforcement, as well as EU international policies and commitments thro ugh three dedicated Earth Observation-based services: Copernicus Maritime Surveillance (CMS), Copernicus Border Surveillance (CBS), and Support to EU External and Security Actions (SESA). The services are available only to Institutional players in the Euro pean Union or to third parties under specific conditions justified along related EU policies. At its core, the Security Service exploits a network of satellites (Sentinel and Copernicus Contributing Missions) combined with ground-based data and information, and rely on data processing systems to generate added-value information for security applications. Security services are very demanding in terms of observation requirements, usually requiring short acquisition to delivery times during periods that may not coincide with satellite overpassing. They also often require fusion with other information based on local intelligence or in-situ data, to deliver actionable intelligence in response to specific user needs. Earth Observation (EO) technologies have advanced significantly, both in terms of observation and data processing capacities, as well as commercial delivery models, allowing for narrowing the gap between user expectations and service delivery. As such i t became key to incorporate new tech nologies into operational services, rendering them more fit for the purpose they were designed for , while countering obsolescence. The EU Space Programme Regulation, which establishes the rules and objectives for the EU space program for the period 2021 -2027, came into force in 2021 and marks the beginning of the second phase of CSS operations. It also underlines the importance of ensuring the evolution of CSS and reinforcing the link with Horizon Europe to meet the evolving security needs and challenges of the EU. Due to the operationally oriented nature of the Agencies under the Copernicus Security agreements, it was necessary to complement the effort done through dedicated R&D activities aligned with service evolution needs, while maximising the probabi lity of uptake of R&D results and foster Industrial competitiveness. To meet the challenges above, DG DEFIS and DG JRC, in coordination with the Copernicus Committee, User Forum and Entrusted Entities, joined efforts since 2022 to enhance the streamlining of research and innovation based on user demand and operational requirements. This joint effort materialises through a Strategic Research Agenda (CSS-SRA), with the following objectives in mind:  Ensure that the Copernicus Security Services evolve by utili zing stat e–of-the-art data, technologies and tools to deliver timely information in response to evolving user needs and security challenges.  Expand the service scope and increase user uptake, by improving fitness for purpose, complement the existent service portfolio, integrate new technologies.  Foster EU resilience, strategic and economic autonomy, and Industrial competitiveness. The CSS -SRA is published on a yearly basis setting the medium -term priorities for R&D and detailing actionable actions for the near-future. It also promotes dialogue among stakeholders and the exchanges

of best practices, avoids unnecessary duplications and promotes technology spin -off to operational environments. Main portfolio and expected evolution Copernicus Maritime Surveillance (CMS) service The CMS service provides EO products (satellite images and value-added products) to support monitoring of human activity at sea, covering seven function areas: maritime safety, maritime security, customs, law enforcement, marine pollution monitoring, fisheries control, and international cooperation. The service offers a wide geographical coverage based on high-quality earth observation data combined with maritime surveillance information . Users can obtain coverage in areas of Euro pean interest worldwide, with varying resolutions and sensor types. Additionally, the CMS service forms one component of the broader maritime picture offered by EMSA, allowing users to access other complementary datasets, such as vessel position reports, and selectively combine the data and integrate the data into their decision- making process. CMS looks further to explore emerging AI and ML technologies and introduction of the New Space Data to increase coverage, detection capacities, response time, and quality of services in general. Copernicus Border Surveillance (CBS) service The CBS service offers to its end -users a set of products based on EO data enriched with other data sources. By means of the Copernicus products, Frontex provides support to national border management authorities in managing irregular migration and combatting cross-border crime in line with the European Integrated Border Management. CBS offers 16 products categorised under three main groups: Reports; Map and Cartographic Products; Digital and Interactive Products. Currently, Frontex is moving from a general service approach towards a product centric approach. The transition has been introduced as a result of lessons learnt and users’ feedbacks and requirements collected during operations, workshops and survey, over the whole implementation period of the programme. The CBS evolution efforts are focused on further process automation and data fusion to ensure up -to- date picture of the EU external borders with the final aim to increase internal security and contribute to the EU strategic autonomy. Copernicus Support to EU External and Security Actions (SESA) service The SESA service aims to support the EU and its Member States by providing geospatial information and products related to security and crisis management. It focuses on monitoring and analysing activities and events of interest in regions outside the European Union. The service utilises a wide range of Earth Observation data, including satellite imagery, in combination with other sources of information to provide timely and accurate situational awareness to EU d ecision- makers and operational actors involved in EU External Action. It is an on -demand service that can be activated to react within very short timelines, as needed to respond to such as political or armed conflicts.

Copernicus Maritime Surveillance (CMS), implemented through EMSA, serves a wide range of users across over 60 National organisations, the United Nations and the EU Fisheries Control Agency (EFCA), providing services across a plethora of pol icies related to environmental compliance, law enforcement, security, safety at sea or supporting EU International commitments. Monitoring oil spills outside EU waters, helping National authorities in detecting Illegal an Unregulated Fisheries, supporting National Navies in capacity building in 3rd countries, or counter -piracy and anti -drug and smuggling operations are everyday examples of what is being address by CMS. As such, it serves a series of policies under DG MOVE, DG MARE, EEAS, TAXUD, HOME and clearly also Member States National policies. The Support to EU and Security Action Service (Sept. 2023) SESA replaced the precursor SEA service and is implemented by the EU SatCen. It offers support to a variety of users involved in security and crisis management activities as well as humanitarian efforts ’ It's worth noting that the lis t of users may vary depending on the scope of its operations, regional priorities, and the specific needs. SESA supports EU external policies and international commitments and some EU internal policies along its service portfolio. Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats Strengths: Copernicus security services provide valuable information to users over wide areas with difficult access, either due to remote location or difficult access due to e.g. topography or on -going conflicts. They are normally used as a first screening to plan or re -focus subsequent operations. CSS can save precious resources, e.g. over maritime areas inspecting only those areas where suspicious activity has been detected and, therefore, allowing large savings in unnecessary flyovers. Weaknesses: a major weakness has been the system reactivity and resolution, often falling well below what is needed for many applications. That is mostly due to the lack of persistency in observations, satellite revisiting times and processing capacities on ground often depending on human interpretation. Opportunities: The NewSpace sector, advances on processing capacity through e.g. machine learning, provide a clear opportunity to improve the service fitness for purpose. It is key to bring these new technologies to a level of maturity where they can be fully exploited by the services, as well as empowering the users in developing additional capabilities, relying on the ‘core’ services while adding specific know -how and using complementary informa tion. Technology demonstration in operational scenarios and validation, new processing paradigms for increased reactivity as well as data fusion with

non-EO data are therefore key elements for a R&D strategy for the coming years. This goes together with expansion of the current scope of the services to cater also for specific local and regional needs. Threats: given its sensitive nature, the security service s and associated data are provided on a need-to- know basis and related information, if not duly protected, may have severe consequences.

R&D in support to products and data quality Context and rationale A major asset of the Copernicus Space Component lies in the quality of the Sentinel core Level-1 and Level- 2 data products. In this respect, ESA in cooperation with partner agencies, conducts the appropriate R&D activities for Sentinel missions to maintain high-quality data and services, enabled not only by the excellence of their technologies but also by the Space Component of on -ground efforts in terms of calibration, validation, and processing algorithms performance. Those activities are fundamental to

preserve data accuracy and stability as well as to maintain the necessary level of confidence required for a wide spectrum of operational and science domains ( e.g., applications for Climate). A sustained and focused effort on R&D should be enhanced to suppo rt evolutions of core data products and guarantee the end-to-end performance of the overall Copernicus Programme. There is a clear need to further invest in that domain and coordinate ESA -led activities with SRIA to get the best out of the Copernicus Programme and facilitate that way, the interoperability and synergy of the Sentinels first generation missions and Copernicus Contributing missions with other EO satellites, including the future Copernicus Expansion Missions (CEM) and their integration into Copernicus services.

R&D and short-term improvements of Sentinel core data products As a consensus and ESA lesson-learned in operating Sentinel-1, 2, 3 (land part) and 5-P missions, there is a continuous need to further reinforce R&D and Cal/Val activities to support improvements of level-1 and level-2 operational processing algorithms and derive traceable uncertainty parameters. Those activities managed by ESA are instrumental to guarantee the quality of Sentinel core date products which are now used as the reference in Cal/Val for NewSpace and Copernicus Contributing Missions. Enhancing the data quality of Sentinel missions and characterizing uncertainty parameters all along their lifetime is also necessary to e nsure their proper exploitation by Copernicus services for higher level processing stages, data assimilation into operational models, downstream applications as well as in support to science projects funded under the Horizon Europe framework. Aligned with the fast-evolving SAR Analysis Ready Data landscape, one of short -term R&D priorities for Sentinel-1 is the definition of algorithms and processing flows which shall allow for a harmonized family of Analysis Ready Data (ARD) ranging from detected product s to ARD which explore the complex nature of the SAR data ( e.g., geocoded Single-Look Complex Data or Interferometric Data). On the geophysical products (Ocean Level-2), priorities include the expansion of the product family such as wave parameters retrieval. This should allow for a significant extension of the coverage of the product, further promoting data assimilation in weather models. For the ocean wind fields, the focus is on the improvement of the inversion with the addition of new constraints to re move of the a-priori wind information from ancillary data, which should extend the usability of the SAR retrieved product. For Sentinel -2, a line of short -term R&D activities is devoted to the improvement of atmospheric correction and the very important cloud screening algorithms generating Level-2A operational products. Despite the great success in improving these algorithms, these domains are still maturing within the scientific community, with international efforts exchanging in the framework of CEOS A CIX and CMIX activities. A second line of R&D activities focuses on the improvement of the pilot products Level -2H (harmonizing Sentinel-2 and other missions such as Landsat and PRISMA) and Level -2F (adding a fusion step to Level-2F product to bring spatial resolution of all input missions to the one of Sentinel-2). R&D will focus on bandpass adaptation, bands inter -calibration and BRDF (Bi -directional Reflectance Distribution Function) corrections. It would be important to define a common reference or standard in this context. Additional R&D are also recommended by the Copernicus Services and the user community in the field of Sentinel-3 altimetry, especially over complex areas over the sea ice and ice sheet margins where the potential of core level-1 and level-2 data products is not fully exploited. Such activities are fundamental not only for Climate, to narrow the gap between the Sentinel -3 Altimetry mission, the ESA CryoSat and CRISTAL but also for a better integration into operational numerical models. Long term R&D and preparation of the Copernicus Expansion Missions Despite the core sentinel products being already mature, several R&D are running or planned by ESA in cooperation with EUMETSAT to further improve their quality on longer term and to prepare the development operational processing algorithms for Copernicus Expansion Missions (CEM). Here below is a non-exhaustive list of priorities already identified by ESA. The planned approach for Sentinel-1 is to develop algorithms which can be also extended to ROSE-L CEM and favour multisensory and multi-mission applications. ROSE-L Level-2 product development will focus on the retrieval of forest and ocean variables. This shall allow both for continuity of Sentinel-1 geophysical products and for the synergy with products and applications from other ESA and non-European upcoming missions (e.g., BIOMASS and NISAR), promoting the development of multi -mission and multi -frequency applications. On the optical side, a long-term line of sentinel-2 R&D regards the improvement of the aquatic reflectance product. This product is currently under development based on the operational algorithms used by the Copernicus Services, and further improvement of the algorithms ( e.g., in terms of glint removal, white caps removal or atmospheric adjacency effects correction) is expected. Also, the preparation of LSTM activities is ongoing, with the development of the LSTM L2 Processor and Product aiming towards developing a product with different thematic layers for Land, -Ice and -Sea surface temperature as well as land and a quatic reflectance layers. For CHIME, L2 activities will focus on the Bottom -Of-Atmosphere (BOA) reflectance retrieval fulfilling the CEOS ARD surface and aquatic reflectance specifications. Additionally, a range of higher -level high priority products rela ted to canopy & leaf level and soil & mineralogy will be prototyped. Beyond Sentinel -3 based R&D to improve current altimetry processing methods for polar and inland water, the upcoming passive and active microwave CEM will also require dedicated R&D effor ts. For CRISTAL, the main challenge will consist in the characterization of dual band acquisitions over the ice. This implies studying and understanding the different scattering from the Ka and Ku band as function of the snow layer and the different resolution cells. The operational algorithms shall be defined to combine and harmonize the measurements at the Ka and Ku bands allowing as final aim to infer the snow characterization. Exploiting the on -board radiometer acquisitions to characterize/classify the sea ice overflown will be also a challenge to improve the retrieval of sea ice variables. For CIMR, significant effort will be spent to define consistent and physically based joint retrieval algorithms for the geophysical variables in each thematic domain (i.e. Polar regions and land) to leverage the full capabilities of the on - board instruments.

On longer term, effort shall be made to define level-1 and level-2 retrieval algorithms exploiting co-located acquisitions from different sensors, especially the ones relevant to the polar regions (i.e. CRISTAL, CIMR and ROSE-L). This should foster synergies among the different CEMs to fully exploit the capabilities of the Copernicus constellation and address the EU Arctic policy. As an example, CRISTAL altimetry measurements could be used in synergy with other high-resolution data such as ROSE-L imagery to jointly estimate the extent and elevation of icebergs and in turn to estimate the iceberg volume. Perspectives for Copernicus mentioned in the Copernicus Space Component Long Term Scenario include gravity field measurements, in particular quantum gravity prospects. On a shorter timescale, continuity and enhancement of current capabilities are implemented through the US/Europe MAGIC constellation concept. The NGGM mission is the ESA-led component and is designed to provide better performance in several systems elements such as satellite-to-satellite tracking, improved instrumentation, orbit control: these enhancements are necessary to match the increased sensitivity expected from quantum gravimetry. Preparing for a potential sustained European gravity field measurement capacity in Copernicus requires to keep a strong synergy between NGGM development on ESA side and other quantum -related R&D studies, Quantum Space Gr avity pathfinder mission developed through Horizon Europe, and other potential preparatory studies to strengthen user requirements, operational services.

FRM and Cal/Val activities A priority of ESA is the harmonization and standardization of calibration and validation (Cal/Val) procedures as well as the fostering of multi -mission intercomparison exercises. The Cal/Val activities ideally rely on Fiducial Reference Measurement (FRM) data as reference and represent a key element for the performance of EO mi ssions to give confidence to the end users in the quality of the data products . Many ESA initiatives have been conducted in this domain such as FRM4SOC, FRM4SST, FRM4ALT, FRM4SAR, FRM4GHG, … This effort is to be pursued for the Copernicus Expansion Missio ns as a preparatory activity for all new geophysical variables and associated uncertainty parameters. Those activities include the definition, development, and data provision of FRM in support to the validation of the Copernicus core products and in complement to existing in-situ networks. Preparatory activity to set- up FRM for the future Copernicus Expansion Missions (CIMR, CRISTAL, LSTM, ROSE-L, CHIME) will be also fundamental to guaranty the availability of a ‘trustable’ reference against which performance of the core products can be anchored or assessed against. Moreover, the plan is to foster the “supersite” concept, promoting the establishment of FRM in key areas covered by different Sentinel and Copernicus Contributing missions. The expanding SAR landscape driven both by Space Agencies as well as the NewSpace sector together with the increase of freely available SAR data to the science community highlight the need of a sound and standardized Cal/Val approach. This is not only to ensure the high quality of data processed by ESA, but also to promote the development of multi -sensor and multi -mission applications and to promote the usability of NewSpace data. Accordingly, a priority is on the harmonization and standardization of Cal/Val procedures as well as the fostering of multi-mission intercomparison exercises. The Cal/Val approach ideally relies on FRM data as reference. This is indeed in place for the Cal/Val of Level -1 Sentinel-1 data, which mainly rely on few high-quality artificial targets. Nevertheless, there is the growing need to increase the calibration networks as well as to foster the open sharing of standard information about calibration targets. This is crucial to ensure the quality of S1 and ROSE-L Cal/Val approaches and, consequently of the data. Moreover, t his is fundamental to achieve harmonized calibration between different missions,

promoting synergy. The use of FRM for Level-2 SAR Cal/Val is less consolidated w.r.t the Level-1 case. This is due to the lacking availability of global forest and ocean FRM collocated with the SAR measurements. Hence, a long-term priority is the refinement of the definition and the collection of forest and ocean FRM. A new activity endorsed by CEOS has started, aiming at developing a network -based Thermal Infra -red top-of-atmosphere calibration capacity for thermal sensors of ~100m or higher spatial resolution. Destination Earth (DestinE) is a flagship initiative of the European Commission (DG CNECT) to develop highly accurate DTE on a global scale. These models will monitor, simulate and predict the interaction between natural phenomena and human activities at a level that represents a real breakthrough in terms of accuracy, local detail, access -to-information speed and interactivity. This initiative is supporte d by dedicated data infrastructures and core user platform elements to address big data and interactivity challenges. ECMWF is creating the software and data environment needed to power the high-resolution digital twins, the digital twin engine; and will also provide the first two digital twins. The Digital Twin on Weather - Induced and Geophysical Extremes will provide capabilities for the assessment and prediction of environmental extremes in support of risk assessment and management. The Digital Twin on Climate Change Adaptation will support the analysis and testing of scenarios. This in turn will support sustainable development and climate adaptation and mitigation policy-making at multi- decadal timescales, at regional and national levels. Analogue efforts are developing for other digital twins, such as the Digital Twin Ocean supported by DG MARE and DG RTD or the ESA EO-base Digital Twin Earth. Those products and applications, once operational, could eventually make their way into the Copernicus services. The concept of a digital twin of the Earth cannot be dissociated from high performance/cloud computing, big data, physics-based models and data assimilation. Moreover, the development of interactive Digital

Twins represents a clear opportunity to transfor m the Copernicus portfolio, workflows, data production (user-pull/on-demand vs provider push) and user interfacing. Machine learning (and particularly deep learning) emulation of digital twins context offers the potential of reaching high dimensionality, complexity, nonlinearity and interactivity of real-life Earth systems and would expanding the learning ability from Big Data. Progress in physics-constrained learning, causal inference and reinforcement learning will increase the interpretability of Big Data and the ability of simulations to solve sequential decision. Future research must focus on each HPC/Cloud/Edged computing, big data, Earth system models, and deep learning methodologies and the optimal way to integrate them to achieve the full potential of interactive digital twins and transform Copernicus. Close collaboration with DG CNECT is paramount to achieve this. 5.4 Artificial Intelligence/Machine Learning Whilst Artificial Intelligence (AI)/Machine Learning (ML) is not a new topic, it is only over the last decade or so that the simultaneous advent of big data, HPC/cloud /edge computing and new AI algorithms (e.g., deep learning and generative methods) are revolutionizing almost every sector of society. Most, if not all, Copernicus implementing entities have developed specific strategies and/or activities to tap into those opportunities in support of their value chain (e.g. ECMWF AI Roadmap for the next 10 years, Copernicus Marine Service Evolution Strategy: R&D priorities; EUMETSAT AI and Machine Learning Roadmap: driving Innovation as a Community, ESA’s AI4EO challenges of the Φ-lab). The potential is huge for AI/ML to transform the way Copernicus operates, enhanc e the user experience and improve the Copernicus offe r, quality and timeliness of its products and services. This is an opportunity Copernicus cannot miss and requires a strong coordination with DG CNECT and its R&I planning. AI-related research is specifically needed on:  onboard processing to enhance detection of features of interest, speed up workflows and reduce data downlinks for focused applications;  fast, reliable, consistent, and as much as possible sensor agnostic identification of clouds and shadows in optical sensing;  AI-supported retrieval algorithms on both passive and active sensing for existing and upcoming Sentinel missions;  physics parameterization and parameter optimization to emulate poorly understood processes and increase model fidelity;  fault and outlier detection in production and delivery workflows to ensure more robust services;  support to automated pre-processing and QA/QC of observations and data to reduce the risk of man-made errors and product deficiencies;  data fusion techniques towards added-value products;  data compression and mining methods to navigate big data efficiently , as the amount of data is becoming a limiting factor;

 hybrid ensemble data assimilation techniques, error calibration and uncertainty quantification towards improved (re-)analyses and forecast skill;  analysis-driven Earth system deep learning models to boost prediction skill and timeliness , including with Digital Twin Earth models . These methods have shown great promises when applied to reanalyses for example;  exploring the potential of large pre-trained foundation models and transfer learning at scale for Earth system modeling;  experimenting observation(-only)-driven forecasting to support time -critical service elements, circumventing analysis steps . These approaches could be particularly suited for observation - dense areas from which processes can be inferred from observations alone;  downscaling and super resolution applications building on Copernicus data to refine products in space and time;  adaptive workflow optimization;  enhanced interactive interfaces enabling on-demand product and service generation;  chatbots that can guide the user across a wide range of information sources within and across Copernicus services for enhanced user support and experience. Machine learning applications will have to interact with all important Copernicus and other data. Deep learning (emulated) models can replace entire modelling workflows enabling also the full activation of the interactive digital twin capabilities at unpre cedented time and energy to solution, and the possibility to work with dynamic datapoints in space and time. Large pre-trained foundation machine learning models offer the potential for transfer learning at scale into wider Earth system applications. Given the QA/QC requirements on Copernicus products, explainable , trustworthy and open -source AI approaches are of particular interest, as AI mainly operates as a black box. A robust framework is required to ensure the same stringent quality, reliability, and verifiability requirements of AI-generated products, as well as transparency and clearly labelled information to users. This is particularly true for generative AI and natural language processing. It is recommended to pursue three main lines of R&I activiti es towards disruptive capabilities for Copernicus in the near future: 1) data curation and AI-ready data; 2) modelling, analytics, mining, fusion, and prediction; and 3) interface, visualization and user experience (cf generative AI). This will require a simultaneous coordination of R&D activities on the three broader key AI/ML ingredients (big data, computing and algorithms). Here again, a close collaboration with DG CNECT is paramount to achieve this ambition. 5.5 Synergies and partnerships at European and Global level Close cooperation between DG DEFIS and DG RTD is more important than ever to ensure Copernicus stays at the forefront , benefits from the latest science and innovation and feeds back user requirements to research. The previous chapter outlined the numerous R&D synergies already existing at each entity level to ensure proper implementation of their entrusted tasks. The expanding pan-Copernicus initiatives such as the Thematic Hubs adding value beyond single services highlight the need for R&I synergies and partnerships across corresponding Copernicus implementing entities.

Copernicus builds intrinsically also on Member States’ R&I initiatives and expertise which helps identifying gaps and answering operational needs. R&I common to a critical mass of Member States not covered by the current Copernicus offer could be addressed through a dedicated multinational partnership element in the programme for example, subject to programmatic decisions. One shall note that the EU also offers financial instruments, such as INTEREG, for such initiatives. At the Commission level, t he same need for synergies and partnerships holds across relevant European Commission Directorate Generals to inform on policy needs and subsequent research requirements. Synergies with DG CNECT and Destination Earth are particularly crucial to leverage the latest development in digital infrastructures, digital twins and AI/ML, which offer great potential to transform the Copernicus portfolio and user experience towards a Copernicus 3.0 digital revolution. On the international scene, there is a need to cooperate closely at working level with international bodies such as the Committee on Earth Observation Satellites (CEOS), the Coordination Group on Meteorological Satellites (CGMS), the European Association of Remote Sensing Companies (EARSC), the Group on Earth Observation (GEO), the Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC), etc. EuroGEO, the European regional GEO initiative delivers downstream services for end -users building on Copernicus services and other EO data. The tools and experiences of this community can also feed back into the evolution of Copernicus services. Future activities could foster this loop, and also strengthen the link with the private sector as provider s of value -added services or as an end-user. An activity that supports the role of public procurers of EO services is already planned for 2024 and may need follow up. The GEO flagships or GEO initiatives offer an opportunity for global outreach of EO based services made in Europe. For example , the European Commission is engaging in the GEO Blue Planet initiative by financing its European Office which is hosted at Mercator Ocean International ( MOI), the entity also implementing the Copernicus Marine Service. GEO Blue Planet delivers actionable information to coastal communities on issues like marine litter, Eutrophication, or Sargassum in Europe and the Caribbean and supports fisherman in Africa with target marine information. This example demonstrates the opportunities of a close relationships of Copernicus Services and GEO initiatives, which should be brought forward by R&I, where possible, in the coming years. The new Thematic Hubs in Copernicus might be one supportive element to achieve this.

This then allows to identify specific metrics that can be followed. These could include: 1. The number of research projects addressing emerging policy priorities, including the development of last mile policy applications building on Core Service outputs; 2. The number of research projects that address gaps identified by Entrusted Entities in their implementation of Core Services to address policy needs; 3. It is obvious that many of these aspects require a strong coordination between the research project consortia and the Entrusted Entities for the Copernicus Core Services. This should happen from the project inception, through implementation to review and evaluation. At the same time mechanisms implemented should account for differences across Entrusted Entities in how they can participate and engage with research consortia, as well as the scalability in resources to follow numerous research projects at the same time. Competition under those activities should be open, but Entrusted Entities should also be eligible and equally encouraged to apply and/or get involved in proposals as much as Horizon Europe permits. In particular, projects shall include dedicated efforts to develop transition pathways for subsequent uptake in the services.

Equally important is to foster t he explicit exploitation of Copernicus data, products and applications in R&D activities across funding instruments, e.g. via eligibility criteria when appropriate, in close cooperation with implementing EU executive agencies to further inform the programme evolution. Furthermore, a cross-cutting aspect of this strategy, which will need to be monitored, is the ambition to mainstream Europe’s Earth Observation research throughout the Framework Programme and beyond. This would ensure the complementarity of different calls to address identified needs and Service evolution priorities and should include all of the DGs and Executive Agencies having responsibility for the different research area s. A specific suggestion would be to organise dedicated discussions across these stakeholders at appropriate points in the co -creation process to ensure this complementarity, avoid overlaps and updates when appropriate. This could be organised in the context of the KCEO Working Group on Research and Innovation. Finally, the Implementation of the strategy with respect to the three dimensions identified above also has a cautionary aspect which need s to be considered. It is apparent that both the research effort s and the sustained EO Services have interface s to the EU policy priorities. Increasing ly research projects are required to make these linkages and this is, of course, a core part of the mandate of Copernicus Services. We need to ensure that these diverse interfaces to the policy communities (at EU and national level) do not create confusion or “request saturation”. Th is engagement with the user stakeholder communities should be a key element of the closer engagement of Entrusted Entities in research projects, as discussed above. This will ensure a seamless transition pathways of research outcomes into operations and where relevant European research infrastructure could also offer dual use. Funding should avoid dispersion of resources, e.g. by supporting single successful proposals per topics.

The present Earth Observation Strategic Research and Innovation Agenda (SRIA) serves as a comprehensive framework designed to guide the evolution of Copernicus . It was developed to meet growing user expectations, leverage technological innovations, and align with evolving EU and global policies. Research and innovation are inherently in continuous evolution and progress, and the field of Earth observation is no exception to this. The current Phase II of Copernicus, the Earth observation component of the EU Space Programme, is well underway and the enhanced continuity of the services already delivers new and improved data, products, and services to their users, leveraging the latest science and technology developments. The SRIA updates the 2020 roadmap for the curre nt Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF) period, incorporating feedback from growing user requirements, new technologies, evolving EU and global policies, and changes in the global geopolitical context. The document will serve as a reference for calls issued for the period of 2025 -2027 and sets broad directions for the next MFF, with plans for further updates before 2028. The agenda emphasizes the need for agile and resilient Earth observations, service enhancements through digital transformation, the devel opment of federated data infrastructures, and the promotion of the downstream market uptake and mass adoption of Copernicus products. The main initial tenets of the Copernicus evolution remain largely valid. Yet the SRIA also highlights the need for an agile approach to R&I for Earth observation and Copernicus. The SRIA aims to inform long -term research and innovation, justify investments, and guide the development of research calls over the years. It was developed in coordination with various EU entities and builds on extensive consultations with stakeholders, including Entrusted Entities, Executive Agencies, Member States, to mainstream research and innovation across Horizon Europe. The document highlights both specific and broader research priorities for the Copernicus services, including climate change, atmosphere monitoring, emergency management, land monitoring, marine environment monitoring, and security services. The report also discusses the importance of upstream space and ground segments, and downstream elements such as market uptake , and horizontal elements like thematic hubs, computing and data infrastructures, digital twins, AI/machine learning, and synergies and partnerships at European and global levels. The need for close cooperation betw een various EU entities and international bodies is underscored to ensure a cohesive and effective approach to Earth observation R&I. The report proposes measures of success and makes recommendations for R&I implementation in the future. The SRIA is a dyn amic tool and shall be considered as a living document. A further update is warranted before the start of the next MFF, to ensure that a new full R&I roadmap can sustain the Copernicus evolution and ensure all services remain at the forefront to serve our core user s and stimulate wide market uptake.
