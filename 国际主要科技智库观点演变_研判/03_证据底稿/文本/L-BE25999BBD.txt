High Bandwidth Memory
What It Is and Why It Matters
H
igh Bandwidth Memory (HBM) is a type of computer memory used in leading artificial
intelligence (AI) chips and other hardware for high-performance computing. HBM can
store and move large volumes of data quickly, making it suitable for increasingly data-
intensive AI workloads.
1 As of 2026, most leading AI chips use HBM, and almost all
leading AI models are trained and deployed on AI chips with HBM. 2 In the following section, we
explain what sets HBM apart from other memory technologies and why HBM is important for AI’s
computational needs.
KEY TAKEAWAYS
■ Leading artificial intelligence (AI) workloads place extreme demands on memory, and
Hi
gh Bandwidth Memory (HBM) has emerged as the central technology to meet them.
Large AI models require chips with both high memory capacity —how much data can be
stored—and high memory bandwidth —how fast data can be transferred to a processor. HBM
is the only memory technology that meets both requirements: It offers higher bandwidth than
other types of dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) while providing enough storage
capacity to hold model weights and contextual data close to the processor, which is some-
thing that smaller, faster memory, such as static random-access memory, cannot do on its
own. This combination is why most of the top AI chips use HBM.
■ HBM p
roduction is a complex, capital-intensive process that demands some of the
world’s most advanced manufacturing equipment. First, companies fabricate individual
DRAM memory dies —unpackaged silicon chips—at large production facilities. Each die
requires etching nanometer-scale features using lithography tools that can cost more than
$300 million. The DRAM dies are then stacked vertically, which requires extreme preci-
sion because a single defect can compromise the entire HBM stack. HBM’s complexity
and resource-intensiveness make it expensive to produce: It can account for 45 percent of
the total production cost of an AI chip, roughly three times the cost of fabricating the chip’s
processor.
■ Onl
y three companies—SK Hynix (South Korea), Samsung (South Korea), and Micron
(United States)—produce globally competitive HBM. Their production is concentrated in
South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan. Chinese company CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies)
is attempting to increase its domestic HBM production but lags in overall output and does
not manufacture the most-advanced HBM generations. In December 2024, China was barred
from importing HBM from foreign producers, so Chinese access to advanced HBM depends
on whether CXMT can improve.

High Bandwidth Memory in the
Broader Memory Landscape
The ability to store and access data is central to com-
puting, but different workloads have varying require-
ments for how much memory is needed and how
quickly they need to access that memory. Computer
systems thus use a hierarchy of memory technolo-
gies that fall into two broad categories. The first is
volatile memory: It requires constant power to retain
data and serves as a system’s active working memory,
holding the data and instructions that running pro-
cesses need.
3 The second is non-volatile memory,
which retains data without power and is used for
long-term storage.
4 In each category, there are mul-
tiple subtypes of memories that offer different trade-
offs in access speed, capacity, and cost. In practice, a
single device, whether a laptop or a server, relies on
several of these technologies at once, each handling a
different role. At one end of the spectrum are fast and
expensive volatile memories with low capacity, such
as static random-access memory (SRAM).
5 SRAM
is built into processors, such as central processing
units (CPUs) or graphics processing units (GPUs),
and provides data near-instantaneously, but it has
low capacity. At the other end are non-volatile storage
types that are slow and cheap and offer high capacity.
These include hard disk drives (HDDs) or solid-state
drives (SSDs): They can hold large amounts of data
affordably, but accessing that data takes a long time.
Dynamic random-access memory (DRAM)
occupies a middle ground in capacity, bandwidth,
and access speed (Figure 1). It is a volatile memory
that has higher bandwidth and higher access speed
than SSDs or HDDs and offers much greater capacity
at a lower cost than SRAM.
6 It comes in several vari-
ants optimized for different applications. Double data
rate (DDR) DRAM is the standard, general-purpose
variant, used in servers and personal computers;
LPDDR (low-power DDR) is designed for energy
efficiency in phones and laptops; and GDDR (graph-
ics DDR) is optimized for graphics processing and
offers higher bandwidth than standard DDR. HBM
is the most specialized form of DRAM, designed to
meet the extreme memory demands of leading AI
workloads. Unlike conventional DRAM modules that
place dies side by side on a circuit board, HBM stacks
multiple DRAM dies vertically and places them next
to the processor, providing greater memory capacity
and higher data transfer rates in a smaller footprint.

Why Do Artificial Intelligence
Workloads Need High Bandwidth
Memory?
Leading AI workloads rely on specialized AI chips
designed by such companies as Google, Nvidia,
and Advanced Micro Devices (AMD).
7 These chips
deliver high processing speeds, but two things are
required from memory to make effective use of that
computational power: first, the ability to keep large
amounts of data close to the processor, and second,
high bandwidth—the rate at which data can move
between memory and the processor.
Both AI training and inference require sub-
stantial memory capacity close to the processor. AI
chips must hold a model’s parameters (the numerical
weights that determine the output of the model) in
working memory so that the processor can access
them quickly. Large AI models contain hundreds
of billions of parameters, requiring hundreds of
gigabytes (GBs) of memory just to hold the weights
alone.
8 For example, a 500-billion-parameter model
in FP16 precision would need approximately 1 tera-
byte (TB) of memory, so much that the model needs
to be distributed across at least six Nvidia B200 AI
chips, each carrying 192 GB of HBM3E.
9 Alterna-
tively, the same model would need at least hundreds
of SRAM-based chips to run because of their lower
capacity per chip. Relying on long-term storage
devices, such as HDDs or SSDs, would be impracti-
cal because they are orders of magnitude slower and
cannot deliver data quickly enough to keep work-
loads running.
Model weights are not the only data that must
reside in working memory. Modern AI models based
on the transformer architecture also maintain what is
known as a key-value (KV) cache. The KV cache func-
tions like a notepad for everything the model has read
so far in a given request so that the model does not
have to reprocess the entire input each time it gener-
ates the next word. As the amount of input data a
model can process (also known as the context window)
has grown up to a million tokens, the KV cache has
grown accordingly, consuming an increasing share
of available memory alongside the model weights.
10
Importantly, each concurrent user requires their own
separate KV cache, leading to memory demands that
scale along with the size of the user base.
11
Keeping all this data in memory is necessary
but not sufficient: The processor also needs to read
it fast enough to avoid sitting idle. During training
and inference, massive volumes of data flow between
memory and the AI chip’s processor. If the data is not
delivered fast enough, the processor stalls and the
computing power goes unused.
12 In practice, much of
the time spent generating new tokens during inference
is not limited by how fast the processor can compute
but by how fast it can read from memory. For those
reasons, it is a memory-bound workload. This is why
memory bandwidth is so critical for AI performance.
HBM provides a balance of specifications that
meet AI’s memory needs. It delivers far higher band-
width than other types of DRAM, such as DDR
or LPDDR, while offering enough dense capacity
close to the processor to hold model weights and
KV cache—something that on-chip SRAM, despite
being faster, cannot provide on its own because of its
limited capacity. No other single memory technol-
ogy matches this combination. That said, as model
sizes and context windows continue to grow, AI chip
designers are actively adopting and exploring trade-
offs across the memory hierarchy. These include
allocating more die area to SRAM, pairing HBM
with high-capacity DDR for less frequently accessed
data, using SRAM-based chips alongside HBM-based
designs for parts of inference, or developing new

High Bandwidth Memory Design
HBM’s combination of high memory bandwidth and
capacity makes it stand out from other memory alter-
natives, and both metrics have improved substan-
tially across generations. The first generation of HBM
debuted in 2013 and offered 128 GB per second of
bandwidth and 4 GB of capacity per stack.
13 HBM4,
whose standard was finalized in 2025, delivers up to
2 TB per second of bandwidth and 64 GB of capacity
per stack, increases of around 16 times for each.
14
A typical AI chip contains multiple stacks of
HBM (Figure 2).15 For example, Nvidia’s B300,
released in 2025, uses eight stacks of HBM3E. Each of
the HBM stacks uses 12 DRAM dies and a base die,
enabling the chip to reach 288 GB of total capacity
and 8 TB per second of bandwidth.
16
Two design features enable HBM to reach these
performance specifications: vertical stacking and
wide interfaces that can transmit high volumes of
data in parallel. We explain both in detail in the fol-
lowing sections.
Vertical Stacking
A core feature of HBM is its vertical architecture:
Multiple DRAM dies are vertically stacked (Figure 2)
and thus substantially increase the amount of data
HBM can store in a compact package. Microscopic
vertical channels (TSVs) route data to and from
individual dies in the stack at high throughput.
17 At
the bottom of the stack is a base logic die (base die),
which orchestrates the rapid transfer of data between
memory and the processor of the AI chip.

Interfaces are physical communication pathways
that allow components to exchange data. HBM has
wide interfaces of 1024 bits (and 2048 bits in new

Because the channels are wide, they do not need
extreme speeds to reach high bandwidths. This is
a wide and slow design paradigm, which contrasts

slow approach offers another important advantage:
Wide channels with lower speeds consume less power
than narrower memory designs.

While HBM is well suited for AI, it has signifi-
cant trade-offs, particularly its high production costs
(an estimated $2,900 for 192 GB of HBM3E, which is
45 percent of the cost to produce Nvidia’s B200 and
three times the cost of producing its processor) and
manufacturing complexity.
21 We discuss this topic in
the following section. 
How Is High Bandwidth Memory
Produced, and What Makes It
So Challenging?
In the previous section, we explained HBM’s place in
the broader memory landscape, why it is useful for
leading AI workloads, and its main design features.
In this section, we provide an overview of HBM’s
manufacturing processes, which can be divided into
three technically challenging domains, each consist-
ing of many substeps: DRAM wafer fabrication, base
die fabrication, and die stacking.
DRAM Wafer Fabrication
The HBM manufacturing process begins at DRAM
fabrication facilities, known as fabs for short
(Figure 3, Step 1). Here, manufacturers transform raw
silicon wafers into functional memory dies through
a series of steps that deposit, pattern, and remove
materials on a nanometer scale. The fabrication pro-
cess requires large clean rooms that span the size of
multiple football fields and are outfitted with billions
of dollars in specialized tools, including lithography
machines—produced exclusively by the Dutch firm
ASML—that can cost more than $300 million.
22 As
of 2026, front-end DRAM production for HBM is
primarily in South Korea, Taiwan, and Japan, and
there is more-limited capacity for lagging generations
in China.
23 The United States does not have facilities
that produce leading-edge DRAM for HBM at scale. 
A key metric that determines a manufacturer’s
DRAM sophistication is called the manufacturing
node.
24 In semiconductor manufacturing, this metric
measures the smallest feature size in nanometers
(nm) that can be printed on a silicon wafer.
25 Smaller
features are an important improvement because
they increase memory cell density, allowing more
bits (and thus more data) to be stored per unit of
silicon area.
26 Importantly, these features also enable
reductions in heat generation and energy consump-
tion. Since producers started making DRAM with
feature sizes below 20 nm in 2016, they have named
nodes using the number “1” combined with a lettered
progression (x, y, z, α, β, γ).
27 The big three memory
manufacturers—Samsung, Micron, and SK Hynix—
started volume production of 1z process nodes with
feature sizes of around 16 nm between 2019 and
2020 (Figure 4).
28 As of 2026, they have advanced
to feature sizes approaching 10 nm. Meanwhile, the
most-advanced DRAM that Chinese memory manu-
facturer CXMT (ChangXin Memory Technologies)
has produced at commercial scale in 2026 has a fea-
ture size of 16 nm, putting its technology around four
years behind the industry’s state of the art.
29

generations because improvements in DRAM density

Base Die Fabrication
HBM requires a base die to manage data transfers
between the stacked DRAM dies and the AI chip’s
processor (Figure 3, Step 2). For HBM generations
up to HBM3E, base dies for HBM were exclusively
produced in house on DRAM process nodes. How-
ever, memory manufacturers are starting to migrate
their base die fabrication to dedicated logic found-
ries, either at Samsung or Taiwan Semiconductor
Manufacturing Company (TSMC).
40 Moving base
die fabrication to logic foundries will contribute to
bandwidth and power efficiency gains, making the
base die increasingly important for overall HBM per-
formance.
41 As of HBM4, base dies are increasingly
customized to meet the design and performance
objectives of a customer’s processor.
42 They can be
tuned to different priorities depending on the use
case; for example, a power-constrained edge deploy-
ment might sacrifice bandwidth for a smaller, more
efficient footprint, while a large-scale training cluster
would optimize for maximum bandwidth and capac-
ity regardless of power or size.
43
Base die fabrication is difficult because it com-
bines the complexity and tool requirements of
advanced logic with unique memory constraints.
The die must accommodate high-speed interfaces
and signaling to manage communication between
the DRAM dies and the processor.
44 The base die
also coordinates error correction functions across
the stack, so defects can compromise the reliability
of the entire module.

Die Stacking
The final step in HBM manufacturing is bonding
together the DRAM dies (Figure 3, Step 1) and base
die (Figure 3, Step 2) into a vertical stack (Figure 3,
Step 3). This process uses bonding technologies—
typically thermocompression bonding or mass-
reflow molded underfill (MR-MUF)—to connect dies
using micro-bumps, tiny metal connections that are
critical for the overall electrical and thermal perfor-
mance of the HBM stack.
47 As stack heights progress
to 16 dies and beyond, manufacturers expect to
eventually adopt a technology called hybrid bond-
ing, which stacks dies directly without the need for
micro-bumps. This approach improves performance
and power efficiency but introduces additional chal-
lenges, including high costs and difficulties related to
yield, alignment, and warpage.
Notably, HBM’s vertically stacked design creates
a distinct yield problem: When the dies are stacked
together, the defect rate compounds and an error in
a single die can cause problems for the entire stack.
48
Here, the base die plays an important role because it
helps route around defective parts of the DRAM dies.
HBM’s front-end and back-end complexity means
producers commonly face production yields 20- to
30-percent lower than those for DDR5 memory.
49
For newer producers, such as CXMT, which are sub-
ject to manufacturing equipment restrictions from
U.S. export controls and without the same years of
accumulated learning, yields are expected to be even
lower.

The Global High Bandwidth
Memory Market
In the previous section, we explained the main steps
involved in producing HBM and what makes this
process so technically challenging. In this section, we
provide an overview of the companies that produce
HBM as of 2026, explain where HBM is produced,
and explore the factors shaping the HBM market.
A Small Number of Companies Are
Competing for Market Share
Three companies produce advanced HBM at scale as
of 2026: SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron. Chinese
memory giant CXMT has begun to produce lagging
generations of HBM, but as of 2026, its output is
small and it has yet to capture any measurable por-
tion of global market share (Figure 5).
51 
SK Hynix leads globally by HBM market share,
followed by Samsung and Micron.52 The company
secured its lead because of its position as priority
supplier of Nvidia GPUs, which accounted for more
than 60 percent of total HBM consumption in both
2024 and 2025.
53 SK Hynix’s development and use
of MR-MUF bonding, which improves heat dissipa-
tion and overall yield, has been a key differentiator
in achieving its dominant market position. The com-
pany was also the first to develop and mass-produce
HBM in a 2014 partnership with AMD.
54 More than
a decade of accumulated expertise has enabled SK
Hynix to release new generations of HBM before its
competitors, a compounding advantage that other
companies have found difficult to overcome.
55
The world’s largest memory manufacturer by
DRAM capacity is Samsung, which is vying to win
back HBM market share from SK Hynix.
56 Samsung
was the primary supplier of HBM into China (a
significant source of HBM demand) until Decem-
ber 2024, when the United States extended export
controls to cover finished HBM units.
57 Samsung’s
chip unit has had a mixed record in winning cus-
tomers since. In 2024 and 2025, it took the company
18 months after development was completed for Sam-
sung’s 12-layer HBM3E to meet Nvidia’s qualification
standards, a rigorous customer validation and testing
process that suppliers must pass before their HBM
products can be used in a customer’s systems.
58 How-
ever, despite a period of reported challenges, Samsung

Conclusion
HBM is a central part of modern AI infrastructure.
As AI models have scaled in size and complexity,
HBM has been the only memory technology that is
capable of delivering the combination of bandwidth
and capacity that keeps pace with leading AI work-
loads. Other memory types might offer lower costs
or greater capacity, but none match HBM’s combined
performance profile in a compact package close to
the processor. Although AI systems and chip designs
are actively evolving and incorporating alternative
memory types, HBM is likely to retain a central role as
AI models and inference demands continue to scale.
HBM’s performance comes at a price, both tech-
nologically and economically. HBM can account for
roughly 45 percent of the total cost of an AI chip,
driven by a resource-intensive and complex manu-
facturing process that demands some of the most
advanced semiconductor equipment, years of accu-
mulated process expertise, and billions of dollars in
capital. These barriers have kept the market narrow:
As of 2026, only SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron
produce advanced HBM at globally competitive scale,
and virtually all of it is manufactured in East Asia.
CXMT, despite receiving significant financial and
policy support, produces only low volumes of HBM
that lag several generations behind.
For policy researchers, two areas are worth
underscoring. First, given that U.S. export controls
bar advanced HBM from export to China as of April
2026, how long it will take CXMT to narrow the gap
with competitors will determine HBM access for
China’s AI chip makers. CXMT’s ability to advance
its HBM designs and production volumes will shape
whether China’s AI chipmakers remain bottlenecked
or can secure a viable domestic source over time.
Second, an open analytical question is whether U.S.
industrial policy should aim to incentivize additional
capacity to build a more integrated, end-to-end
domestic HBM supply chain or whether the risk of
relying on South Korean supply is sufficiently low
specialized equipment to produce HBM.
74 In absolute
terms, total HBM wafer production capacity more
than tripled between the end of 2023 and the end of
2025.
75 However, these expansions have been tightly
tied to major customer roadmaps rather than more-
speculative demand signals, creating the tight supply
conditions observed in early 2026.
76
Two factors help explain why new supply has
been relatively slow to come online despite fast-
accelerating demand. First, meeting AI-driven
demand for HBM requires more physical production
capacity—more clean room space and more manu-
facturing tools. Manufacturers can add capacity by
reallocating clean room space and tools used for
other products, such as conventional DRAM and
NAND flash storage, but beyond that, they must
build entirely new facilities, which takes years and
billions of dollars. Memory manufacturers are cau-
tious about building this capacity, given the indus-
try’s long history of boom-and-bust cycles in which
capacity built during strong demand periods comes
online just as prices collapse.
77 Second, strict cus-
tomer qualification requirements mean that added
wafer capacity does not immediately translate into
usable supply. Each new HBM generation must be
validated for use in customer systems, sometimes
creating monthslong delays between capacity addi-
tions and usable, commercial products.

Glossary
Artificial intelligence (AI) chip. An integrated cir-
cuit optimized for performing highly parallelized
workloads, such as artificial intelligence training and
inference. Also referred to as an artificial intelligence
accelerator. Includes graphics processing units, tensor
processing units, and other specialized processors.
ASML. A Dutch multinational corporation, it is the
world’s leading supplier of photolithography equip-
ment, which is used in semiconductor manufactur-
ing. ASML is the sole producer of extreme ultraviolet
lithography machines and the dominant producer of
deep ultraviolet lithography machines.
Cache. A small, fast memory layer located close to or
directly on a processor that stores frequently accessed
data to reduce the time and energy cost of retriev-
ing them from slower memory. Caches are typically
implemented using static random-access memory
and are organized in hierarchical levels (e.g., L1, L2,
L3). Lower levels are faster and smaller.
Central processing unit (CPU). A general-purpose
processor that executes a wide variety of comput-
ing tasks and serves as the primary control unit in
computer systems. CPUs are less specialized than
artificial intelligence (AI) accelerators for AI work-
loads, but they are present in AI servers for handling
non-AI tasks.
Changxin Memory Technologies (CXMT). A
state-backed memory semiconductor manufacturer
in China. CXMT is, as of 2026, the most advanced
memory manufacturer in China.
Context window. The amount of information that an
artificial intelligence model can recall when generat-
ing a response, measured in tokens.
Deep ultraviolet (DUV). A lithography technol-
ogy that uses short wavelengths of light—typically
193 nanometers or 248 nanometers—to pattern semi-

Parameters. The numerical weights that define an
artificial intelligence model’s behavior, which are
progressively refined during training.
Process node. A measure of miniaturization in
semiconductor manufacturing, typically expressed
in nanometers. Smaller nodes generally enable more
transistors per chip and improved performance
and energy efficiency, although cross-vendor com-
parisons can be misleading because node names
do not always correspond directly with a physical
dimension.
Processor. A semiconductor chip that executes com-
putational instructions. Processors include central
processing units, graphics processing units, and
custom artificial intelligence (AI) accelerators. In AI
systems, processors are typically paired with High
Bandwidth Memory to meet the high memory band-
width and capacity demands of training and infer-
ence workloads.
Registers. The smallest and fastest memory in a
computer system, located directly within the proces-
sor. Registers hold the data and instructions that the
processor is actively computing at any given moment.
Solid-state drive (SSD). A non-volatile storage device
that uses flash memory to read and write data. SSDs
are faster and more durable than hard disk drives
but slower and less expensive than dynamic random-
access memory.
Static random-access memory (SRAM). The fastest
type of memory, typically located directly on a pro-
cessor chip. SRAM delivers data near-instantaneously
but is expensive and low-density, making it unsuit-
able for large-scale data storage.
Through-silicon via (TSV). A microscopic vertical
electrical connection drilled through a silicon die.
TSVs are used in High Bandwidth Memory stacks
and enable extremely wide data pathways that give
High Bandwidth Memory its high bandwidth.
Token. The smallest building blocks a model pro-
cesses, such as a part of a word, image, or video.
Training. The process by which an artificial intelli-
gence model learns by adjusting its parameters based
on exposure to large amounts of data. Training is
computationally intensive and places heavy simul-
taneous demands on both memory bandwidth and
capacity.
the standard memory technology used in advanced
artificial intelligence chips.
Inference. The process of generating outputs from
a trained artificial intelligence model. For example,
when a user sends a message to a large language
model and receives a response, that is inference.
Interposer. A thin silicon layer that provides high-
density electrical connections needed to transfer data
at high speed. The interposer is a key component of
the advanced packaging that makes High Bandwidth
Memory’s performance possible.
Key-value cache (KV cache). A memory structure
used during artificial intelligence inference that
stores intermediate values from previously processed
tokens, allowing the model to generate new tokens
without recomputing prior context from scratch. The
KV cache grows with context length and batch size
and can reach terabytes in large-scale deployments.
Lithography. A semiconductor manufacturing pro-
cess that uses light to transfer circuit patterns onto a
silicon wafer. Lithography is one of the most critical
steps in chip fabrication, determining the minimum
feature size achievable on a given process node.
Logic process. A semiconductor fabrication process
optimized for manufacturing digital logic circuits,
including graphics processing units, central process-
ing units, and networking processors.
Low-power double data rate (LPDDR). A variant of
double data rate designed for energy efficiency, com-
monly used in mobile devices and laptops. LPDDR
offers lower bandwidth than High Bandwidth
Memory but consumes significantly less power.
Memory bandwidth. The rate at which data can be
read from or written to memory, typically measured
in gigabytes per second or terabytes per second.
Memory bandwidth is a critical constraint in artifi-
cial intelligence systems.
Memory capacity. The total amount of data that
memory can store at one time, typically measured in
gigabytes. Distinct from memory bandwidth.
NAND. A high-density, non-volatile memory
technology that retains data without power, com-
monly used in solid-state drives, USB drives, and
smartphones.
Non-volatile memory. A type of memory that retains
data without power.

15
Volatile memory. Memory that requires continu-
ous power to retain data. Dynamic random-access
memory, including High Bandwidth Memory, is
volatile. When power is cut, all data stored in volatile
memory is lost.
Wafer. A thin circular disc of silicon on which semi-
conductor chips are fabricated. Multiple dies are
produced simultaneously on a single wafer before
being cut apart.
Wafer starts per month (WSPM). The number of
silicon wafers that begin the fabrication process at a
given facility each month. WSPM is commonly used
to estimate a fab’s total production capacity.
Working memory. The volatile memory that holds
data and instructions for use by the processor. Work-
ing memory keeps data close to the processor for fast
access during computation, as opposed to long-term
storage, which retains data persistently but is much
slower to access.
Yield. The proportion of functional dies successfully
produced from a wafer. Higher yields indicate more-
efficient manufacturing with fewer defects. Low
yields increase per-chip costs and constrain supply.

About This Paper
Artificial intelligence (AI) systems depend on several enabling hardware tech-
nologies, and High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) is among the most critical. HBM
is a specialized type of memory used in most leading AI chips, but it is produced
at globally competitive scale by only three companies, and it relies on complex
manufacturing processes concentrated in a handful of facilities in East Asia. In
this paper, we synthesize technical and market analysis of the HBM ecosystem:
how HBM works, how it is produced, and who controls its production. We aim
to equip policymakers and researchers with a primer that can be referenced in a
variety of policy discussions.
