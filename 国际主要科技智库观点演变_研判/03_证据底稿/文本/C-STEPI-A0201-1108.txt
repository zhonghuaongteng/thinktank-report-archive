Executive Summary

From 2021 to 2023, STEPI and MESTI jointly implemented the K-Innovation Partnership
Program under the following title: “Establishment and Operation of Ghana Innovation &
Research Commercialization (GIRC) Centre”. The third year of the programme (2023) was
a i m e d a t e x p l o r i n g t h e c o o p e r a t i v e p a r t n e r s h i p b e t w e e n K o r e a a nd G h a n a i n o r d e r t o
establish and operate the GIRC Centre.
To achieve the aforementioned objective, the project has been carried out in two parts: i)
development of R&I capacity and ii) exploration of a cooperativ e partnership. First, the
Capacity Development Workshop was h e l d t o s t r e n g t h e n t h e G I R C Centre’s operational
capacities, which was then followed by the Field Study where participants had opportunities
to visit STI-related agencies in Korea. In addition, the Cooperative Partnership Workshop
was held to explore future partnerships between Korea and Ghana. As part of this workshop,
the participants also attended the Field Meeting to discuss con crete measures for
promoting cooperation between Kor e a n S T I a g e n c i e s a n d t h e i r G h anaian counterparts.
During the project, five ‘Project Concept Notes’ were proposed for future cooperation
between five potential Korean STI agencies and their Ghanaian counterparts, and concrete
measures for enhancing cooperation between the Korean and Ghanaian partner agencies
were discussed. Five project agendas proposed in the process of the project are expected
to serve as a foundation for oper ating the GIRC Centre and buil ding up the national
innovation system in Ghana.
This project, which falls within the last year of the three-yea r programme (2021-2023),
concludes this three-year-long effort. For the successful establishment and operation of the
GIRC Centre, the project suggests the following initiatives. First, the governance structure of
the GIRC Centre (‘GIA’ in the fu ture) and the legal systems req uired to support its
governance need to be mapped out and established as quickly as possible. For this purpose,

website; however, full-time employees, including the director of the centre responsible for
its daily operation, have yet to be secured. Therefore, full-time employees including the
director need to be immediately recruited, and their skills and capacities for building and
running the centre’s operational programmes need to be developed and strengthened as
soon as the centre’s governance system and funding mechanism are finalised.

1. Project Background
As of 2023, Ghana is placed in the rank of the lower-middle-income countries (LMICs) with
a per capita GDP of USD 2,002. From the inauguration of the new government in 2017 up
until 2023, Ghana’s economy maintained a CAGR of 3.9% (IMF, 2023)1. The Nana Akufo-
A d d o g o v e r n m e n t a n n o u n c e d “ G h a n a , B e y o n d A i d ” a s i t s k e y n a t i o nal agenda, seeking
economic growth without resorting to foreign aid while pursuing a vision of transforming
the country from an LMIC into a UMIC (upper-middle-income country). As an STI framework
to support its vision, the Ghanaian government has come up with seven components and is
now pursuing STI-based sustainable development under a mid-to-long-term plan called the
“Decade of Innovation”.
However, Ghana’s capacity in certain key components of its national science, technology,
and innovation (STI) system has long been extremely limited, for example, in such areas as
university-industry-research institute cooperation, R&D, and te chnology transfer and
commercialisation. To overcome such weaknesses, the Ministry of Environment, Science,
Technology & Innovation (hereinafter referred to as ‘MESTI’) officially announced a plan to
establish the Ghan Innovation & Research Commercialisation Centre (hereinafter referred
to as ‘GIRC Centre’) in July 2018. In 2020, the Ghanaian government asked for help from the
K-Innovation Partnership Program hosted by the Science and Technology Policy Institute
(hereinafter referred to as ‘STEPI’) out of its aspiration to learn from Korea’s development

2. Project Objectives
The project was designed as a three-year programme with specific project objectives for
each year. In the first year (2021 ), the programme focused on a nalysing Ghana’s
Technological Innovati on Support Programmes (TISPs) and sharing South Korea’s
experience. In the second year (2022), the programme focused on developing a master plan
for establishing and operating the GIRC Centre based on the analyses of the first year. Finally,
in the third year (2023), the pro ject team aimed to develop the capacities required for
successful operation of the GIRC Centre and to explore cooperative partnerships between
Korea and Ghana.
To be more specific, the 1st year of the project was focused on analysing Ghana’s TISPs at
t h e n a t i o n a l l e v e l a n d s h a r i n g K o r e a ’ s e x p e r i e n c e s , a s s h o w n i n the Figure below. TISPs
extensively cover the following nine themes: i) tax & finance, ii) manpower, iii) technology
development and information, iv) technology transfer and commercialisation, v) start-up
and incubation, vi) intellectual property management, vii) purchase and public procurement,
viii) accreditation and certification, and ix) U-I-R (universit y-industry-research institute)
linkage. The project team shared Korea’s experiences in each of these areas and raised the
respective implications for Ghana.
In 2022, the second year of the p roject, the project team develo p e d a m a s t e r p l a n f o r
successfully establishing and operating the GIRC Centre at the institutional level, comprising
three key components: strategic directions, internal systems, and operational programmes.
First, as regards the strategic directions to be pursued by the GIRC Centre, the master plan
p r e s e n t s t h e c e n t r e ’ s v i s i o n , m i s s i o n , r o l e s , a n d f u n c t i o n s . A s f o r t h e c e n t r e ’ s i n t e r n a l
systems, the master plan reviews the direction for designing its governance structure and
explores a series of measures fo r securing its budget, manpower , and networking.
Regarding the centre’s operational programmes, the master plan suggests programmes for
enabling the centre to play the roles of an STI agency, an R&I secretariat, and a hub that
facilitates the innovation ecosystem.

1.1 Introduction
K o r e a i s a s m a l l p e n i n s u l a c o u n t r y i n t h e F a r E a s t , h i n g i n g u p on the Chinese continent,
sharing a narrow border with Russia in the north, and surrounde d by the Japanese
a r c h i p e l a g o i n t h e s o u t h . B e i n g i n t h e n e i g h b o r h o o d o f t h r e e s uperpowers and
geographically remote from the Western world, Korea’s internati onal interactions --
political, economic, cultural and others – at one time seldom reached beyond the region. In
a sense, therefore, Korea’s history up until the end of World War II was more a reflection of
the political, economic and military tensions and cooperative relations among the regional
powers, rather than its own efforts to secure political stability and promote its economic
development. Korea could only enjoy stability when the regional p o w e r s w e r e o n g o o d

from external influences, including those of the Industrial Revolution among others. It took

The above brief description is sufficient to explain how miserable the economic situation
was back in the 1960s when Korea launched its drive for economic development. At that
time it was one of the poorest economies in the world, with a per capita GDP of $82(1960),
relying on the primary sectors for more than 60% of its employment. A such, it suffered all
the problems commonly found in the poor countries in those days. However, Korea differed
from those poor countries in that it had a large pool of well-educated and highly motivated
but underutilised workers with an average level of education as high as that expected of a
country more than twice as rich as Korea then was.
It was in this context that Korea launched its drive for industrialisation in 1961. Since then,
this drive for industrialisation has brought about an astonishing transformation of Korea
from a poor agrarian society int o a dynamic industrial economy within less than half a
century. Today it is world’s tenth largest economy with a per capita GDP of over US$32,000
(Nominal value, 2021, UN Statisti cs Division, National Account) 3 . Underlying this
phenomenal growth are South Korea’s unparalleled efforts to promote science, technology
and innovation, which can be explained in part by the resources Korea allocates for R&D –
it is currently the fifth largest spender on R&D in the world a nd tops among the OECD
countries in terms of R&D intensity (i.e. gross expenditures on R&D as a share of GDP, Korea:
over 4.5%).4 The rapid growth of R&D investment has translated into the rapid growth of
GDP and a dramatic transformation of the country’s economic structure from a low-mid-
technology-based one into a high-tech-based one. Indeed, no country matches Korea in its
efforts to harness the potential of science and technology for economic development, and
in this sense, one may refer to Korean’ growth as “S&T-based” growth.

1.2 Why did South Korea adopt an S&T-based growth strategy?
 National development strategy refers to a set of schemes, acco rding to which the
government and industries are to leverage the nation’s endowments – both tangible and
intangible - for social and economic development, under the giv en social, political and
economic conditions. Different countries choose different strategies for development for
the following reasons:
1) Because they are endowed with different resource that they can exploit for economic
development;
2) Because they have different attitudes towards development and well-being, in other
words, differences in motivation and desire for change and development; and/or
3) Because they differ in terms of their capacity to initiate, carry out and deal with changes
in an effective and efficient manner.
South Korea’s choice of S&T-based development strategy can be explained by the above
logic. As mentioned in the previous section, Korea was one of the poorest economies in the
world as late as the early 1960s, and thus the key challenge for Korea as a nation was how
to develop the economy and liberate its people from chronic poverty. But in tackling that

challenge, Korea was bound by diverse constraints: First of all, Korea was extremely poor in
natural resources and had a weak agricultural base, which exclu ded a resource-based
strategy as an option. The only possible option, then, was industrialisation, for which capital
and technologies are essential requirements. Many countries rel y on foreign direct
investment (FDI) for capital and technologies, but even that was not a feasible option for
Korea back then because (1) the Korean people in the early 1960s had very strong feelings
against foreign investors due to concerns about the possibility of being recolonised
economically by foreign capital, and also because (2) foreign investors did not want to invest
in Korea because of the political uncertainties and economic risks associated with investing
in a country situated on an ideologically divided, cold-war frontier.
Korea could meet the capital requirement for industrialisation by borrowing from
international financial institutions, but technology was not something that could be simply
borrowed and utilised; rather, it required a process of absorption and assimilation before it
could be applied to domestic industries. Fortunately, Korea had a large pool of well-trained
human resources, which means that Korea also had a relatively s trong technological
absorptive capacity. So, Korea resorted to non-FDI channels for technology acquisition, such
as turn-key plant importation, f oreign licensing, OEM (Own Equi pment Manufacturing)
production arrangements, and so on. In addition, it could acquire technologies by learning
how to operate the technologies brought in from foreign sources in the early stages of
development. In the 1980s, Korea began to switch the mode of technology acquisition from
learning to domestic R&D, and launched national R&D programmes for the development of
key technologies required for the development of strategic industries such as electronic and
electric technologies, machinery, etc. Since then, Korea has pursued a development strategy
based on domestic “R&D and innovation”. Thus, “S&T-based development strategy” was
not in fact a strategy that Korea opted for but rather one that was imposed upon Korea.

 The role of the government: Setting the stage for S&T-based growth
Innovation is not a natural phenomenon but a result of human interventions in the form of
new ideas, research, development, and other factors that are costly in the economic sense.
When people face competition, new economic opportunities, or external threats or have an
internal desire for changes for the better, they seek ways of dealing with the situation, for

2. STI Governance

2.1 STI Committees
South Korea’s STI governance is often characterised by STI-related committees representing
three different hierarchical levels. At the uppermost strategic level is the Cabinet Meeting,
which is presided over by the President of the Republic of Korea. Then, there is a slew of
councils at the middle level, which are assisted by the group of secretariats and supporting
organisations positioned at the bottom level. These three levels are closely interconnected,
affecting STI policy planning and decision-making.
The history of the first Korean STI committee dates back to the early 1970s. The Korean
government, led by late former president Park, Jung-hee, establ ished the Overall S&T
Review Council in 1973 to effect ively implement policies for promoting the heavy and
chemical industries. The council coordinated STI policies and p rojects by appointing the
country’s prime minister as its chair and the deputy prime minister for economic affairs as
its vice-chair. The members of the council also included related ministers and experts from
the private sector. After twenty years of active operation, this council was reshuffled into
the Ministerial Council on S&T in 1996, presided over by the Mi nister of Finance and
Economy. One of the key missions of the renewed council represe nted by STI-related
ministries was to coordinate STI policies and projects.
As the scope of the National R&D Programme initiated in the early 1980s started to expand
to involve multiple ministries, there rose a need for a council at the highest level capable of
inter-ministerial coordination. To meet such needs, the National S&T Council (NSTC) was
launched in 1999 as the highest-ranked STI-related decision-making body and remained
operative until 2018. In addition, the Cabinet Council on S&T (CCST) was formed in 1996 to
effectively address working-level issues among S&T-related ministries. Aside from these
decision-making bodies, the Presidential Advisory Council on S&T (PACST) was established
in 1991. Since then, the council has faithfully fulfilled its roles as the presidential advisory

meet the increasing demand for expertise due to the increasingly complex nature of the
policy environment resulting from massive economic development.
The history of Korean STI think tanks dates back to the 1970s. Ever since the Park Jung-hee
government launched the first Five-Year Economic Development Plan in 1962 to revive the
impoverished Korean economy, successive Korean governments have consistently pursued
the implementation of five-year economic development plans. As the five-year cycle
repeats itself over the years, however, the complexity of the plans has increased greatly,
raising the need to secure expertise in economic policies and policy options. In the early
stages, Korea tended to rely on foreign brains from the developed countries as an important
s o u r c e o f e x p e r t i s e . H o w e v e r , t he country soon ran up against t he limitations of using
foreign expertise in developing policies tailored to the Korean context, which increased the
need to develop and secure internal expertise.

industrial development with strong support from the then President Park Jung-Hee. KIST

6.1 Definition of Technology Transfer
Technology Transfer (TT) is a multifaceted concept that encompasses a relatively wide range
of definitions and interpretations, and which is vital for establishing a common ground of
understanding in this field. Initially defined somewhat narrowly by Reisman in 1989, TT is
seen as the process of conceivin g and implementing novel applic a t i o n s f o r e x i s t i n g
technologies. However, a more comprehensive view suggests that TT should be regarded
as an extensive suite of processes. This broader definition includes all aspects pertaining to
the origins and adoption of knowledge, experience, and equipment across diverse contexts,
such as countries, organisations, and institutions. While it remains challenging to measure
and analyse due to its implicit nature in the international tra de of goods, services, and
processes, and the difficulty in quantifying tec hnology content , TT is nonetheless
particularly instrumental in the manufacturing sector. In this context, it involves the
utilisation of human, physical, and capital resources, leading to improvements in facilities,
e q u i p m e n t , p r o d u c t i o n , m a n a g e m e n t , a n d m a r k e t i n g . T h i s i s a c h i eved by providing the
relevant knowledge, skills, information, and assistance related to TT, underlining its
significance in advancing technological capabilities and organisational growth.

contrast, informal channels encompass various methods not classified under the formal
routes. Notably, Original Equipment Manufacturing (OEM) and Rev erse Engineering are
prominent in this category, especially in East Asian countries like Korea and Taiwan, as
highlighted in the paper. These informal methods have been recognised as vital pathways
for technology acquisition and ad aptation in developing countri es, offering alternative

1. Science and Technology Policy Institute

STEPI, or the Science and Technology Policy Institute, is a South Korean research institute
that plays a pivotal role in shaping and advising on the country's science and technology
policies. Established in 1987 with the aim of driving national development through science
and technology innovation, STEPI conducts in-depth research and analysis of current and
emerging trends in the science and technology sector. It serves as a think tank that provides
strategic insights and policy reco mmendations to the government , assisting in the
formulation of effective and forward-looking science and technology policies.
The Centre for Science and Technology Policy (CSTP), establishe d under KAIST, was
rebranded as STEPI in 1993 and aligned with KIST. It laid the groundwork for science and
technology policy research, acting as a resource for government b o d i e s , a n d a n a l y s i n g
global trends and methodologies for national R&D investment assessments. STEPI played a
pivotal role in enhancing R&D pr oject planning and evaluation, formulating long-term
strategic visions, and fostering an S&T innovation system to position Korea alongside the
developed nations. Addi t i o n a l l y , S T E P I ' s e f f o r t s i n c l u d e d r e s e ar c h o n S & T s e c u r i t y , t h e
execution of national S&T projects, and providing educational programmes on S&T policy
for developing nations.
In 1999, STEPI was positioned under the National Research Council for Economics and Social
Sciences which, in 2005 , expanded its scope to include the huma nities, thus being

4. Gyeongbuk Technopark(GBTP)

industry to adapt to shifting mar ket demands, including the tra nsition towards
environmentally-friendly vehicles and the integration of advanc ed materials into
automotive manufacturing. The centre aims to catalyse a paradigm shift among automotive
parts manufacturers towards business restructuring and innovation.

impact. Its strategic goals encompass enhancing equipment operation, diversifying research
areas based on corporate demand, and bolstering long-term corporate growth. The centre's
commitment is towards fostering exceptional local companies, en couraging company-
