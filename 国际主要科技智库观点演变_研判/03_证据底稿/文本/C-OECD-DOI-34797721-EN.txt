Abstract
Research Infrastructures (RIs) play a prominent role in advancing research
in all scientific domains. However, individual RIs do not host all the
equipment and resources required to tackle complex scientific or societal
challenges, particularly when these are multi- or inter-disciplinary in nature.
The development of integrated RI ecosystems offers new opportunities for
enhancing the scope, impact and efficiency of individual RIs, but requires
significant incentives to overcome the practical challenges that hinder their
creation and sustainability. This policy report identifies and analyses good
practices and presents a set of recommendations and guidance to help
policymakers and RI managers support RI ecosystems across research
disciplines and enhance the added value of RIs for society.

Foreword
Research Infrastructures (RIs) play a prominent role in advancing research in all scientific domains. By
providing users access to a diversity of high-performance and sometimes unique scientific equipment , as
well as data and digital services , RIs enable researchers to carry out projects and investigate problems
that cannot be addressed at the lev el of a traditional small-scale research laboratory serving a limited
number of users . RIs themselves are increasingly complex structures, often multimodal, and at times
distributed over different sites . As data providers, they often connect a wide diversity of databases and
foster relationships with local or national research institutes, post-secondary institutions, and private sector
organisations.
The COVID -19 crisis demonstrated the significant potential of RIs in addressing complex societal
challenges. RIs all over the world worked together in a complementary way (in RI ecosystems) to address
challenges beyond their traditional scientific domains and to provide services to new categories of users,
who required their products to address complex interdisciplinary questions relevant to the pandemic. While
similar collaborations are pursued primarily to support excellent science , the last five years have also
increasingly shown RI ecosystems’ role in assisting decision-makers as they address grand societal
challenges.
During the recent OECD Global Science Forum (GSF) activity on “Very Large Research Infrastructures
(VLRIs): issues and options”, surveys conducted among RI managers and decision-makers identified the
development of closer collaborations between VLRIs and other RIs as an interesting option for enhancing
their scope and impact. Even though the development of integrated RI ecosystems holds promising
potential, there are practical challenges that hinder their creation, efficiency and sustainability. Among them
are issues in establishing governance models to facilitate cooperation and coordinated or integrated
operations of RIs that function under very different frameworks. Funding and the sharing of material and
human resources also often present a significant barrier to cooperation and collaboration, particularly
across borders.
Based on its previous work on RI policy, the GSF launched a new project in 2023 entitled ‘ Fostering
research infrastructure ecosystems for addressing co mplex scientific and societal challenges ’. The main
output of this project, which is described in this report, is a set of recommendations and guiding tools to
help policymakers and RI managers to support the formation of RI ecosystems across research disciplines
and enhance the added value of these RI collaborations for society. Interesting examples of RI ecosystems
and good practice examples are described in annexes.

Executive Summary
In 2023 the Global Science Forum (GSF) mandated the OECD Secretariat and an international Expert
Group with analysing the current and potential future landscape of RI Ecosystems and how they enable
solutions to global scientific and societal challenges.
This report is based largely on an analysis of information collected by the Expert Group which oversaw the
work (see Annex 1), composed by experts from 14 countries, the European Commission and two
international organisations. It also inc orporates the outcomes of two dedicated international workshops.
The report provides information on the current landscape of RI collaborations and identifies and analyses
good practices to develop and sustain RI ecosystems. It also identifies policies and drivers that are required
to foster these collaborations and provide added value.
An exploration of existing ecosystems provided a robust picture of the current landscape and revealed a
broad variety of structures with various objectives that could potenti ally be defined as RI ecosystems. A
consensus definition for an RI ecosystem was determined for this report as a “Dynamic collaboration
between research infrastructures which ha s been developed around a set of shared strategic
objectives and provides, supports or coordinates services and/or activities at the level of the RI
collaboration, while the individual RIs remain responsible for their own governance, strategy and
business”.
The landscaping analys is showed considerable heterogeneity in the level of synergy and integration
among RIs in these partnerships and a trend towards an evolution from national or regional partnerships
between similar RIs to more international and multidisciplinary collaborations . It also indicated a deficit of
structured ecosystems in some geographical areas and some disciplines, such as social sciences and
humanities. Taking into account the diversity of RI ecosystems, the report proposes a tentative typology
based on the different rationales for development of collaborative initiatives among RIs.
Detailed analyses of case studies focused on key elements of RI ecosystems, including the rationale
behind their creation, their objectives and key achievements to date, including their potential for addressing
complex scientific and societal challenges. This included also an analysis of: organisational, governance
and financing models; user and data policies; human resource issues; and, impact assessment outcomes.
Based on this analysis, a RI ecosystems checklist was deve loped that identifies issues that should be
considered by RI managers to ensure that RI ecosystems are able to form and successfully meet their
goals.
The successful development of an RI ecosystem is not solely dependent on a robust operational
organisation of the partnership. Policymakers and funders also play a critical role in ensuring the conditions
for sustainable and successful RI collaborations. This report discusses key policy enablers for fostering
successful RI ecosystems, including supportive str ategy and planning policies, dedicated financial
resources and funding instruments, and adapted organisational models.
Building on the Expert Group analyses, this report lays out ten recommendations, each of which requires
actions from a variety of actors. These recommendations, including the context in which they are defined,
are laid out more fully in the next section of this report.

Recommendations
This section summarises ten key recommendations to advance the development of RI ecosystems. These
recommendations emerged from the analysis of the survey responses of over 40 RI ecosystems (see
annex 4). This survey explored the rationales for the development of partnerships between RIs, the
challenges faced by RI managers in developing closer collaborations with other RIs, and the policies that
encouraged their development and deepened their impact . The recommendations reflect the complexity
and variety of actors and interactions that are active in a thri ving RI ecosystem, and they underscore the
requirement for a systemic policy approach1. They are organised below according to specific issues, with
examples of practical solutions and good practices being described in more details in the report and
annex 5.
RI collaborations represent a unique opportunity for accelerating the pace of scientific discovery as well as
to structure and strengthen the international research landscape (for example through enhanced
collaboration between large and small/medium siz e RIs, and collaboration between RIs from developed
and emerging economies) and increase the value and efficiency of RIs. There is need for effective
information sharing to support the development of successful RI ecosystems.
Science policymakers, research funders and RI managers should recognise the unique value of RI
ecosystems in supporting scientific advancement and addressing complex societal challenges. RIs should
be enabled to network and add participation in RI ecosystems to th eir value proposition. This can be
incentivised by including it as a strategic objective for individual RIs and embedding it in their mission.
Existing international fora, such as the International Conference on Research Infrastructures (ICRI) should
take account of the critical role of RI ecosystems in the research landscape and provide opportunities for
RI ecosystems to share good practice and lessons learned. RI road-mapping and strategic exercises play
an important role in establishing funding priorities and in shaping the RI landscape.
1. Strategic exercises and road-mapping by institutions and governments should incorporate
an ecosystem view into their design objectives and therefore incentivise RI collaboration
and synergies, particularly at the international level where collaboration between RIs
remains challenging.
RI ecosystem initiatives require proactive consideration of cooperation arrangements or models and may
require more formalised agreements to achieve ambitious objectives. These should be flexible and
responsive to facilitate the access and integration of new members, evolution of objectives and adaptation
to unforeseen challenges.
2. Governments and research agencies should explore various models of cooperation that
can respond to the need s of RI ecosystems , and in particular flexibility and dynamism .
There is considerable opportunity for mutual learning across countries and scientific
domains in this respect.

RI ecosystems require adequate resources to maximise the added value of synergies and collaborations.
This should not be limited to financial support and is likely to be more effective when integrated into an
overall strategic approach towards supporting RI ecosystems.
3. Research funders and governments should implement sustainable funding models for RIs
to stimulate the creation of RI ecosystems and incentivise RIs to join existing ecosystems,
with enhanced efforts to include the participation of RIs from emerging economies .
Access to reliable data often remains an obstacle to the advancement of many areas of science. Although
many RIs manage vast amounts of data of critical importance and have made considerable efforts to make
them accessible, there is still a considerable heterogeneity in data policies at the level of individual RIs,
sometimes even within the same field. RI ecosystems have the potential to act as key digital enablers
within and across different research fields.
4. RI ecosystems should, where appropriate, include in their objectives the task of developing
shared digital platforms and data access mechanisms and policies as well as standardised
data formats to enhance the usefulness of the data produced by their members.
Many RIs are facing challenges to attract and retain the highly skilled workforce they require and enhanced
mobility within RI ecosystems could both improve efficiency and provide better career opportunities for RI
staff. At the same time, RIs serve increasingly diverse community of users, which require training, and are
expected also to transfer skills and know-how to the economy and society, which are challenging tasks for
individual facilities.
5. RIs should be incentivised, including with funding, to leverage their capacities in training,
staff development and mobility, and skill s exchange with different sectors through RI
ecosystems.
While individual RIs increasingly carry out impact assessment for their activities, in response to funder and
host requirements, the value of collaborative undertakings is often poorly assessed.
6. RI ecosystems should lead and actively facilitate activities to monitor and evaluate not just
the impact of their member facilities , but also that of the ecosystems themselves. They
should develop common approaches to impact assessment and building evidence to
support funding applications for individual facilities and for the ecosystem.
Research security challenges are an increasing concern worldwide. RIs play a unique role as gatekeepers
to technologies and capabilities and rely heavily on international collaboration. The security challenges
they face can be more acute , and more damagi ng, than those faced by most research and higher -
education institutions. Such challenges may be better addressed collectively than by individual RIs in
isolation.
7. RI ecosystems should develop common actions in security risk assessment and risk
mitigation through the sharing of good practice, raising awareness and developing shared
principles and protocols.
The emergence of integrated RI partnerships that enhance synergies between RIs offers valuable
opportunities for accelerating scientific discoveries and addressing complex societal challenges but
realising these will require strategic vision and revised financial and policy incentives. Dialogue should be
established between policymakers, funders, and RI managers to align strategies and support mechanisms
and support RI ecosystems.

Background
Research Infrastructures (RIs) play a prominent role in advancing research in all scientific domains. By
providing users access to a diversity of high -performance and sometimes unique scientific equipment as
well as data and digital services, they enable researchers to carry out projects and investigate problems
that cannot be addressed at the level of a traditional small -scale research laboratory serving a limited
number of users. RIs themselves are increasingly complex structures, often multimodal, and at times
distributed over different sites. As data providers, they often connect a large diversity of databases, and
foster relationships with local or national resea rch institutes, poste -secondary institutions, and private
sector organisations . Nevertheless, most existing RIs are domain -specific and often serve a restricted
expert user community, an issue that was underlined in the 2020 report on “Optimising the Opera tion and
Use of National Research Infrastructures” (OECD/Science Europe, 2020 [1]). Many RIs have unexploited
potential to expand their user communities. In addition, expectations from funders and policy-makers have
grown and now include an emphasis on socio-economic impact, which goes beyond the production of new
knowledge (see the 2019 GSF report on assessing the scientific and socio-economic impact of research
infrastructures (OECD, 2019[2])).
Recent crises have triggered turbulence, instability, and insecurity in contemporary societies, with impacts
on economies, politics, and global affairs. Science, technology and innovation (STI) are uniquely positioned
to help drive the necessary transformation towards more resilient economies and societies. However, STI
systems may need to reform to fulfil this potential. To support a reappraisal of STI policy, the OECD
developed the Agenda for Transfo rmative Science, Technology and Innovation Policies (OECD, 2024[3])
which was endorsed by a 2024 ministerial declaration on Transformative Science, Technology and
Innovation Policies for a Sustainable and Inclusive Fu ture2. Research and technology infrastructures are
expected to play a major role in this transformation agenda as an important vector for the new STI-based
solutions needed to address emerging societal challenges. Indeed, the COVID-19 crisis demonstrated the
potential of RIs to address societal challenges: not only were RIs able to mobilise quickly but they adapted
their activities to respond to the challenges raised by the pandemic. Thus, not only were biomedical RIs
fully refocused on COVID -19 issues, but many RIs from other domains also reoriented their activities to
support scientists involved in COVID -19 research (OECD, 2023[4]). For example, physics RIs proposed
their equipment for identify drug targets and therapies or their high -capacity computing facilities for the
development of epidemiological models and data mining. The pandemic also fostered new coordination
mechanisms and partnerships between RIs: Genomic centres and high-performance sequencing platforms
linked up to facilitate the survey of variants of the virus, and in Europe, a common Alliance of Medical
Research Infrastructures was established to facilitate broad user access to facilities, data and resources.
Collaboration between RIs did not start with the COVID -19 pandemic. In a number of scientific domains,
RIs have long been operating in partnerships to connect complementary equipment and modalities and
enable more complex experiments. IceCube, the neutrino observatory, for example, has formed
collaborations on topics of common interest with other neutrino telescopes deployed in the Mediterranean
and in Lake Baikal through cooperative agreements. There are also strong collaborations among fusion
facilities, between synchrotrons, or research vessels for example. However, what the COVID -19 crisis
demonstrated is the capacity of RIs to work together in a complementary way to address complex

Considering these issues, OECD Global Science Forum launched, in 2023, a new activity on Research
Infrastructure ecosystems. The objective was to i nvestigate the opportunities and challenges as well as
the practical models for the development of integrated RI ecosystems.
Methodology
This activity was undertaken by an international Expert Group (EG) nominated by the OECD Global
Science Forum (Annex 1). The EG was initially co -chaired by Maria Faury (CEA, France), and Roland
Roberts (NSF, the United States), and then by Roland Roberts alo ne following a change in Maria Faury’s
position. Experts from international organisations interested in RI policy were also invited. The Terms of
Reference of the work were proposed by a Scoping Group and approved by the Global Science Forum in
October 2023. Preliminary fact-finding and discussion with the EG were conducted to identify existing RI
ecosystems worldwide and agree on a set of common definitions, as well as to identify existing challenges
to provide a basis for surveys. A landscaping exercise was then carried out by the EG to review the diversity
of existing and planned RI networks, consortia, agreements and collaborations and to identify the diversity
of ecosystem models, their organisation and main objectives. This exercise was also useful in defining
what is an RI ecosystem and what it is not (see next chapter).
Based on the results of this landscaping activity, a survey was then carried out among RI managers
involved in RI ecosystems to provide detailed information on their rationale, history and strategic
objectives, their main achievements to date, their organisational and financial models and their
sustainability, their user and data access policies, their human resource policies, and their impact
assessment. It is important to note that th e survey, carried out on over 30 RI ecosystems, was designed
to be illustrative rather than statistically representative and was focussed on analysing various options and
deliverables from a diversity of examples. Survey responses do not necessarily reflec t the full range of RI
ecosystems but were deemed sufficiently diverse to capture both heterogeneities and commonalities.
Two international workshops specific to this activity were organised, one held virtually on 28 February
2024, and another held in hybrid format on 5 November 2024 in Amsterdam, The Netherlands (see agenda
in Annex 2). Both workshops included case study presentations from a subset/sample of RI ecosystems
stakeholders illustrating the challenges they face and providing interesting and practical solutions and
options. The first workshop discussed issues related to rationale, sustainability and user/data access, while
the second investigated more broadly the impact of RI ecosystems on the research system itself, including
the perspectives of funders and policymakers. Stakeholder inputs from the survey and workshops on
practices and policies provided more detailed information on key challenges and good practices, which are
included in the report.

This chapter introduces the definition of RI ecosystems that is adopted in this report and its relationship
and extension to other related polic y terms. It also contains a brief review of the existing RI networks,
consortia, agreements and collaborations, desc ribing the current diversity of ecosystem models, their
organisation and objectives.
What is a research infrastructure ecosystem?
There is no generic definition for what constitute s a RI ecosystem. It can be defined under its local or
regional dimension or included within a whole international network that connect RIs to other institutions.
It may include only relationships with other research institutions or connections between RIs of different
sizes and domains, including all their socio-economic activities and links with industrial partners, suppliers,
or policy communities.
In this report, a RI ecosystem is defined as “A dynamic collaboration between research
infrastructures which has been developed around a set of shared strategic objectives and provides,
supports or coordinates services and/or activities at the level of the RI collaboration, while the
individual RIs remain responsible for their own governance, strategy and ‘business” . Importantly,
this definition emphasises on the added value brought by the active partnership. It excludes loose networks
that have no clear common objectives and/or have no common deliverables as well as collaboratives that
are centered around single RIs. Other relevant definitions for this report are detailed in Box 1.

RI ecosystems diversity
An initial investigation was carried out to explore existing ecosystems in their diversity to help identify
existing gaps and get a more comprehensive picture of the RI ecosystems landscape, that could serve as
a starting point for a more detailed survey on interesting cases (the objective was not to carry out a
complete and systematic inventory). This initial investigation included a broad variety of structures with
various objectives that could potentially be defined as RI ecosystems (see Definitions in previous section),
including partnerships that may n ot be fully formalised and still have a loose organisation but that have
developed synergies between members . Both emerging and consolidated ecosystems examples were
included in order to learn from a diversity of cases and understand challenges as well as to identify
solutions. Priority was given, whenever possible, to ecosystems that include in their role a capacity to
respond to societal challenges.
A generic template was agreed upon for collecting information (presented in Annex 3) . During this
landscaping exercise, the EG collected information regarding over 40 RI ecosystems of national, regional
and international dimension , which are listed in Annex 4 together with some of their key characteristics .
What emerged is a very large diversity of initiatives, that informed a number of interesting findings:

• The level of synergy and integration between RIs in these partnerships varies a lot. This
landscaping survey helped determine what could be identified as a RI ecosystem (see previous
sub chapter) and what should rather just be considered as a research network.
• While there is an increasing trend in the development of RI collaborative networks or consortia at
national or regional level, few have an international dimension, which highlights real challenges for
RIs to collaborate at international level.
• The majority of identified ecosystems were developed between similar RIs or within specific fields;
the development of ecosystems that include RIs from different domains is usually more recent ,
suggesting that incentives for multidisciplinary partnerships have mostly been developed recently.
• RI ecosystems have a broad diversity of strategic goals. This usually includes some generic
objectives, s uch as increasing the visibility of their user community and strengthen ing their

Rationale/history
This section articulates the motivations and reasons behind the creation of the RI ecosystems and the
major steps marking their evolution since inception; For many of the RI ecosystems analysed (see the
NCRIS Health group case study in Annex 5), respondents described a two-pronged rationale pertaining to
the benefits of the collaboration for its RI members and for their users.
1. Expected impact on the RIs:
• Building a community and increased visibility, broadening the user community and opening up to
new users;
• Increasing influence (on research funders and policy makers), more effectively leveraging existing

• Promoting coordination and integration of the member RI activities;
• Supporting research on common ambitious/complex goal s (beyond the capacity of single RI

addressing global challenges or from bottom-up synergies in response to user;
• Coordinating or centralising RIs’ proposal management or peer review systems;
• Sharing and thereby optimising human, material and capital resources;
• Sharing technologies, techniques and know-how
2. For the users:
• Increasing the chances of securing funding (applying for funds to use the capacities of an RI
ecosystem is often perceived positively by funders compared to using a single RI);
• Increasing/facilitating access to RIs: enhanced visibility for users (ecosystems can better advertise
their facilities), shared/simplified/harmonised access procedures (“one-stop-shop”);
• Increasing/facilitating access to the data and data products origina ting from member RIs (in

RI ecosystems to address complex challenges
The COVID-19 crisis demonstrated the added value of RIs working together to tackle complex issues (see
examples in Box 2). Since then, t he potential of RIs to contribute to addressing societal challenges has
increasingly been put forward by decision-makers and funders, as well as by RI managers and users.

Grand societal and scientific challenges cut across disciplinary siloes, sectors, geographies, and
government domains. As such, the development and operation of elements of science systems, and in
particular RIs, deployed to address these challenges requires collaboration at the system level. Two types
of RI ecosystems aiming at addressing these complex challenges co-exist: those that were specifically
developed with this as their primary objective, and existing ecosystems that take advantage of the added
value of working together to include this goal as a new deliverable. RI ecosystems might be permanent or
transient features of the RI landscape. This flexibility/agility for assembly and dissolution, as needed, must
be built into any RI ecosystem framework as it allows for models of RI ecosystem development, interaction,
and evolution as the landscape of scientific and societal challenges evolves.
In addition, individual RIs are sometimes a member of more than one RI ecosystem , developing
collaborations and synergies between RIs in adjacent research fields. Indeed, the evolution of cooperation
between existing RI ecosystems can be important for tackling global challenges and incorporating different
regional or thematic perspectives. Such dynamic systems are typically being developed in environmental
and health domains, for example integrating different branches of earth sciences (e.g., in global monitoring
system), or across disciplines (e.g., for studying environmental impact on human health ). They may be
driven by mission-oriented policies (see Box 3) or broad internati onal agreement s on societal priorities
(such as the sustainable development goals adopted by the United Nations or other large UN -driven
programmes). While these collaborations are often loosely organised, there are opportunities for increased
synergies between RIs operating as ecosystems.

Geopolitics
Geopolitics has an important influence on the broad policy context that may affect the success or the
orientation of RI ecosystems. This influence is very much domain- and context-specific. For example, the
COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the development of RI ecosystems (see the Japanese ARFP case study
example in Annex 5). In Europe, the priorities and structure of the EC funding schemes have had a strong
impact on European RI ecosystems, and, similarly, top-down policy directives can have a strong influence
on the development of RI partnerships (see the GC -MAC case study example in Annex 5) . International
ecosystems are potentially more sensitive to geopolitical context, as they have members in different
countries and often have no dedicated support funding for their joint functions.
Research security issues, which are increasingly important from a policy perspective (OECD, 2022[6]), may
have significant implications for RI operations. These may be related to cybersecurity threats, international
collaboration, Intellectual Property concerns or user and staff management and mobility. Although a
growing number of RIs (or their host institution(s)) are aware of the need to respond to security threats that
require better risk management3, these are still poorly considered at the level of RI ecosystems . Indeed,
what emerges from discussion with RI managers is that potential security threats are still largely addressed
on a case -by-case basis, with limited sharing of informat ion and good practices between RIs. RI
ecosystems present a strong opportunity for sharing good practice, raising awareness and developing
shared principles regarding security risks that could reduce hurdles in international collaboration.
Sustainability of RI ecosystems (governance and funding)
RI ecosystems are meta-structures that function above and beyond the governance and business model
of their individual components. Ensuring the sustainability of the ecosystem was raised as a major
challenge by most RI ecosystem managers, since there is usually no given organisational model or
resourcing mechanism to support such initiatives. This highlights the need for a clearly defined operating
framework and governance mechanisms as well as a long-term business model. A number of issues were
identified, in this regard, in the survey:
• Most RI ecosystems report a lack of an adequate legal or governance model adapted to their need
(particularly for partnerships with an international dimension) and hav e had to work around the
limitations of their model to develop something practical and adapted to their objectives. Even in
Europe where RIs are more used to work in networks, existing models have been described as
poorly adapted to the RI ecosystems needs . For example, the European Research Infrastructure
Consortium (ERIC) status in the European Union, which has been considered by a number of
ecosystems as a potential solution as it has been extensively used for European distributed RIs,
is also perceived as not necessarily adapted to RI ecosystems that present different characteristics
and needs. The more flexible “Association Internationale Sans But Lucratif” (AISBL, International
non-profit association) status has been a preferred choice for a number of initiatives, but still
presents limitations for ecosystems that strive for increased synergy.
• Many RI ecosystems require an evolution of their legal model from a loose membership agreement
to a more structured legal entity as they develop. This is often deemed necessary to enhance their
sustainability and their capacity to build agreements with partners, to develop new and more
ambitious common activities, to attract funding for shared activities, to hire staff etc. (see the case
study of LaserLab EU in An nex 5). There is a demand/need for long -term legal entity/structure to
achieve objectives beyond simple coordination.
• Built-in flexibility/responsiveness in the model is very important to facilitate the access/integration
of new members and for members to leave as well if needed (flexibility in financial management

• Financial support for RI ecosystems is nearly always a challenge. In some case, part of the funding

mechanisms. Furthermore, setting up major initiatives/projects between RI ecosystem members
often depends on external funding that may come with strings attached (there may be conflicting
agenda/priorities between the individual RI funder and alternate funding sources that support and

example through returns on the provision of services.

discuss the growing problem of energy cost/use (for exa mple investigating options for shared

environmental footprint of RIs has yet to be addressed at ecosystem level.

Facilitating and/or simplifying access to RI facilities and to the data generated by RIs is frequently indicated

(in data, computational, or indeed spoken language ) that need to be harmonised . Furthermore, within RI
ecosystems that have an international dimension , members may be subjected to specific and c onflicting
national rules or requirements. Survey feedback indicates that:
• There is a trend towards the development of common access portals (one -shop-stops) that can

Human resources
Although RI ecosystems have various levels of integration, human resource management is usually not
within their remit even in those ecosystems that have formed a legal entity. However, this is a topic that is
increasingly of concern to RI ecosystem managers and f or which some common issues are being
discussed at ecosystem level:
• There is often no common human resource strategy within RI ecosystems (HR policies are still
devolved to individual members) but the recruitment/retention of personnel is a challenge for many
facilities. Some RI ecosystems are thus now working on better career paths and on
gender/minority participation.

Impact assessment
Although a few RI ecosystems have been running for several years, and the development of RI networks
is often perceived favourably by funders and science policy makers alike, there is still a limited knowledge
of their actual value and impact:
• The added value of RI ecosystems is rarely assessed on a systematic basis, whether internally or
externally, although many ecosystems do have yearly reports that include some form of self -
assessment of performance (often focused on organisation, successful funding support for their
members etc.). Some are interested also in assessing their socio -economic impact (see the NEF
case study in Annex 5). There are no standard frameworks or performance indicators for assessing
their impact as a whole, but some RI e cosystems support the development of common impact
assessment for all their members.
• Some RI ecosystems have developed support for their members in assessing the potential impact
of their projects when submitting proposals to increase their chance of bein g funded.
Guiding tools
As introduced in the previous chapter, the concept for this chapter is to provide g uidance for RI
managers on key elements to take into consideration when developing RI ecosystems and on
important supporting policies that could be developed by research policy stakeholders. Thus, in
this section we provide some guiding tools to proactively foster an environment that supports the formation
and success of RI ecosystems.
Based on the surveys and case studies undertaken during this act ivity (see Annexes 4 and 5), the RI
ecosystems checklist described in Box 4 provides elements that should be considered by RI managers
wishing to set up collaborative partnerships to ensure that RI ecosystems are able to form and successfully
meet their go als/objectives. It should be emphasised that productive collaboration of RIs should be
intentional and not left to chance. At the same time, it should be recognised that not all RIs may find it
productive to enter such collaborations and that when collabor ations do develop, the resulting ecosystem
may be transient or develop into something that is more permanent. In the latter situation the membership
arrangements should be such as to allow for the inclusion of new RIs and the exit of those already in the
partnership, under clearly defined conditions.

Box 4. RI ecosystem checklist for RI managers
Rationale/strategic objectives
Agreeing on strategic priorities and common objectives is critical to the success of RI ecosystems . Future members
need to clearly define the nature of the activities th at will be developed under the scope of the ecosystem. This can involve
extensive consultation between partner organisations and the development of a strategic plan, as well as agreeing of issues
such as intellectual property rights and data policies. Whi le agreements may evolve over time, shared priorities are required
to ensure the commitment of all members and attract the necessary support from users and external stakeholders.
Geopolitics and security
RI ecosystems need to be responsive to emerging cons traints generated by the evolution of the global political
context. This is particularly important in relation to societal challenges and to potential security threats. RI ecosystems should
serve as fora and testbeds for discussions and pilot actions to address shared challenges (such as environmental sustainability
and energy/resource use) and develop shared principles regarding security risks.
Governance of the RI ecosystem
RI ecosystems require structured agreements between its members to achieve objectives beyond simple coordination,
to attract funding and enhance their sustainability. There is no perfect model for RI ecosystem governance, especially when
there are members from different countries. Flexibility/responsiveness is very im portant to facilitate the access/integration of
new members, develop new objectives and respond to unforeseen challenges . RI ecosystems should establish collaborative
governance mechanisms, with clear roles and accountability, that bring together relevant experts and stakeholders.
Establishing a dedicated secretariat to assist with the RI ecosystem operations and develop appropriate strategies to address
new challenges is also recommended.
Financing RI ecosystems
Finding the necessary resources to support t he ecosystem’ objectives can be a real challenge as there is often no
adequate national or international incentive nor funding mechanism dedicated to RI ecosystems. There is a need for innovative
business models that generate the sustained resources required for the ecosystem’s operations. This can integrate diversified,
funding sources such as public-private partnerships, international funding, in-kind contributions, etc.
Data governance and user access
RIs operating in an ecosystem must have clear underst anding/agreements related to data and user access . There
should normally be an added benefit of the ecosystem for users of the individual RIs. RI ecosystems should have a shared
digital and data access strategy as well as standardised proposal submission p rocedures that foster open science. This can
encompass: piloting and experimenting with novel approaches to data standards; harmonisation of procedures for open
access to data that preserve rights and research ownership; shared cybersecurity policies; and, other outputs such as
standardised meta-data, methods, protocols, software, and code.
Human resources
Human resource policies adopted at the RI ecosystem level should foster a level of integration that leverages training
and staff development of its individual RIs. Promoting mobility and rotation of staff across RIs should be encouraged to improve
efficiency and provide better career opportunities.
Success metrics and evaluating success
As the operation of RI ecosystems can require significant resources from members, external funders and research institutions,
emphasis must be placed on clearly articulating the added value of the collaboration to all relevant stakeholders .
Impact assessments should be carried out, using appropriate performance and impact indicators, that are relevant for both RI
members and RI users.

The successful development of RI ecosystems is not solely dependent on a robust operational
organisation. Policy makers and funders also play a critical role in ensuring that the option to initiate or join
an ecosystem is built into the terms and conditions of individual RIs. Collaborative activities at local,
regional or international scales, if deemed a useful pursuit, should be costed and sufficient levels of funding
should be available to support those initiatives. Additionally, policymakers shape the operating context for
RIs around issues such as research security, intellectual property, or international cooperation. Potentially
useful policy actions for fostering successful RI ecosystems are described in Box 5.

Box 5. Policy actions for enabling the development of RI ecosystems
The concept of RI ecosystems should be included in strategic planning exercises (RI roadmaps etc.). RI strategic
exercises often constitute strong incentives and guidance for funding priorities and including RI ecosystems in these exercises
would help raise the awareness of their usefulness (note that ecosystems can be transient). RI ecosystems represent excellent
opportunities for North -South and South -South scientific collaboration and should therefore be integrated in regional and
international strategic planning.
One of the most significant obstacles to the development of RI ecosy stems is the fragmented approach to developing these
collaborative partnerships within and across countries. This fragmentation stems from differences in individual research
infrastructure policies (on data, intellectual property etc.), culture and programmes. Governments and research institutions
should promote and incentivise coherent RI policies (particularly for data management) that can facilitate collaboration.
There is currently lack of adequate organisational/legal models for international RI ecosystems. Governments and research
agencies should investigate new forms of organisational models, including legal entities where appropriate, that respond
to the need of RI ecosystems as well as of other integrated international research collaborations.
Funders need to develop specific funding mechanisms that promote and recognise the value of collaboration
between RIs and the development of RI ecosystems . Besides dedicated program funding for RI ecosystem development,
including the participation of RIs in RI ecosystems as a merit criterion or a criterion of eligibility for funders, could constitute a
strong incentive for collaboration. Incentives could also take the form of pilot projects that demonstrate the value of RI
ecosystems and their challenges early on
There is a lack of specific indicators to assess the added value of RI ecosystems. Funders should support the
development of indicators and methodologies that could be used in impact and performance evaluation.
Existing links between partners often constitute a prerequisite to the capacity of RI ecosystems to address complex challenges
(building on existing collaboration is much easier than starting from s cratch). Funders and research institutions should
therefore incentivise collaboration and synergies between RIs , and codevelop with RIs challenge-oriented projects that
are identified as strategic priorities.
Considering the complexity of the RI landscape amidst strained financial resources, there is a need for
funders and decision-makers to work together and promote a positive environment for the development of
efficient and sustainable RI ecosystems. M ission- and challenge -oriented funding schemes (Larrue,
2021[7])) can provide an opportunity for multiple RIs to cooperate to better leverage and help structure
existing research (and innovation) bases. One such example is Canada’s recent approach to revitali sing
its domestic life sciences and biomanufacturing sectors (see Box 6) in the wake of the COVID -19
pandemic. M ission-oriented approaches can help mitigate gaps in the research funding landscape but
must do so without generating superficial partnerships or additio nal layers of bureaucracy. Ideally such
approaches should help connect, structure and boost existing working partnerships and networks.

Box 6. Canada’s Biomanufacturing and Life Sciences Strategy: funding to the RI ecosystem
In 2020/21, the Government of Canada mobilised stakeholders across the entire life sciences domain to develop a
comprehensive Strategy5 focused on quickly and durably mitigating gaps in the country’s pandemic readiness and ability to
contribute competitively on the global scene through structuring and strategic investments along the biomanufacturing value
chain.
Adopting an end-to-end, whole-of-ecosystem approach with the engagement of several ministries, the Strategy set in motion
wide-ranging and coordinated programming across academia, government and industry, including upstream investments
approximating 2.2 billion CAD (not withstanding mandatory partner funding) into foundational elements of science. As such,
this represents the first-of-its-kind, large-scale “mission-oriented” RI funding initiative in Canada.
As part of these investments, the Canada Foundation for Innovati on (CFI), Canada’s main funder of RI, in coordination with
domestic funding counterparts launched three sequential and coordinated calls for proposals centred around RIs – notably
RIs for surveillance, diagnostics and therapeutics development, and high -level biocontainment facilities – but also covering
components for research and talent attraction and development. The calls were desi gned to identify, leverage and boost
existing networks of players and RI capabilities across sectors, and create meaningful synergies within the pan -Canadian RI
ecosystem. Central to this aim was the Strategy’s provision of a robust governance structure for decision-making and oversight
which provided guidance, strategic input, and feedback throughout the different stages of the merit review process. This
ensured that the final recommendations of the Strategic Committee for all three calls, represented the most globally relevant
and coherent portfolio of investments in alignment with the Strategy.
An interesting feature to this seemingly “top -down” initiative is the incorporation of a “bottom -up” element: the s election for
funding of five complementary Hubs of excellence6 through which the top-down aims of the Strategy could be more efficiently
realised. Each hub contribut es a critical mass of RI assets and well -established linkages; proposes solid governance to stir
the efforts of their coalition of partners and maintain their cohesion; and serves as a single point of entry to the vast ecosystem
of academic research and its connection to industry. Once identified, the Hubs were responsible for receiving and preselecting
within their coalitions, and among themselves, the best proposals according to their comparative strengths and
complementarities for review by the CFI and partners. Moving forward, the Hubs were also entrusted with a central role in the
implementation, oversight and accountability for the funded projects, in addition to that of the funders, as well as for developing
a common set of objectives and vision to prolong their sustainability on the longer term.
Ultimately, the benefits of this pilot approach will be gauged following the monitoring activities planned in the near term as part
of the Strategy’s oversight framework. It is nevertheless agreed upon among the many partners involved that this first
experience – while challenging to coordinate – provides a good starting point for the successful delivery of other mission -
driven initiatives in priority domains for a large group of stakeholders in Canada.

In order to test some of the preliminary conclusions that emerged from this project, a short visioning
exercise was conducted with Expert Group members and RI ecosystem managers. This included a survey
on the future landscape of RI ecosystems and a summary of the survey responses is provided in Box 7.
Box 7. Prospective views on the future of RI ecosystems
Q1: What might be the most important drivers for developing RI ecosystems in coming years?
1. Facilitating scientific breakthrough/frontier research and addressing complex and multidisciplinary challenges
(although the emphasis on these different drivers may differ between RIs and funders).
2. Enhancing the efficiency (governance, organisation…) of the RI system (more a priority for funders).
3. Increasing data and user access.
Q2: Where are future RI ecosystems most likely to develop in the next 10 years?
1. Regional dimension seems to be the most likely focus for new ecosystems. Europe is ahead in that res pect, but
there are opportunities and ongoing discussion in Latin America, Asia-Pacific and Africa for new partnerships.
2. National and International levels both offers opportunities, but there are complicated challenges for
international/global collaborations. Purely national ecosystems may offer less added value. Local ecosystems could
potentially generate more opportunities for technology transfer and regional economic development .
Q3: What is the most likely composition of future RI ecosystems?
1. Ecosystems composed of heterogeneous RIs in size/organisation but of the same domain are considered as most
likely to be developed as members share common features and scientific goals that would facilitate partnerships.
2. Interdisciplinary ecosystems are li kely to be more common. While there are challenging to develop, they offer
considerable potential for responding to emerging challenges, bringing complementarities and unique capacities to
the table.
Q4: What may be the most important obstacles in the future development of RI ecosystems?
1. Sustainability is perceived as the most important challenge, as dedicated resources are still rare.
2. Barriers to international collaborations (such as increased security concerns) are likely to be significant obstacle.
3. While there are important, obstacles related to practical organisation or heterogeneous RI policies and practices,
these considered as manageable if there is a strong will (and resources) to pursue collaboration.
Q5: What are the key policies that should be implemented to promote the future development of RI ecosystems?
1. Including RI ecosystems in national/international RI strategies is considered as potentially a very strong incentive.
In some countries, addressing the current fragmentation of the RI landscape is also considered as a prerequisite.
2. The development of dedicated funding streams is underlined as a necessary condition for thriving ecosystems
3. Recognising (and monitoring) the added value of RI ecosystems should help getting recognition and support

organisation and objectives (see Annex 4), their future development is dependent on a number of common

drivers may be perceived differently whether one takes the top-down view of policy makers or the bottom-
up view of RI managers and users. Increasing the overall agility and efficiency of the system may be a top
priority for funders/policy makers, while for RI managers and users, the capacity to deliver the best science
will remain the primary driver. Thus, a complementary mix of top -down (roadmaps/strategic exercises,
mission-oriented policies) and bottom-up actions (according to user needs) is required.
Another potential driver is the inclusion of RI ecosystems (i.e., their formation and persistence) in countries’
or institutions’ strategic planning exercises, or in domain-specific long term planning exercises, such as in
the Canadian example above (Box 6). RI roadmaps and similar strategic planning exercises have
considerable influence on RI support and policy orientation. Including RI ecosystems in these processes
can enhance their visibility to relevant stakeholders and encourage the development of partnerships. This
in turn can strengthen the argument for future funding and stimulate stronger engagement and commitment
from policymakers (missions and challenges embedded in strategies and roadmaps provide tangible goals
around which decision-makers can coalesce).
Regarding the optimal geographical scale for future RI ecosystems, there is a general agreement that
regional or international partnerships have strong added-value potential, provided that appropriate funding
mechanisms are developed in parallel . Similarly, the increasing push towards more interdisciplinarity
(noting that even RI ecosystems that start with a disciplinary focus tend to integrate more interdisciplinary
objectives) should be encouraged. However, the geographical distribution and disciplinary focus of an RI
ecosystem, should be flexible and align with its intent and goals.
Finally, the need for clear goals, metrics of success, and plans for assessing such success are deemed
essential and should be considered and planned for very early in the formation of RI ecosystems.
The picture that emerges from this work is that of a growing self -organisation of the RI landscape
worldwide, supported both by a need for better efficiency and for rationalising resources, and for providing
users with increased opportunities to combine RI services (equipment and data). The emergence of
integrated RI ecosystems with enhanced synergies offers new opportunities for scientific discovery and for
research addressing complex societal challenges, but requires recognition, together with financial and
policy incentives. In some senses, a more professional approach is now necessary to address a number
of common and recurrent practical c hallenges. A dialogue needs to be established between funders,
policymakers, RI managers and users to align strategies and support mechanisms to support RI
ecosystems.

3. Australian National Collaborative Research Infrastructure Strategy (NCRIS) Health Group
Objectives To advocate for, and to promote cooperation, collaboration, communication and reciprocal facilitation specifically
between the members, and also with other related NCRIS supported capabilities.
Rationale There are benefits to Health Group members from participation in the ecosystem. These include sharing of
knowledge and expertise, joint appointments and coordinated communication channels. Members have also
worked together on joint funding submissions.
There are also benefits to RI users who may experience more coordinated access to a range of national RIs and
have greater success with research grant proposals through leveraging the ecosystem. The breadth of exposure
to the health and medical research system through the five participants far exceeds what each organisation
could achieve as a single entity.
Organisation The NCRIS Health Group is a network governed by a MoU.
Achievements • A better coordinated approach to delivery of health and medical RIs in Australia – including
demonstrable examples across areas such as cancer, traumatic brain injury and cardiovascular
disease. Greater mutual awareness of capabilities and what the NCRIS Health Group can offers its
stakeholders.
• A proactive culture within the NCRIS Health Group of seeking out opportunities for collaboration.
• Joint staff appointments and initiatives between NCRIS Health Group members.
• Stronger connections between Australian national RIs and EU counterparts.
• Shared capacity to promote NCRIS supported capabilities to the broader research community (locally
and internationally), specifically to industry.
• Building the capacity to have a shared and thus a more powerful voice in terms of advocacy and for
issues the RI community faces.
• Shared advocacy with Australian health and medical research systems (NHMRC, Medical Research
Future Fund) in delivering research infrastructure at a local and national scale.

5. Japan Advanced Research Facilities Platform program (ARFP)
Objectives • Establishment of one-stop service centred on coordinating organisation, mutual cooperation for the use of
each organisation’s facilities
• Sharing of knowhow and data for remote use and automation of experiments
• Assignment, training of professional staff
Organisation Network managed by a committee set up by Japan’s Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and
Technology (MEXT) and steering committees for each discipline (4 discipline-based platforms), but still a loose
organisation with no legal structure; ongoing discussion for a more sustainable structure and regarding the
degree of integration
Achievements Increased visibility towards potential users, exchange of know-how between facilities, some exchanges of
resources, and increased support for users to help determine the best facility suitable for the project
Geopolitics The ARFP was established in 2021 for 5 years initially; the main driver was the Covid crisis that impeded the use
of many facilities. From 2016-2020, the MEXT had been promoting cross-institutional efforts to form platforms for
innovation by supporting several institutions with advanced research facilities that wanted to collaborate and by
requiring a central contact point and an advanced use support system for their use within each platform.
However, COVID-19 resulted in restricting the mobility of researchers, making access to research facilities
difficult. In particular, the limited availability of other alternatives for some of the leading advanced research
facilities had a significant impact on research activities, with the entire research plan being reviewed and/or
delayed. In response, MEXT decided to provide support to remote use and automation to maintain research
activities. The objective was also to improve the efficiency of facility use and the capacity to conduct experiments
remotely, which in turn would promote improvements in the use of advanced research facilities. The initial goal of
ARFP was thus to promote the use of the facilities and share user access practices (particularly the development
of remote access and data sharing). Following an open call by the MEXT, 4 networks were selected for support
in the disciplines of NMR, microscopic imaging, power lasers and research MRI, and activities to improve user
and data access are underway in each network.

6. German Canadian Materials Acceleration Centre (GCMAC)
Objectives Coordinate and integrate German and Canadian activities in accelerated materials research:
• Support technology-focused materials research.
• Advance method and tool development.
• Accelerate discovery, design, and scale-up of energy materials.
• Provide education and training programs
• Knowledge sharing & network/ecosystem building
Organisation Collaborative partnership of research activities between Germany and Canada, funded by matching (mostly in-
kind) resources from participating institutions, linked by an Agreement for Cooperation (April 2021). Includes a
virtual office supported by Germany and Canada which provides administration support, secretariat,
communication, and project management support, and management committee consisting of the principal
investigators from Germany and Canada.
Achievements GCMAC deployed one of the most highly interdisciplinary project scopes of any major project but with a modest
budget allocated to activities like collaborative support actions rather than flagship projects. It aligned facilities,
capabilities and expertise in two countries in a common goal to accelerate materials discovery in the important
topic of hydrogen production as an energy carrier.
Geopolitics The idea for the creation of GCMAC evolved from activities under the umbrella of the global initiative Mission
Innovation (MI), Clean Energy Materials Innovation Challenge IC6 (2017-2021), later Materials for Energy (M4E)
Collaborate Platform (2021-present), currently co-led by Germany and Canada. Concurrently, the ecosystem has
grown through engagement with other related international initiatives, with a focus on joint activities to promote
knowledge sharing and network building.

7. LaserLab EU
Objectives • To maintain a competitive, inter-disciplinary network of European national laser laboratories;
• To strengthen the European leading role in laser research through Joint Research Activities (JRA),
pushing the laser concept into new directions and opening up new applications of key importance in
research and innovation;
• To offer transnational access to top-quality laser research facilities in a highly co-ordinated fashion for
the benefit of the European research community;
• To increase the European basis in laser research and applications by reaching out to neighbouring
scientific communities and by assisting in the development of Laser Research Infrastructures on both
the national and the European level
Organisation Initially a cooperative undertaking between European laboratories, evolved into an Integrated Infrastructure
Initiative (I3) supported by the European FP6 programme, and currently in transition to a more permanent
organisation.
Achievements LaserLab fostered the development of an internationally recognised structured community, very visible in Europe
and highlighting the European laser science. The creation of ELI (European Light Infrastructure) stemmed from
discussion and lobbying from LaserLab.
LaserLab was instrumental in increasing the role of laser facilities in European science, for example the 2023
Nobel prize in Physics was linked to experiments conducted using lasers (Pierre Agostini, Ferenc Krausz and
Anne L’Huillier received the award for having developed methods that produce ultrafast laser pulses, which have
applications in chemistry, biology and physics).
Governance The governance structure for LaserLab as a I3 project is fairly standard with a general assembly, and
administration office (https://www.laserlab-europe.eu/about-us/governance). When information about the end of
the I3 came in, two possibles models were discussed: an association under Belgium law (AISBL) or a European
consortium (ERIC). The choice for an AISBL was determined because of its simplicity and because it can
accommodate institutions as members: only institutional agreements were required, not country agreements.
This has proven to be efficient in managing the network and useful when trying to approach or influence EC
projects/calls. The role of LaserLab as an AISBL is that of a coordinator and manager of the network, but it does
not have the capacity to conduct joint research projects; this is a problem because many members are small RIs
that are not access providers but just research facilities that are mostly interested in developing joint R&D
projects. Hence there are discussion whether the ERIC solution might still be adopted. This would provide more
core funding and hence allow the development of joint research activities.

8. Ocean Facilities Exchange Group (OFEG)
Objectives • To represent Europe’s leading oceanographic research organisations
• To provide a forum to consider barter exchange and co-operation opportunities for the Global and
Ocean Class research fleet.
• To maximise the overall scientific output using its state-of-the-art facilities in support of the worldwide
oceanographic community
• To develop interoperability of large equipment, the adoption of common working practices,
harmonised mechanisms, protocols and tools
Organisation OFEG functions as a collaborative partnership fostering information sharing and resource exchange. Originally a
tripartite agreement between Germany, France and UK; since joined by the Netherlands, Spain and Norway.
OFEG is an equal partnership between all members. OFEG-TECH, a more recently established subsidiary of
OFEG, was founded in 2007 to aid the development of OFEG from a pure ship barter organisation to one that is
also capable of delivering state of the art marine scientific facilities across all OFEG platforms, through
interoperability of large equipment, adoption of common working practices, harmonised mechanisms, protocols
and tools.
Achievements Compared to working as individual entities, the OFEG ecosystem provides scientists with significantly wider
access to research facilities and equipment; OFEG boasts a combined fleet of 21 research vessels, including a
majority of Europe's "heavy" equipment like submersibles, ROVs, and advanced survey systems, which
individual institutions might otherwise find too expensive to acquire on their own. This access extends beyond
just quantity, as OFEG includes 10 out of 11 Global Class and 8 out of 15 Ocean Class research vessels in
Europe, offering access to cutting-edge technology. Additionally, OFEG fosters cost-effectiveness by optimising
fleet movements based on the geographical location of ships, reducing travel time and associated costs. This
allows scientists to access a broader range of geographical regions for research within a single year.
Business model OFEG operates on a barter system, enabling joint cruises, ship-time exchange and equipment sharing without
the exchange of money. A points value is agreed for each asset and a running total or tally is kept for all partners
based on how much they take advantage barters as much as offer them. There have been a small number of
instances of charter within the OFEG group, but these have also come with added value as they have been run

9. Engineering Research Infrastructures for European Synergies (ERIES)
Objectives To provide transnational access to advanced research infrastructures in the fields of structural, seismic, wind and
geotechnical engineering.
Organisation ERIES is a project consortium (12 RIs from 7 European countries + Canada) supported by EU Horizon Europe
programme (running project from 2022 to 2026).
Achievements ERIES has broadened past networks focused on earthquake engineering to other hazards, gathering
complementary facilities for more interdisciplinary activities. It is a RI ecosystem aiming at impact beyond
research i.e. civil authorities, risks mitigation and risks management etc.
User and data access ERIES operates a unique user access mechanism, which is dedicated to transnational access (access of the
facilities to users outside the facilities’ host country). After an initial interaction with the targeted facilities, user
groups submit their proposals via a Google form to a centralised Transnational Access Selection and Evaluation
Panel (TA-SEP) which is in charge of reviewing the applications and selecting the projects to be funded and the
hosting facilities. Each research infrastructure provides feedback to the TA-SEP for the proposals submitted
requesting access to their installations. Competing proposals for each research infrastructure’s installation are
ranked based on the total score they receive from the TA-SEP.
Experimental data produced at ERIES facilities are stored and organised via online tools following FAIR
principles. All metadata and other information are available on a Data Access Portal.

10. ODISSEI
Objectives To improve user access to social sciences and humanities (SSHs) RIs. ODISSEI provides analytical tools,
algorithms, secure computing environment and specialised services and expertise.
Organisation ODISSEI is collaborative consortium of 45 member organisations in the Netherlands. It is financed structurally by
its members for up to €1 million per year (the total Operational Budget is ~€5 million per year).
Achievements ODISSEI coordinates all the Social Science Infrastructure of the Netherlands in a unique consortium. Social
Science is traditionally a very scattered field with very different data access policies and data standards. Not only
has ODISSEI been able to coordinate a wide range of social research institutions in the Netherlands but also
includes and provides secured access to data from the National Statistics agency (Centraal Bureau voor de
Statistiek). Secure access to the large diversity of social science data has allowed the conduct of complex
interdisciplinary studies of societal interest (such as the creation of population scale networks and high-resolution
digital twins).
Data access ODISSEI is unique in enabling longitudinal scientific studies to be linked to complex and high-dimensional
administrative data in a secure HPC environment. This includes a rich a diverse range of data including
administrative data, digital trace data, biomedical data, and high-quality longitudinal survey data. Under the CBS
Wet and GDPR, these data can be linked at the individual level and accessed by researchers under secure but
practicable conditions. ODISSEI enables access under the five safes principles via verified and audited secure
environments and supports open science and community development under these secure conditions via
bespoke and specifically developed tools. As the national statistics institution and government research agencies
were brought into the consortium, this also allowed the development of interoperability standards between
research and government data sets.

12. UK National Epitaxy Facility (NEF)
Objectives The National Epitaxy Facility’s mission is to provide services for the provision of semiconductor epitaxial
materials, structures, and devices to enable the UK’s research community to compete at the highest levels on
the world-stage in this highly active field. They support UK university research and industry development through
the provision of high-quality Semiconductor Epitaxy for custom designed structures and devices.
Organisation NEF is a consortium between the Universities of Sheffield, Cambridge and University College London and is
funded by the Engineering and Physical Sciences Research Council as part of a network of National Research
Centres. The NEF has a steering committee comprising of leading academics and industrialists from both the UK
and Europe. They meet every 6 months with the Management Team to review the performance of the facility,
provide feedback, and advise on improvement opportunities.
Achievements A broad range of novel devices are produced through research done in the Epitaxy Facility, including LEDs,
lasers, quantum circuits, transistors, advanced detectors, and solar cells.
Impact assessment NEF’s impact and success are measured not only through KPIs and risk management but through a diversity of
impact criteria. The first are based on scientific achievements and input. They keep an updated list of
publications that arise from the work on their materials, especially where their staff has been named as authors
on the paper. NEF also tries to demonstrate the impact of their work on a broader societal or economic basis.
This is part of making their case for funding to the EPSRC. NEF’s impact is also assessed regarding their
coordinating role, showing how they are facilitating and supporting the community and the health of that
community itself. The NEF also highlights their advocacy and community support activities, such as running
conferences, helping with workshops, and being involved in various scientific and advisory committees for other
facilities. They also work with institutes for training. These activities are part of their role but are not KPI-related.
