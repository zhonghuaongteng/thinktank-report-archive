About This Report
In this report, we examine how China’s techno-industrial policy has evolved under Xi Jinping into a more
centralized, security-linked, finance-driven system. We analyze how the Party-state sets industrial priorities
and mobilizes institutions, financial resources, firms, and real economy inputs to advance industrial and
technological goals.
The report’s purpose is to clarify how China’s contemporary techno-industrial system operates in prac -
tice. Our analysis focuses on institutional design, policy instruments, implementation mechanisms, and
emerging constraints, particularly tighter fiscal conditions and changing center-local dynamics. We also
examine how these domestic arrangements are projected abroad through overseas investment, industrial
parks, and standards-setting efforts.
The report is intended to provide policymakers and analysts with a broad understanding of China’s
techno-industrial policy system in the Xi era to support more informed analysis of China’s economic trajec -
tory and its implications for global economic competition and governance.
This report draws in part on a June 2025 expert workshop held jointly with the Center for Strategic and
International Studies Trustee Chair in Chinese Business and Economics. Research for the main analysis con-
cluded in fall 2025; the report reflects developments through March 2026 and incorporates selected proof-
stage updates available through June 2026.

Summary
In this report, we examine how China’s techno-industrial policy has evolved under Xi Jinping into a more
centralized, security-linked, finance-driven system. We explain how this system operates in practice by orga-
nizing Xi-era policy into five channels (fiscal, financial, real economy, Party-firm, and overseas) and 18
instruments through which the Party-state defines priorities, mobilizes institutions and financial resources,
disciplines firms and local governments, and projects these arrangements abroad. Our analysis draws on
Chinese-language policy documents and Party directives, official statistics, financial disclosures, sectoral
data, a June 2025 expert workshop, and original analysis of China National Knowledge Infrastructure aca -
demic literature. Research was conducted through fall 2025 and reflects developments through March 2026.
Under Xi, China’s techno-industrial policy has shifted from prioritizing growth and catch-up to priori -
tizing national security, technological self-reliance, and frontier leadership. The Party-state has overhauled
institutions, financing, and corporate governance to direct resources toward priority industries. China’s
techno-industrial ecosystem has produced impressive gains in technological capabilities, manufacturing
scale, and supply chain resilience in priority sectors. But the system also generates significant tensions: Cen -
tralization risks suppressing the local experimentation that fueled earlier gains, mandates and designations
spread the costs of industrial policy to firms and institutions in ways that are difficult to measure, and the
politicization of investment and corporate governance may degrade the quality of economic decisionmaking
even as it tightens alignment with state priorities. Techno-industrial policy does not resolve China’s underly-
ing demand and productivity problems that increasingly drag on aggregate growth and prosperity.
Key Findings
• Fiscal constraints are reshaping the policy toolkit. After roughly 2021–2022, tighter local budgets
r
educed room for discretionary subsidies and other outlays. Several measurable channels, such as direct
subsidies, tax breaks, and government procurement, have plateaued or declined since their peaks around
2022. The 2026 Government Work Report and the 15th Five-Year Plan (FYP) (2026–2030) signal that
Beijing intends to keep fiscal deficits roughly steady as a share of gross domestic product (GDP) while
sustaining industrial policy support by curbing wasteful local incentives.
•
 Th
e Party-state is shifting from direct spending toward mandates. The Party-state increasingly acts
as an investor and coordinator through guidance funds, targeted credit, tax incentives, procurement
mandates, and capital-market reforms. These tools operate through market channels but under tighter
political direction. The effect is to spread the costs of industrial policy across firms and institutions
rather than bear them on the public budget. Whether this amounts to greater efficiency or to a prolif -
eration of unfunded obligations is an open question. For firms, the incentive is to signal alignment with
strategic sectors and Party guidance to gain access to resources, a logic that rewards political position -
ing alongside—and sometimes over—commercial performance.
•
 Th
e “investor state” faces structural limits. Government guidance fund commitments have grown
dramatically since 2014, but corruption, short-termism, and geographic restrictions have exposed seri -
ous weaknesses in the local fund model. Beijing is centralizing oversight, but earlier reform rounds
also set ambitious goals that were unevenly implemented. Structural incentives—such as short fund
horizons, politicized investment decisions, and jurisdictional restrictions—are features of the system’s
design and are unlikely to be resolved by tightening oversight alone.
•
 Pa
rty-state corporate control has deepened. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) remain preferred instru -
ments in upstream and security-sensitive sectors, while private firms are steered through regulation,

Party embedding, minority state stakes (“golden shares”), and champion-designation programs, such as
the “Little Giants” (小巨人) and “Single Champions” (单项冠军). Beijing’s intent is to reserve upstream,
capital-intensive, and security-sensitive sectors for SOEs while delegating the fast-moving, innovation-
driven segments (artificial intelligence, electric vehicles, biotechnology, drones) to private firms, steer -

•
 Th
e external dimension is explicitly techno-industrial. The Belt and Road Initiative (BRI)—especially
its Digital Silk Road, Green BRI, standards, and industrial park components—creates external demand
for Chinese technology, locks in Chinese equipment and standards in partner markets, and aligns out -
bound investment with domestic industrial priorities.
•
 Th
e 15th FYP codifies greater discipline and deeper intervention. The published FYP outline calls
for “extraordinary measures” (超常规措施) on key core technologies, including integrated circuits, and
identifies ten “new industry tracks” (新产业新赛道) for priority cultivation. It also elevates the “unified
national market” (全国统一大市场) to a core objective, aiming to standardize rules, procurement, and
competition enforcement across provinces, curbing the duplicative local subsidy races that have fueled
overcapacity while pooling national demand.
•
 Ch
ina’s techno-industrial strategy is generating structural global friction. China’s manufacturing
trade surplus (roughly 10 percent of GDP) is comparable as a share of the domestic economy to those
of Germany, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan. But China’s economy is far larger, making the scale of its
surplus unprecedented. Depreciation of China’s real effective exchange rate since 2021, driven by com -
paratively weak domestic inflation, has compounded the competitive advantages generated by indus -
trial policy and scale. If manufacturing output continues to outpace domestic demand, the surplus must
be absorbed abroad, inviting growing resistance through tariffs, industrial policy responses, and trade
remedies.

CHAPTER 1
Introduction
China’s rapid industrial and technological ascent has spurred great interest in the mechanics of its develop -
ment model. Today, the country is the world’s dominant industrial producer and is rising in measures of
innovation and technological capacity, catching or even surpassing global leaders across some industries and
scientific domains. Yet, on measures of economic and financial efficiency, China’s trajectory is more worri -
some. The institutions of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP) and the Chinese state play changing roles in
this process. Under Xi Jinping, even as China reaches closer to the technological frontier, Party-state guid -
ance of the economy is intensifying.
China’s techno-industrial ambitions are reshaping the global economic landscape. No economy so large
and so deeply integrated into global markets has ever attempted an industrial transformation as broad,
state-guided, and security-driven as the one Beijing is now pursuing. These policies influence supply chains,
tilt standards, and increasingly set the pace of technological change. China’s push to dominate advanced
manufacturing and frontier technologies poses both a competitive challenge and a test of coordination for
the United States and its allies, forcing them to rethink how innovation, security, and industrial resilience
intersect. Understanding how China’s techno-industrial system works is essential for assessing both China’s
growth trajectory and the ways its industrial strategy will reshape the global economy, particularly as West -
ern nations pursue their own industrial policies.
This report provides a structured overview of China’s techno-industrial development strategy and its
evolution under Xi Jinping. As defined by the International Monetary Fund (IMF), industrial policy refers
to “targeted government interventions aimed at supporting specific domestic firms, industries, or narrowly
defined economic activities to achieve certain national (economic or non-economic) objectives.”
1 In China’s
case, those interventions now extend along the “technology chain,” from research and development (R&D)
and standards to commercialization and diffusion. We use techno-industrial policy to describe Beijing’s
fusion of traditional industrial policy with science and technology (S&T) policy to promote not just specific
industries but also the underlying technologies and production systems that enable them.
2 We therefore treat
industrial policy and aspects of S&T policy as an integrated policy. Given the breadth of this system, our
analysis focuses on the industrial side of the fusion. We touch on S&T where it intersects with industry, but
we do not provide a full treatment of “pure S&T” topics, such as the basic research funding system, national
lab governance, academic evaluation, or higher education administration.

A Very Brief History of China’s Industrial Policies
Since the late Qing dynasty, every generation of Chinese leadership has pursued the same core goal: restor -
ing national strength by matching and eventually surpassing the industrial and technological capabilities of
leading global powers. Methods have shifted from early state-led modernization to Mao Zedong’s mobilized
self-reliance, reform-era pragmatism to Xi’s “grand steerage,” but the underlying aim remains building a
strong, independent, and technologically advanced China. Today’s industrial policy language and concepts
reflect this enduring project.
10
China’s industrial policy has transformed in response to changes in political priorities, economic strat -
egy, and the global environment (Table 1.1). During the planned economy (1949–1976), industrialization was
central to socialist development, guided by Soviet planning. The First FYP (1953–1957) prioritized heavy
industry, aiming for an “independent and relatively complete industrial system” ( 独立的比较完整的工业
体系). The Great Leap Forward (1958–1962) disrupted this focus, mobilizing communes and small work -
shops for rapid catch-up through “self-reliance” (自力更生). After a period of retrenchment (1961–1963), the
Third Front (1964–1976) relocated factories and research institutes inland to build a defense-industrial base,
embedding strategic self-sufficiency, a concept that would later reappear as “supply-chain security.”
11
After 1978, “reform and opening” ( 改革开放) dismantled rigid central planning but retained industrial
policy as a tool of state guidance. Early reforms prioritized light industry, exports, and foreign investment. In
the 1990s, the focus shifted to “pillar industries” ( 支柱产业), such as automobiles, petrochemicals, and tele -
communications. This was supported by enterprise reform and World Trade Organization (WTO) accession.
Marketization and selective state support began to blend, setting the stage for more-targeted development.
For much of the reform era, China’s transformation was driven less by top-down industrial policy and
more by the coevolution of the market and the government. Township and village enterprises, agricultural
liberalization, foreign direct investment (FDI), housing privatization, and state enterprise reform spurred
growth, enabling China to become the world’s top exporter without a unified national industrial strategy.
12
Unlike Japan and Korea, which built sectoral targeting industrial plans onto functional markets, China
started as a command economy. Early planning efforts, such as the 1978 Ten-Year Plan, were top-down and
largely ineffective. FYPs in the 1980s and 1990s struggled to keep pace with market-driven growth. Deng
Xiaoping’s 1992 Southern Tour marked a shift toward experimental liberalization. Policymakers studied
Japan’s model but lacked the institutional capacity to replicate it. Such initiatives as the 863 and 973 Plans

Stage of Development and Economic Rebalancing
China’s strategy to move up the value chain had been worked out long before the 2010s, combining “market-
for-technology” arrangements with investments in human capital. During the 2000s, local officials courted
foreign investors, fostering production ecosystems that became “learning-by-doing” platforms for Chinese
firms. At the same time, university enrollment expanded more than fivefold after 1999, laying the ground -
work for a more skilled industrial workforce.
12
By the early 2010s, China had established itself as the world’s largest manufacturer and a global exporter. It
was approaching the technological frontier in such areas as telecommunications, high-speed rail, and renew-
able energy, while lagging in “chokepoint” technologies, such as semiconductors, jet engines, and advanced
manufacturing equipment. The catch-up model of absorbing foreign technologies and competing on low-cost
exports was delivering diminishing returns. Rising wages eroded China’s competitiveness in labor-intensive
industries, while productivity growth slowed. At the same time, global trade demand weakened following
the 2008 financial crisis. These pressures created the need for a shift toward higher-value, innovation-driven
growth, including to sustain China’s export competitiveness, which remains important for its manufacturing
sector (Figure 2.1).

The Politics of Techno-Industrial Policy:
Centralization and Institutional Design
Nothing has been more central to Xi’s tenure as General Secretary than his effort to reinvigorate, strengthen,
and direct the CCP. Xi’s project has been to coordinate China’s often fragmented political system by recentral-
izing authority in the Party center. At the heart of the project is the concept of “top-level design” (顶层设计),
a method of systemwide planning and coordination in which the Party leadership defines the architecture,
objectives, and authority relationships that guide the entire bureaucracy.
1 At the same time, much of China’s
developmental success has been rooted in decentralized local implementation. 2 Xi’s organizational ambition
is to harness that local dynamism while tightening central steering through “top-level design” (顶层设计). To
date, the results are mixed. Beijing has expanded tools for standardization and compliance, yet local incen -
tives and protectionist practices persist, and implementation gaps remain evident in such campaigns as the
“unified national market” (UNM).
Organizational restructuring represents concrete reallocations of personnel and resources (Table 3.1).
These institutional changes can offer credible signals of intent and, in broad strokes, are visible enough to
allow analysts to trace how the Party-state retools itself to exercise industrial policy.

CHAPTER 4
Sharpening the Tools of Techno-Industrial Policy
Under Xi, China’s industrial policy toolkit has grown larger, more structured, more financialized, and more
explicitly tied to security and self-reliance. Earlier approaches leaned on broad subsidies, cheap credit, and
underpriced inputs. Those instruments remain, but they have been reshaped by two forces: tighter fiscal con-
straints and Xi’s political preference for mechanisms that bind firms more closely to state priorities. Nomi -
nally consistent with the 2013 Third Plenum pledge to give markets a “decisive role,” many tools now operate
through market-like channels, such as equity investment funds, tax deductions, procurement mandates, and
capital market reforms that harness private actors. But these channels are increasingly governed by central -
ized regulatory frameworks and Party guidance rather than market signals or local discretion, raising the
question of whether market mechanisms are being empowered or instrumentalized.
1
The result is a toolkit organized around five channels: fiscal levers (subsidies, tax incentives, R&D support,
procurement, talent programs), financial levers (state equity, targeted credit, capital markets), real economy
levers (market inputs, special zones, FDI measures), Party-state corporate relations (SOE privileges, Party-
state regulation, private-sector mobilization), and overseas initiatives (BRI, financing, industrial parks, stan -
dards, outbound investment) (see Table 4.1). These channels are analytically distinct but operationally inter-
twined. Across these domains, broad, sometimes ad hoc support has given way to instruments that are more
selective and centrally influenced. For firms, the incentives are to signal alignment with strategic sectors and
Party guidance to gain access to resources.
2
Coordination of these five channels is driven less by a single industrial-policy agency than by Party-led
priority setting and planning, which are then translated into policy packages that combine fiscal measures,
financial guidance, regulation, and implementation mandates for ministries and localities. Recent institu -
tional reforms have explicitly strengthened centralized leadership over S&T policy and Party oversight of the
financial system, reinforcing Beijing’s ability to align innovation, finance, and industrial objectives.
3
In practice, coordination is strongest at the level of agenda-setting and mobilization and weaker at the
level of execution. Instruments often stack, with the same sector supported through multiple channels, which
can accelerate scaling but lead to inconsistent incentives or center-local slippage. Leadership increasingly
acknowledges this coordination problem and calls for tighter cross-policy alignment, including stronger
interdepartmental communication and mechanisms that use limited fiscal funds to mobilize more financial
resources.

Xi-era fiscal policy has leaned on tax relief to support favored industries. The authorities have rolled out
large-scale value-added tax (V AT) cuts and refunds, preferential corporate income tax rates, and sector-
specific exemptions that lower costs in priority industries.
15 The 2019 V AT overhaul cut the headline manu-

on new-energy vehicle purchase taxes.

corporate income tax rate and firms’ effective rates, multiplied by pretax profits.

Mobilizing Finance for Techno-Industrial Policy
In the Xi era, Beijing has sought to use the state-owned financial sector and state-linked investors as the
dominant channels of state support for industries. As tighter fiscal conditions and central efforts to curb dis-
cretionary local support narrowed the scope for cash subsidies and ad hoc incentives (such as tax rebates and
preferential land and utility deals), policymakers sought mechanisms to discipline capital allocation, sharpen
incentives, and sustain support for priority sectors without expanding public budgets. This shift marks the
emergence of an “investor state,” in which the Party-state acts less as a direct spender and more as a capital
allocator using banks, funds, and markets to pursue strategic outcomes.
1
As Vice Minister of Science and Technology Qiu Yong observed in May 2025, “The emphasis is on shift -
ing from a ‘fiscal mindset’ to a ‘financial mindset.’” 2 In practice, that means leveraging credit, equity, and
bond markets. Finance is a way to direct resources toward priorities through channels that carry market-like
features but remain subject to political direction.
Raising the (Equity) Stakes: China’s Investor State
Under Xi, the Party-state has evolved into an “investor state.” Initiated with the 2013 Third Plenum’s directive
to “manage capital,” Beijing seeks to direct resources into strategic sectors via state-backed guidance funds,
equity market interventions, and “golden share” stakes in private firms. This approach embeds political over-
sight into corporate governance, positioning the Party-state as fund sponsor, holding company, market sta -
bilizer, shareholder, and overseer.
The guidance fund model became the central innovation. Guidance funds are state-backed investment
vehicles in which governments provide seed capital and set priorities but delegate day-to-day management to
professional fund managers, often alongside private co-investors. The number of government guidance funds
surged from fewer than 100 in 2014 to more than 2,100 by 2024.

Even without outright fraud, guidance funds often proved too risk-averse to serve as patient capital. Stud-
ies show that more than half of local funds used “valuation adjustment mechanisms” that required start-ups
to buy back equity if revenue or IPO targets were missed, effectively turning equity into disguised loans.
Typical fund horizons were only six to seven years, far shorter than the 12–15 years many hard technology
firms require. Local officials, judged on short-term performance, had little incentive to tolerate failures or
wait for long-term payoffs.
Structural constraints compounded these issues. Local guidance funds are contractually required to invest
within their own jurisdictions, often channeling money into weak local firms rather than the best national
candidates.

Fiscal stress further eroded the model. Guidance funds relied heavily on land-sale revenues and LGFVs,

Integrating the State and the Market: The Party-
State and Its Firms
Beijing has tried to integrate state and private enterprises into a coordinated corporate system to advance its
techno-industrial strategy. SOEs remain the backbone of heavy industry, infrastructure, and “commanding
heights” sectors that secure national capacity, while private firms drive technological innovation in com -
petitive and fast-moving fields. Through political supervision, regulatory control, and selective incentives,
Beijing has embedded coordination across both. The result is a managed hierarchy in which market actors
operate within Party-defined boundaries to serve national technological and strategic objectives.
SOEs: From Commanding Heights to New Frontiers
Since Xi became leader in 2012, SOEs have been further integrated into China’s industrial strategy. The
CCP increased political oversight through anticorruption efforts and the “two-way entry, cross appoint -
ment” (双 向 进 入 、交 叉 任 职) system, making Party secretaries also serve as board chairs.

Disciplining, Steering, and Reassuring the Private Sector
Beijing employs a three-pronged approach to manage private firms: disciplining through Party-state involve-
ment and regulatory controls, steering by elevating selected champion firms, and reassuring through policies
and laws that promise fair treatment. The goal is to harness private-sector dynamism within boundaries that

Projecting Techno-Industrial Policy Abroad
China has extended its techno-industrial policy beyond its borders, using such initiatives as the BRI to blend
financial support, overseas industrial parks, digital and green infrastructure, and standards diplomacy. This
comprehensive approach advances China’s industrial and technological influence while aligning foreign
investments with its own economic goals.
Techno-Industrial Policy Under the Belt and Road Umbrella
The BRI is Xi’s flagship framework for China’s economic diplomacy. Launched in 2013, it was designed to
promote infrastructure connectivity, trade, and investment with China’s partners. Although the BRI is not a
formal industrial policy (it does not set sectoral targets or allocate subsidies), it has functioned as an indirect
industrial policy by creating overseas demand for Chinese industries, such as construction, steel, rail equip -
ment, and power generation.
Initially, Chinese policy banks funded major overseas construction projects, helping to absorb surplus
capacity in heavy industries, even though China’s domestic demand for those industrial goods dwarfed the
scale of overseas BRI projects. Since 2019, however, new projects have slowed as Beijing has emphasized
“high-quality” cooperation, and the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted economic cooperation.

Reordering Priorities: Technology Leadership, Security, and Ideology
Throughout most of China’s reform era, the main goal was catching up with advanced economies. This
meant building up manufacturing capacity, integrating into global value chains, and absorbing foreign tech -
nology, fueled by FDI.1 Industrial policy reemerged after the 2006 MLP and the 2010 SEI lists. 2 Still, China’s
focus remained on promoting specific sectors and export competitiveness, with “indigenous innovation”
(自主创新) mostly about adopting and localizing existing technologies rather than leading at the global
frontier.
Under Xi, China’s techno-industrial strategy has shifted in three main ways:
•
 In
novation and upgrading: China aims to move beyond low-cost, catch-up growth to innovation-
driven, higher-value production and global commercial leadership. As wages rise and productivity
slows, sustaining growth requires reaching the technological frontier before becoming a high-income
economy.
•
 Na
tional security: Security concerns have made technological self-reliance and supply chain resilience
central state priorities. Since 2017, U.S. export controls, sanctions, and tariffs have pushed China to ele-
vate technology self-sufficiency, now viewed as essential for development. Our policy discourse analysis
ranks self-reliance and technology leadership first, followed by structural upgrading, decarbonization,
and regional balancing.
•
 Id
eology and politics: Xi has recast techno-industrial policy as a Party-led mission, linking economic
modernization with national rejuvenation and “common prosperity” ( 共同富裕). Technological prog-
ress is tied to the Party’s vision of governance and China’s ambition for great-power status.
Building a Party-Led Techno-Industrial Policy State
Xi’s approach aims to centralize techno-industrial policy as a Party instrument, prioritizing “top-level
design” (顶层设计) and greater central government control. The main goals are to accelerate technological
development for national power, address the downsides of fragmented local production while preserving
adaptability, align financial and industrial resources with security goals, and make the most of limited fiscal
capacity through coordination and centralized direction.

Unified National Market and Anti-Involution
China’s leadership assesses that the country’s fragmented industrial policy landscape has become a hin -
drance to development. The UNM is a core objective of the 15th FYP, with implications for techno-industrial
policy.8 It aims to streamline implementation, reduce local barriers and local protectionism, and improve
factor allocation by standardizing rules, supervision, and competition enforcement. 9 However, the same
standardization also expands Beijing’s ability to discipline local investment promotion and curb duplicative
projects in strategic industries, so the UNM can serve as both a market-integration mechanism and a tool for
more centralized industrial coordination. Introduced in 2022 and operationalized in January 2025, the UNM
is guided by NDRC principles emphasizing “five unifications, one removal” ( 五 统 一 、一 破 除): Unify basic
market rules and institutions, unify factor and resource markets, unify goods and services markets, unify
high-standard connectivity for market facilities, unify fair and consistent market supervision, and remove
local protectionism and market segmentation.
10
For industrial policy, the UNM is meant to serve as a coordination tool. By defining standards and stan -
dardizing procurement and competition enforcement, Beijing hopes to curb duplicative provincial projects,
pool national demand, and create economies of scale without larger subsidies. The UNM is designed to
reduce interprovincial competition in strategic industries, lower fiscal costs, and facilitate prioritization and
orderly consolidation, including mergers or exits of duplicative or nonviable projects.

Policy selection bias is substantial. Beijing often intensifies support in sectors already showing momentum,
so observed success may reflect reinforcement rather than creation. Empirical evaluation is further con -
strained by data opacity, particularly for unlisted firms and local governments, which limits firm-level or
counterfactual analysis. China’s techno-industrial gains are themselves multidimensional, spanning com -
mercial scaling, frontier research, system integration, and manufacturing execution, with each dimension
responding differently to policy tools, complicating aggregate judgments.
A full assessment of effectiveness is beyond the scope of this analysis, which focuses on how the system
operates rather than how well it works. But the system as described here contains a core tension that any such
assessment would need to confront: The same centralization and mandate-driven approach that Beijing is
adopting to address the acknowledged failures of the earlier, more fragmented model may also suppress the
competitive dynamics and local adaptation that generated many of the gains documented above.
In this sense, much of what Beijing now labels waste (duplicative local investment, subsidy races, excess
entry, price wars) may be better understood as the cost side of a model whose benefits the leadership is keen
to preserve. Decentralized competition generated redundancy, but it also generated the speed, scale, and cost
compression that made Chinese firms globally competitive in EVs, solar equipment, and batteries. The policy
challenge is not simply to eliminate waste but to do so without also eliminating the competitive intensity that
produced the gains. That may prove to be a harder separation than Beijing’s current reforms assume.
Techno-Industrial Might, Global Tensions
China’s ambitions in techno-industrial policy are unprecedented. While Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan
followed similar state-led paths to industrialization and scaled back intervention as they reached the tech -
nological frontier, China is intensifying its efforts at an unmatched scale. If Beijing seeks analogies, it finds
them in manufacturing-oriented economies, such as Germany, Japan, South Korea, and Taiwan, all of which
maintain manufacturing trade surpluses and rely on industrial exports to remain competitive. China’s man -
ufacturing surplus (about 10 percent of GDP) is comparable to that of its peers (Figure 10.1). However, China’s
economy is larger than the combined economies of these countries, and its manufacturing trade surplus as a
share of global GDP exceeds that of any of them (Figure 10.2). The world’s production and demand balances
now hinge on Beijing’s choices more than those of any other economy.
However, China’s ability to keep expanding manufacturing output is ultimately limited by demand, not
by techno-industrial policy. China’s policies remain powerful in mobilizing resources and sustaining priority

For China’s trading partners, the available direct responses broadly fall into two categories. The first is
targeted trade remedies, such as tariffs, countervailing duties, and investment screening. 28 These can offset
specific competitive distortions but are blunt instruments that also raise costs for importers and risk frag -

The second channel is the exchange rate. China’s real effective exchange rate (a measure of the price
of Chinese goods and services relative to those of its trading partners, adjusted for inflation differentials)
depreciated roughly 13 percent from 2022 to 2025, driven primarily by low domestic inflation relative to
trading partners, combined with Beijing’s preference for bilateral exchange rate stability against the U.S.
dollar (Figure 10.3). This depreciation has compounded the competitive advantages of Chinese exporters,
generated by industrial policy, economies of scale, and supply chain integration. The IMF’s 2025 Article IV
assessment, published in February 2026, found China’s external position “stronger than implied by funda -
mentals and desired policies” and estimated the yuan’s real effective exchange rate to be undervalued by 12
to 21 percent. The IMF recommended a package of consumption-oriented fiscal expansion (partially funded
by cutting industrial policy spending), higher social spending, and greater exchange-rate flexibility that, if

At the macroeconomic level, a real appreciation of the renminbi is the most direct path to easing the
trade imbalances that China’s techno-industrial policies amplify. A stronger currency would reduce the price
advantage of Chinese exports, partially allay international concerns about industrial policy subsidies and
overcapacity, and help shift domestic incentives toward consumption and services. To deny that the exchange
rate matters for China’s manufacturing export competitiveness is akin to denying that prices matter. China
would retain substantial competitive advantages and would continue to pursue the industrial policies docu -
mented in this report. But a revalued currency would mean that China’s producers (rather than the rest of the
world) absorb more of the adjustment costs when domestic demand fails to keep pace with capacity. Without
it, Beijing is in effect asking trading partners to accommodate a structural surplus driven in part by policy
choices that depress the real exchange rate.
Whether revaluation occurs through Beijing’s own policy shift or through external pressure is a central
open question. The IMF’s recommended package would achieve it as a byproduct of domestic rebalancing.
But as noted, Beijing shows no inclination to pursue that path in the near term. Trading partners can press
the issue through multilateral channels or bilateral negotiations or turn to the blunter tools of tariffs and
countervailing duties. None of these external instruments can compel Beijing to adjust course, but they can
raise the cost to China of the status quo.

was “our currency, but your problem.”
32 He was describing a world in which one country’s domestic policy
choices imposed adjustment costs on everyone else. Today, although the United States still anchors global
finance, China occupies an analogous position in global manufacturing. Beijing’s techno-industrial policy is
designed for national strength, self-reliance, and development. But the combination of ambitious industrial
policy atop a weak currency produces a structural surplus that the rest of the world must either absorb, resist,
or negotiate away. Like Connally’s dollar, China’s techno-industrial might is Beijing’s strategy, but, increas -
ingly, the world’s problem.

Challenge-based leadership (

defined technical problem and the winner is tasked with delivering results. The phrase evokes publicly list -
ing the challenge and appointing a lead commander. It reflects a preference for mobilizing targeted break -
throughs rather than simply distributing research funds.
Four only (破四唯破四唯). Literally breaking the four onlys, meaning overreliance on papers, titles, diplomas,
and awards in talent and research evaluation. The slogan is an internal reform critique. It signals dissatis -
faction with credentialism and a desire to judge scientists and engineers more by contribution and mission
performance.
World-important talent center and strategic talent forces (
世界重要人才中心世界重要人才中心, 战略人才力量战略人才力量). These
terms frame talent as a national strategic resource, not just a labor-market issue. The first sets an ambition to
make China a globally important hub for high-level talent. The second emphasizes building politically reli -
able, mission-oriented pools of scientists, engineers, and managers for priority sectors.
Hukou (
户口户口). China’s household registration system. In this report, it matters because local governments
use hukou-related benefits (such as access to schooling, housing, and public services) to attract high-level
talent. The term shows how industrial policy in China often depends on both administrative and financial
incentives.
Fault-tolerance mechanism (
容错机制容错机制). A governance term for protecting officials or fund managers
from punishment when policy-aligned risk-taking fails despite following procedure. The goal is to reduce
excessive caution in state-guided investment. It reflects Beijing’s recognition that anticorruption and audit
pressure can suppress experimentation.

require overcoming substantial engineering or scientific barriers, such as semiconductors, advanced materi -
