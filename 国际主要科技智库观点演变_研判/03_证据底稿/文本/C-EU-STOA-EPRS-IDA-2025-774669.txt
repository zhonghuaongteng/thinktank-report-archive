Fusion energy: A
paradigm shift in

for Europe?

Fusion, regarded by many as the ultimate form of clean energy, may
finally be within reach. This analysis looks at the context of the latest
advances, in particular the explosion of private -sector interest.
Overall, this has led to an acceleration of many planned timelines,
although a lot of uncertainty remains, given the magnitude of the
remaining technical challenges. In addition, questions also persist
around skills, the regulatory environment, financing and economic
viability. With a new EU strategy expected in late 2025, this analysis
concludes with an overview of current policy, and discusses some of
the challenges the EU faces  in setting a pathway towards the
commercialisation of fusion energy.

Executive summary
Fusion has long been the holy grail of energy research. It is deemed a safe, clean, dispatchable
(available on demand) and virtually limitless source of power, with widely abundant fuel, which does
not share the main risks of nuclear fission. As such, it could play an important role as a form of clean
power in a future energy mix  in the European Union (EU) , supporting the decarbonisation and
electrification of the economy, while strengthening European energy autonomy.
The EU has historically been a strong backer of fusion research, reflected in its support for the Joint
European Torus  (JET) and International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor ( ITER), the
multinational experimental reactor under construction in France. In the conventional EUROfusion
roadmap, the DEMO pilot plant should follow in the 2050s . However, over the last few years this
scenario has been disrupted by the emergence of private start -ups, mostly in the U nited States,
promising to deliver electricity a s early as the mid -2030s, backed by substantial investment and
pursuing a multitude of approaches. Some public plans are also adapting to this more ambitious
timeline, with , for example,  Germany, the U nited Kingdom, the US and Japan all aiming for an
operational pilot plant by 2040.
Despite this optimism, significant uncertainty remains, particularly around plasma control, the fuel
cycle and materials, and fusion in general is commonly seen as being at low technology readiness
level. In addition, there are challenges related to regulation, availability and training of skilled labour,
as well as financing. The sustainability of the ITER supply chain will also depend on future fusion
initiatives. Furthermore, the economic case for fusion is still unclear as costs are not yet fully known.
The European Commission has committed to presenting a fusion strategy in 2025. Besides clarifying
the pathway towards commercialisation, it is widely expected to present a proposal for future
public-private partnerships to coordinate efforts between the public and private sectors, including
support for fusion start- ups and revised forms of governance, as well as the future approach to
regulation. It will likely also  present a revised fusion roadmap, possibly with more ambitious goals,
similarly to Japan's recent plans. Finally, the strategy may also include proposals on the approach to
international collaboration and intellectual property  (IP) ownership, as well as plans to sustain the
fusion supply chain and ensure availability of skills.

1. Introduction
Fusion powers the sun and the stars. If replicated in a controlled fashion on Earth, fusion is a potential
source of clean energy, which can significantly decarbonise the economy and improve energy
security. Unlike nuclear fission (the technology used in conventional nuclear power plants and small
modular reactors), which involves splitting heavy atoms to release energy, fusion combines light
atoms to form a heavier one, releasing vast amounts of energy in the process. This fundamental
difference makes fusion a more attractive option, as it does not produce long-lived radioactive waste
or present the risk of nuclear disasters like Fukushima or Chernobyl.
The case for fusion is compelling, and multilateral international research collaboration has been
ongoing for several decades, as exemplified by the Joint European Torus (JET), and more recently
the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor ( ITER). However, achieving a sustained and
energy-positive fusion reaction has proved difficult, with unsolved technical challenges and high
investment costs. Nevertheless, the potential benefits make it an attractive option for future energy
systems, in particular from an energy security and strategic autonomy point of view. Firstly, fusion
has the potential to be a nearly limitless source of energy because the fuel used in fusion reactions
is widely available. Secondly, f usion is a low -carbon energy source, producing no greenhouse gas
emissions. Thirdly, fusion energy has the potential to be a reliable source of energy, making it a
potentially interesting complement to intermittent renewable energy sources like solar and w ind
power.
Achieving commercial fusion energy will require significant technical and engineering advances and
investment. The scale of the challenge is encapsulated in the ambition  to build a machine that
contains a plasma ten times hotter than the sun, while magnets colder than outer space sit just
outside. Public and private organisations in the EU, US, China and elsewhere are working on
addressing these issues and testing different fusion energy technologies.
Fusion has long been the subject of a joke that it was '30 years away and would always be'. However,
the field has undergone significant changes in the past few years. Technological advances, for
example in artificial intelligence (AI) and high-performance computing, or the development of high-
temperature superconductors, are enabling important progress  in reactor design and plasma
control. In turn, this raises the possibility of faster and  cheaper pathways towards commercial
reactors, having as consequence a recent explosion of interest in fusion from the private sector, with
multiple start- ups appearing worldwide, exploring different pathways for fusion and raising
significant sums.

2. Fusion basics
To think about fusion, you can start with a simple question: what holds an atomic nucleus together?
Two forces are at play: protons, which are charged, repel each other through the electromagnetic
force, but the strong nuclear force binds neutrons and protons together. When light nuclei fuse, the
strong nuclear force means the resultant nucleus is more tightly bound , with a smaller mass, and
energy is therefore released in the process – according to Einstein's famous equation. However, this
strong nuclear force has a very short range, and therefore to achieve fusion it is necessary to first
overcome the electromagnetic repulsion and bring the nuclei close enough, strongly enough, and
for a sufficiently long time. This is encapsulated by the Lawson criterion , which looks at fusion
conditions through a triple product of density, temperature and confinement time, and which can
be related to the threshold necessary to achieve sustainable fusion reactions.
2 Lacking the sun 's
immense gravity, fusion reactors rely on very high temperatures to achieve fusion. At these
temperatures, matter is in a state of plasma and, consequently, electrically charged. This has very
important consequences, as it means it can be contained using magnetic fields but can also be
subject to coordinated motions over large scales. Plasma instabilities and the difficulty of controlling
plasmas at these very high temperatures have been among the biggest barriers to the development
of fusion technologies.
2.1. Fuels
The choice of fuel mix for achieving fusion has significant implications. This choice has several
important impacts at the level of reactor design, including the  required temperature, neutron
production and fuel availability. The following fuels have all been proposed by at least one of the
public or private fusion developers:
• Deuterium-tritium: this is the fuel mix most widely chosen, as it achieves the highest
reaction rates at lowest temperature and therefore presents lowest technical barriers.
Deuterium and tritium are both heavier isotopes of hydrogen, but while deuterium is
abundant in seawater, tritium does not occur in nature as it decays in around 12 years
and must be bred in the reactor via fission of lithium, taking advantage of the neutrons
produced in the fusion reaction. These energetic neutrons also require special care to
be paid to  shielding and remote maintenance of the reactor. This also leaves some
reactor parts radioactive, posing a challenge for decommissioning, although unlike
with nuclear fission reactors the radioactivity is not long-lived.
• Deuterium-deuterium: as a fuel choice, deuterium presents two key advantages over
a deuterium-tritium mix; it avoids the problem of tritium breeding, and the reaction
produces less energetic neutrons, which are therefore easier to handle. However, it
requires a higher temperature to reach similar reaction rates. Research reactors often
run on this fuel, since many do not have energy break-even as a primary goal.
• Deuterium-Helium-3: This mix produces fewer neutrons and allows for direct
electricity production through charged particles but requires yet again a higher
temperature. Helium-3 is rare on Earth, but exists in abundance on the moon, so this
mix may be better suited to space-based reactors.
• Proton-Boron-11: the key advantage of this mix is the fusion reaction produces
charged particles but no neutrons, and therefore direct electricity production is
possible while requiring minimal shielding and producing little to no waste. It faces

2.2. Reactor design
Equally, different approaches can be taken to designing reactors when it comes to creating the
necessary conditions for fusion and controlling the resulting plasma. They can essentially be divided
into two broad categories: magnetic confinement (MCF) and inertial confinement (ICF):
• In magnetic confinement, the plasma is usually kept inside a toroidal-shaped chamber
(although it can also be spherical) using magnetic fields. In general, MCF devices
suffer from issues of plasma instability, which can cause significant heat discharge
onto the reactor walls, leading to energy loss in the plasma , thereby affecting fusion
efficiency. This, combined with neutron bombardment in the fuel mixes most often
used, leads to degradation and loss of structural integrity in the inner walls, posing
considerable technical and maintenance issues. Furthermore, for efficient operation
very powerful magnetic fields are required, necessitating expensive superconductor
magnets that must be cooled cryogenically. Despite these important technical
barriers, MCF ha s been the most widely pursued approach, given it presents the
clearest route to steady -state operation, with for example tokamak -type devices
being used in public research for several decades. More recently, an alternative to the
simpler doughnut-shaped tokamak has emerged – the stellarator, which uses a more
complex twisted structure. While the latter is more challenging to design, requiring
advanced computer simulations, in operation it offers better and easier plasma
stability.
• Unlike magnetic confinement, which seeks to operate the plasma in a steady state,
inertial confinement works in short pulses, with inertial forces temporarily ensuring
the necessary confinement for fusion to occur. The most typical route is to use lasers
to implode a small fuel pellet –  this is the approach used at the National Ignition
Facility (NIF) in the US, which holds the current record in energy gain from fusion. The
two other pathways being explored involve a compression either through magnetic
forces via strong electrical currents, or through some kind of mechanical force. In
terms of power production, the key challenge of ICF is its pulsed nature, in the sense
that a high pulse frequency would be required, which currently remains theoretical .
There are also technical barriers in the complexity of the symmetry required, as well
as the progressive degradati on of the compression mechanisms, given the high
stresses involved.
There is in addition a third category, which forms something of a hybrid, usually called magneto -
inertial (MIF) approach, which  follows a similar concept to magnetic confinement, combined with
magnetic compression of the plasma. Field -reversed configurations (FRC), where part of the
necessary magnetic fields is formed by currents within the plasma itself, often feature in MIF
designs, although they can also use magnetic confinement  alone. The main advantage of this
category is that it achieves higher densit ies than in MCF, opening the door to the use of different
fuel mixes, while having a simpler geometry as well as avoiding some of the complexity of ICF. This
is the least advanced area of research, mostly being pursued by private actors, and faces significant
barriers in terms of plasma stability and control.
2.3. Net energy gain
An important concept in fusion energy debate, and which requires some care, is the idea of net
energy gain, usually expressed by the letter Q. However, this simple concept can be deceiving, as

when it comes to fusion, energy in and energy out can be difficult to define: considerable energy is
needed to heat the plasma and achieve fusion conditions, much of it might be wasted, and also when
used for other purposes such as cryogenics for superconducting magnets . E qually, there will be
efficiency losses on the output side as most fusion reactions produce heat, which must then be
turned into electricity. Most discussions of Q ignore these factors, and look only at the ' scientific
ratio' between the energy released in the fusion reactions and that used to heat the plasma. So, for
example when the NIF announced it had achieved the first net (scientific) energy gain in late 2022,
with a Q greater than one, this accounted only for the energy actually reaching the target fuel, with
the electricity needed to power the experiment being a couple of orders of magnitude greater. While
achieving a Q of 1 remains a significant scientific milestone, actual commercially viable energy gain
likely requires a Q approaching 20 to 30.3
3. The path to fusion
Developing fusion energy technology is a complex and challenging process requiring significant
investment. Several public and private initiatives are underway to demonstrate the feasibility of
fusion energy and pave the way for its commercialisation. However, stakeholder projections appear
to put the timeline for commercial fusion anywhere between 10 years and several decades away.

3.1. Current outlook
Originally planned as, and to this day, a key project in the development of fusion energy, ITER is an
international collaborative project (EU, China, Japan, India, the Republic of Korea, the Russian
Federation and the USA). The project  aims at demonstrating the scientific and technological
feasibility of fusion energy to achieve a plasma state that can sustain a fusion reaction for a
significant amount of time. ITER is currently under construction in Cadarache, Saint Paul- lez-
Durance, southern France, with research operations planned to start in 2034, and first deuterium -
tritium plasma operations scheduled for 2039. In addition to ITER, several other public experiments
are underway around the world, including the National Ignition Facility (NIF) in the US, the joint
EU/Japan JT -60SA in Japan, the Experimental Advanced Superconducting Tokamak (EAST) in
China, and the Wendelstein 7 -X (W7 -X) in Germany. These experiments aim to advance fusion

3.2. Scaling fusion energy
As previously presented, the timelines for commercial fusion energy differ significantly. National and
international bodies, as well as private companies, offer contrasting forecasts for prototypes, grid-
connected pilot demonstration plants, and large-scale commercialisation.
Public initiatives, including the EUROfusion roadmap, largely aligned with ITER 's scientific
milestones for pilot plants such as DEMO expected  post-2050. However, this picture seems to be
changing. The proposal recently released by the European Commission for the new Horizon Europe
2028-2034 programme includes , as an example of a possible ' moonshot' project, achieving
electricity production from fusion by 2034. 15 The US aims to have a pilot fusion plant for the 2035 -
2040 timeframe, and commercial deployment from 2040. The UK 's STEP program me plans a
prototype by 2040. China, Korea, and Japan  aim for pilot plants by 2040, with commercialisation
from 2050. Companies like Helion, Commonwealth Fusion, and Type One Energy plan
demonstration plants before 2030 and pilot plants in the early to mid-2030s.
The public sector is generally more cautious regarding timelines. Private companies promise faster
progress to secure investor confidence, but face technical and cost uncertainties. However, if the
fusion energy industry achieves early breakthroughs this could sideline some of the public initiatives,
such as the DEMO project, the next step after ITER.
A critical point when trying to estimate the scaling up of fusion energy is that multiple technologies
are being explored simultaneously (including tokamaks, stellarators, inertial confinement fusion, and
magneto-inertial fusion, among others). While as a consequence this maximises the chances of a
breakthrough, the diversity of approaches being pursued also creates uncertainty about which
technology will ultimately prevail, making it challenging to predict the timeline and the future cost
of electricity produced in fusion plants.
The European Commission's 'Foresight study on the worldwide developments in advancing fusion
energy' presents four scenarios that illustrate the uncertainty surrounding the development of
fusion energy, and help make sense of the possible pathways for the scaling up of fusion energy:
• EUROfusion r oadmap ( base case): Fusion energy development advances under
public leadership, with ITER and DEMO progressing cautiously. Net energy gain is
achieved in the 2040s and grid-connected pilots arrive in the 2050s, with commercial
plants following in the 2060s.
• Collaboration scenario: Public and private sectors cooperate  and e arly
breakthroughs from start- ups help accelerate timelines, while public -private
partnerships ensure safety and societal benefit. Fusion energy commercialisation
starts by 2050.

4. Key challenges
Despite the dynamism and optimistic outlook currently prevalent in much of the fusion sector,
significant challenges remain on the road to commercial fusion energy. Importantly, many of these
are technical in nature, with several key enabling technologies still at low levels of technological
development. While public projects looking into solutions to these are ongoing, their timelines do
not always fit with many of the private players' roadmaps. Beyond the technical validation of fusion
energy, additional challenges will also need to be overcome to deploy fusion as a commercially viable
electricity source. The key challenges are summarised below.
4.1. Technical performance
Several important technical obstacles remain, given there have been few opportunities to validate
proposed solutions in practice. These range from the behaviour of burning plasma (where most of
the heating comes from the fusion process) , to key enabling technologies linked to materials and
the fuel cycle:
• Plasma control: one of the main challenges in the history of fusion has been plasma
control, given that plasma is prone to instabilities and turbulence. This is a particular
problem for MCF, where maintaining the plasma in a steady state under confinement
for lengthy periods is a critical step towards efficient power production. Furthermore,
the burning plasma regime has yet to be properly studied experimentally. This is an
area where the use of AI promises to have an impact, as might advanced reactor types
like the stellarator.
16
• Blanket: the inside blanket of a MCF reactor is in a harsh environment, with the inner
walls exposed to significant thermal and neutron radiation. On top of this, the blanket
will also be responsible for neutron shielding, tritium breeding and heat extract ion,
while requiring accessible maintenance and ease of replacement. Blankets are a key
component of future reactors, and research into the best and most resilient materials
is an ongoing task. In the EU, the IFMIF -DONES facility is being built specificall y to
conduct research into such materials but is only scheduled to be fully online in 2035.

4.2. Hardware challenges
Magnets (in MCF) and lasers and targets (in ICF) are the key hardware components of future fusion
reactors. There have been considerable developments in magnet technology recent ly, with private
companies such as CFS pushing research into the use of high -temperature superconducting
magnets for fusion – for a given size, these allow for significantly stronger magnetic fields than the
traditional superconducting magnets being used at ITER. This is part of the reason why the reactor
currently under construction by CFS is aiming to achieve net energy gain, while being considerably
smaller than ITER. However, there have also been claims  that there may be significant structural
issues with more compact reactors using high-temperature superconducting magnets.20
With net energy gain demonstrated at the NIF, the scaling up of ICF approaches would involve two
main areas. Despite the net energy gain experiments, laser energy efficiency is very low at the NIF,
with the lasers only depositing a few percent of the original electrical energy used to fire them onto
the target. While laser technology is progressing rapidly thanks to broader applications, the required
improvement of about an order of magnitude still poses a considerable challenge. The other aspect
requiring significant progress is linked to shot frequency and target construction: as NIF is an
experimental facility, it can do two or three shots per day, but a commercial power plant would
involve at least a cadence of around a shot per minute. This would not only require considerable
development into target handling and laser cooling, but also in construction , to considerably lower
target costs.

4.4. Construction challenges and skills
Although already relevant in some cases, especially in terms of possible skills shortages, challenges
related to construction and skills availability will come into their own for the deployment of
commercial plants. These are related to the build-up of the supply chain, including possible concerns
about critical materials, and the availability and retention of skilled staff. Although not immediately
problematic, some of these aspects (such as having a pipeline of adequately trained, PhD -level
plasma physicists) require long lead times, so proper planning must start well in advance. In addition,
as shown by the experience of ITER, plants are incredibly complex pieces of advanced engineering,
the construction of which presents its own set of challenges. Ensuring adequate build -up of
experience and sharing of lessons learned will be an important part of any scale-up.
4.5. Regulatory environment
No EU legal framework specifically addresses  fusion energy. The EU and national nuclear safety
rules, under Euratom, were designed for fission reactors and do not fully account for fusion energy's
unique characteristics.21 Fusion reactors have no self-sustaining chain reaction or meltdown risk and
create no high -level long -lived waste .22 Some national authorities opt to adapt fission -based or
radiation protection laws to fusion projects. For example, the ITER facility in France was licensed
under the existing nuclear legal framework for basic nuclear installations ( Installation Nucléaire de
Base – INB). Others, such as the German Wendelstein 7 -X fusion experiment, are licensed under
radiation protection law ( Strahlenschutzgesetz), similar to licensing for particle accelerators. The
European Commission study on the applicability of the regulatory framework for nuclear facilities to
fusion facilities, recommended  coordinated EU action on establishing a fusion-specific legal and
regulatory framework, build ing regulatory capacity, and harmonis ing safety requirements acros s
Member States.
4.6. Funding and economic viability
As a complex technology at the cutting edge of current possib ilities, fusion requires considerable
investment. For example, ITER will cost in excess of €30 billion, while private demonstrators are
often estimated to come in at around €1 billion, with later commercial reactors possibly costing
multiples of that  figure. Although the success of future fusion developments  may affect overall
funding levels, and how they impact perceptions of the viability of the technology, public and private
initiatives face different challenges where funding is concerned. While funding for ITER and other
current experiments is relatively secure, funding for projects such as DEMO will be much more
politically exposed to future developments. This creates the risk  of a situation in which public
funding is cut because the private sector, having reached some important milestones first, is
perceived to be ahead, but nevertheless remains dependent on continued research on critical

5. Policy considerations
Fusion energy has been a strategic priority for the EU 's long-term decarbonisation and its energy
security. The Euratom Treaty and EU research and innovation  framework programmes have
contributed to building a research ecosystem around fusion energy.
The Draghi report states that nuclear fusion is a disruptive technology that holds the potential to
revolutionise the energy landscape in the second half of this century.23 The report also mentions that
the ITER project has propelled the EU to the forefront of global fusion research and calls for a stable
and predictable fusion ecosystem for industrial innovation  to be created that leverages the ITER
project while ensuring a clear technology development roadmap.

5.2. EU fusion policy issues
From the studies mentioned above and the stakeholders' dialogue conducted in the 'Foresight study
on the worldwide developments in advancing fusion energy', several key EU fusion policy issues can
be identified. These issues also emerged in recent workshops on fusion energy organised by
Members of the European Parliament.
Strategic direction and technology diversity
• The EU lacks a comprehensive fusion energy strategy. The lack of a comprehensive
strategy coordinating public research efforts with emerging private- sector fusion
initiatives may delay knowledge transfer, infrastructure sharing, and joint innovation
across the ecosystem. The European Commission is preparing a fusion strategy,
expected in Q4 2025, which should address this gap through clearer public -private
co-governance and resource alignment. A public call for evidence on this strategy
took place in June 2025.30
• Fusion R&D is overly concentrated on a single technological pathway.  There is
increasing optimism and a growing anticipation among researchers and engineers that
one or more fusion approaches will succeed by 2040–2050, though it is unclear which
one (e.g. tokamaks, stellarators, or inertial fusion).
31 So far, EU public funding has
mainly focused on supporting tokamak -based fusion, while private initiatives in the
EU are working on a range of other technologies. The focus on a single approach could
limit impact, if alternative concepts prove more viable or faster to deploy.
Additionally, the lack of overlap between public and private initiatives may limit
possibilities for developing synergies.
• Fusion is absent from key EU energy and climate long-term planning tools. Several
fusion energy pilot projects could become operational before 2050. Similar to other
emerging technologies that could have a significant post -2050 impact (e.g. small -
modular reactors, direct air capture and carbon capture and storage) , fusion can
potentially contribute to reduc ing greenhouse gas  emissions, the expected strong
electrification of the global economy and to addressing future increased demand for
electricity from new technologies such as AI.
Governance, regulation, and public-private partnerships
• Limited coordination between public and private stakeholders. EU public and
private fusion stakeholders do not appear to be engaging efficiently. While the public
programmes (e.g. ITER and EUROfusion) take a long -term approach, private
companies appear to be fast paced, but both aim at developing cost -effective and
reliable fusion power. Fragmentation also affects the private sector, with the supply
chain and start-ups having different needs and competing industry associations. The
EU's leadership in fusion  would benefit from a stronger, structured partnership
between public institutions and all private actors, as well as a shared strategic vision.
• Fusion energy is usually regulated under frameworks designed for nuclear fission,
despite the significant differences in safety and operational risks.

6. Way forward
Public initiatives have long dominated fusion research and development, but the current state of the
technology hints that leadership in the quest for commercialisation of fusion energy will shift to the
private sector in the not-too-distant future. With the different public and private timelines often in
tension, the magnitude of the remaining challenges on the road to viable fusion energy production
means the timing of this transition remains very uncertain. How to manage it will be a central plank
of future strategic approaches to fusion. A flexible approach , striking the correct balance between
public and private support, as well as the different technologies in play, will be key.
Smart public-private collaboration, properly leveraging the strengths of both, will be important to
successfully develop fusion technology. On the public side, continuing support for critical projects
such as ITER would allow them to fulfil their scientific value, establishing supply -chains, and
reinforcing the complementarity and experience they can provide to private actors. Adequately
supporting EU -based private initiatives could close gaps with non -EU competitors, although
fundraising potentially remains a traditional comparative weak point that needs to be addressed. A
fine-tuned regulatory environment and skilled workforce, coupled with enhanced public awareness
of the benefits of fusion to facilitate acceptance, could help establish the groundwork and drive the
incentives for a dynamic EU fusion sector.

private investment in start-ups and their ambitious timelines. Europe is currently leading the public
effort by hosting and driving the world 's flagship fusion project, ITER, which contributes to the
creation of a talent pool and an industrial supply chain. However, it is at risk of falling behind when
it comes to private initiatives. This tension may start to be come clearer in as little as two to three
years, when CFS's SPARC tokamak is predicted to begin operations in the US.
To stay ahead in the development of this strategic technology, the EU must do more. There is a need
for a clear vision that brings together public and private efforts with better governance, regulation
and partnerships. The EU 's industrial value chains need nurturing by ensuring a stable pipeline of
projects and financing from both public and private sources. T he t alent and research capacity
required to keep Europe in the lead  should be mobilised while engaging with citizens, managing
expectations, and monitoring progress on cost, sustainability, and market readiness.

Fusion, regarded by many as the ultimate form of clean
energy, may finally be within reach. This analysis looks
at the context of the latest advances, in particular the
explosion of private-sector interest. Overall, this has led
to an acceleration of many planned timelines, although
a lot of uncertainty remains, given the magnitude of the
remaining technical challenges. In addition, questions
also persist around skills, the regulatory environment,
financing and economic viability. With a new EU
strategy expected in late 2025, this analysis concludes
with an overview of current policy, and discusses some
of the challenges the EU faces in setting a pathway
towards the commercialisation of fusion energy.
