he United States and Japan are positioning their alliance for what they call a “new golden age,” built on far
deeper economic and technological integration.1 Among the advanced technologies shaping this new phase
of alliance integration, quantum has emerged as a shared priority. Both governments now explicitly identify
quantum information science in their national security strategies as essential to long-term economic and defense
planning, with the United States’ 2025 National Security Strategy and Japan’s 2026 National Security Strategy
promoting quantum as a strategic capability.2 Strategic alignment alone, however, does not generate mutual benefit.
How policymakers structure cooperation between the two countries’ quantum ecosystems will determine whether
that alignment produces tangible gains.
The U.S. and Japanese quantum industrial bases differ in makeup, scale, and strengths in ways that create natural
complementarities. If more effectively aligned, these complementarities would allow the two countries to advance
quantum technologies further and faster than either could do on its own. There are three steps U.S. and Japanese
policymakers could take to help translate those complementarities into practical cooperation: (1) establish a
structured partnership between the U.S. National Science Foundation’s technology-commercialization directorate
and Japan’s cross-ministerial innovation program to jointly commercialize quantum technologies, (2) build
a bilateral quantum-infrastructure access program, modeled on the U.S. QUEST initiative, to give Japanese
researchers access to U.S. quantum infrastructure, and (3) add a dedicated quantum-technology pillar to U.S.-Japan
defense-industrial cooperation.

Why Quantum Technologies Are
Strategically Important for the United
States and Japan
The U.S.-Japan alliance has long centered on
ensuring security in the Indo-Pacific and sustaining
both countries’ economic competitiveness. Quantum
technologies are directly tied to both of those goals,
shaping future military advantage and building
future economic competitiveness, making quantum
a critical area for bilateral cooperation.
From a security standpoint, the United States and
Japan have long shared an interest in stabilizing the
Indo-Pacific. Instability has intensified in recent
years: North Korea is launching missiles more
frequently, China is expanding military activity
around Taiwan and Japan’s southwest islands, and
Russia is increasing naval and air operations in the
western Pacific. Quantum technologies, particularly
sensors, are directly disruptive to the balance of
military power because they enhance the ability of
any actor to detect, track, and destabilize military
systems. Quantum magnetometers, for example,
could help track submarines that currently operate
undetected. Quantum gravimeters could reveal
underground facilities or seabed movements with
far greater precision. Advanced quantum inertial
navigation could guide missiles or aircraft accurately,
even when GPS is denied. Maintaining effective
deterrence therefore depends on adopting and
developing these capabilities ahead of adversaries.
From a competitiveness standpoint, quantum
technologies could transform economic and
industrial strength for both the United States and
Japan. In the near term, quantum and quantum-
inspired systems could create new sources of
growth and efficiency by solving complex problems
like factory scheduling, supply chain routing, and
advanced portfolio optimization for finance and
logistics. Over the long term, leading in foundational
quantum hardware, software, and applications will
strengthen national competitiveness because these
capabilities will determine who captures the next
wave of high-value industries. Quantum computing,
for example, could accelerate the discovery of new
drugs, chemicals, and advanced materials, enabling
the creation of entire sectors that do not exist today.
Countries that build and control these capabilities
will ensure greater productivity, higher-value
manufacturing, and more resilient economic power,
rather than relying on others for the technologies that
underpin future growth.
Complementarities in the U.S. and
Japanese Quantum Industrial Bases
While both countries recognize the strategic
and economic value of innovation in quantum
technologies, the United States and Japan have
quantum ecosystems shaped by different industrial
foundations. In the United States, progress is
propelled by a dynamic venture-capital culture
willing to fund high-risk, high-reward research.
That environment has produced a large, diverse
landscape with hundreds of firms, combining major
integrated tech companies like Google and IBM with
specialized, well-funded start-ups such as IonQ and
Rigetti. Japan’s ecosystem looks markedly different.
It is anchored almost entirely by long-established
industrial conglomerates—e.g., NTT, Toshiba, and
Fujitsu—whose internal R&D labs direct most of
the country’s private quantum investment. 3 With
little start-up activity and few new entrants, Japan’s
environment is less entrepreneurial and more
incumbent-driven than that of the United States.
The countries also diverge in their technological
focus. The United States concentrates on core
quantum-computing hardware, directing much
of its effort toward developing fundamental qubit
modalities—superconducting circuits, trapped ions,
and other architectures that define the frontier of
computation. Japan’s strengths sit further up the
stack. Its comparative advantage lies in the industrial
implementation layer: the software, algorithms,

hybrid control systems, and precision-manufactured
components that make quantum systems functional
and dependable in real-world settings. This emphasis
is visible in how Japan’s major firms invest. Fujitsu,
for example, works across the entire quantum
stack, but a large share of its intellectual property
and applied R&D focuses on integration-layer
technologies: algorithms tailored to industrial use
cases, hybrid quantum-classical control systems,
and the manufacturing processes needed to link
quantum processors with existing IT infrastructure. 4
Its portfolio illustrates a broader national orientation
in Japan toward the parts of the quantum stack
that turn hardware into usable systems, rather than
concentrating primarily on the core qubit-level
technologies themselves, as in the United States. This
is consistent with Japan’s broader industrial tradition,
evident in sectors from semiconductors to robotics,
where competitive strength has often been found
in turning early-stage technological advances into
robust, production-ready systems that anchor entire
industrial ecosystems.
Because the two ecosystems specialize in different
stages of the quantum stack, cooperation carries clear
commercial advantages. U.S. firms are producing
frontier hardware but often face a long, uncertain
path from laboratory breakthrough to industrial
deployment. Japan’s industrial base, by contrast,
is structured to absorb, refine, and scale complex
technologies within demanding manufacturing and
enterprise environments. Linking U.S. hardware
innovation with Japan’s system-integration strengths
can help both sides shorten the path from prototype
to production, reduce commercialization risk, and
accelerate revenue generation. Rather than duplicating
capabilities, each country can fill a gap in the other’s
value chain, creating a pipeline that moves quantum
technologies from scientific proof-of-concept to
durable industrial application more efficiently than
either ecosystem could do alone.
How to Accelerate U.S.-Japan
Collaboration on Quantum
Technologies
Before turning to the question of how to accelerate
U.S.-Japan collaboration on quantum technologies, it
is useful to take stock of the state of the alliance today
and how quantum cooperation has shifted under
the Trump administration and Japan’s new prime
minister, Sanae Takaichi. Previously under President
Joe Biden and Prime Minister Fumio Kishida,
quantum cooperation occurred within a broader
security-focused agenda, alongside missile defense,
space, cyber, and other emerging technologies as
part of over 70 agreements aimed at strengthening
Indo-Pacific deterrence and defense industrial
coordination. In that context, quantum functioned as
a supporting technology pillar within an overarching
security and geopolitical strategy.
Under President Donald Trump and Prime Minister
Takaichi, industrial and technological competitiveness
have moved to the forefront of the bilateral agenda.
Traditional defense cooperation will likely continue
through established channels, but the political priority
has moved toward economic arrangements. Initiatives
such as the Framework for Securing the Supply of
Critical Minerals and Rare Earths through Mining
and Processing and the Technology Prosperity Deal
memorandum of cooperation, which were both
ratified in October 2025 during President Trump’s
visit to Tokyo, place quantum within a much more
explicit effort to secure supply chains, expand shared
production capacity, and shape the standards for next-
generation technologies.
An alliance focused on industrial and technological
competitiveness presents both risks and opportunities.
To ensure that U.S.-Japan collaboration on quantum
facilitates the alliance’s full spectrum of shared
goals, including deepening Indo-Pacific stability and
effectively strengthening both countries’ industrial
bases, there are several key areas for immediate action.

First, the U.S. Congress should direct and fully fund
the U.S. National Science Foundation’s Directorate
for Technology, Innovation and Partnerships
(TIP) to serve as the primary vehicle for U.S.-Japan
collaboration on quantum commercialization. TIP is
the only U.S. federal directorate explicitly designed
to move technologies from research into deployment,
requiring industry participation and focusing on
fields essential to national competitiveness, including
quantum. Today, most U.S.-Japan commercial
quantum activity occurs only when individual firms
choose to pursue it. Quantinuum is a clear example:
headquartered in Colorado, it has only two strategic
commercial partnerships globally, and both are
in Japan, with Mitsui and SoftBank. This pattern
demonstrates two key takeaways: Japan has strong
industrial demand for applied quantum solutions,
and U.S. firms already see the country as a viable
commercialization partner. But it also illustrates the
limitation of relying on firm-by-firm initiatives alone.
There is currently no U.S. federal mechanism to
systematically match U.S. hardware and algorithmic
innovations with Japanese industrial challenges or to
scale these partnerships beyond bespoke deals, but
TIP could fulfill this role.
Japan’s closest counterpart to TIP is the Cross-
ministerial Strategic Innovation Promotion Program
(SIP), which coordinates mission-oriented R&D
across ministries and industry. Structured TIP-SIP
collaboration would fill the current gaps and ensure
that benefits flow in both directions. A joint program
could take promising U.S. quantum hardware,
software, and algorithms and match them with
Japan’s industrial testbeds—i.e., automotive, logistics,
and advanced manufacturing—where early quantum
tools are already being trialed. It would also help
prevent asymmetric gains. For example, Denso’s work
with North American quantum firms on optimization
has created efficiencies for Japanese manufacturing,
but there is no formal channel to facilitate equivalent
deployments or market returns in the United States. 5
A coordinated TIP-SIP model would require that
jointly supported projects be demonstrated in both
economies, accelerate commercial maturity on both
sides, and ensure that valuable applications emerging
from collaboration strengthen both countries’
industrial bases rather than only one.
Second, the United States should create a structured
quantum infrastructure offering for Japan. Japan
needs dependable access to quantum hardware
to advance its applied R&D, develop domestic
software capabilities, and train the workforce that
will eventually deploy and maintain these systems.
Japanese institutions are already turning to U.S.
companies for this purpose. The National Institute
of Advanced Industrial Science and Technology,
Japan’s largest public research organization, has
partnered with the American quantum company
IonQ to use its systems for applied research and
early capability development. 6 But deals like this are
narrow and difficult to scale. They depend on one-
off commercial arrangements, provide access to only
a single hardware approach, and cannot deliver the
stable, long-term infrastructure needed to grow a
national ecosystem.
A better model comes from the United States’
Quantum User Expansion for Science and
Technology (QUEST) program, which created
a structured mechanism for giving American
universities, labs, and researchers access to U.S.
quantum-computing systems.7 A bilateral version of
this initiative, coordinated with Japan, would create
a dedicated channel for Japanese universities, labs,
and researchers to access U.S. quantum hardware,
standardized interfaces, and shared experimentation
environments. This would offer a genuine win-
win outcome: Japan would gain reliable, diversified
access to U.S. quantum systems for R&D, software
development, and talent training, while U.S. firms
would gain a stable, government-backed route into a
critical market.

A quantum track within DICAS and PIPIR would give

joint projects, such as developing quantum sensors that
improve regional awareness, building quantum-secure
communication links for crisis-resilient coordination,
and running shared test sites where American and
Japanese engineers can perform trials of early systems
together. Because DICAS and PIPIR already conduct
hands-on industrial cooperation, adding quantum
to these structures is a realistic option to build new
defense capabilities quickly, without creating new
bureaucracy or overloading Japan’s already strained
industrial capacity.
Taken together, these steps would give the United
States and Japan a practical way to deliver on what
they have already declared this new phase of the
alliance should be: a partnership built on industrial
strength, coordinated technological development,
and long-term competitiveness. Quantum is a domain
where both countries can shape the landscape, pool
complementary capabilities, and build systems that
underpin military resilience and economic growth
alike. If Washington and Tokyo want their “new
golden age” to function as a more integrated, forward-
looking alliance, then using quantum to build those
foundations is a critical step to ensure that this vision
moves from rhetoric to reality. •
