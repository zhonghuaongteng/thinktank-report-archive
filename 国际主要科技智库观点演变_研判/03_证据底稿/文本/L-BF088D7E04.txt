Preface
Europe lags significantly behind the United States and China in frontier AI capability. The gap has multiple
underlying causes, with a lack of European capital chief among them. This paper , however, focuses on a
more specific challenge. T o train the most advanced AI models, compute has until recently had to be
concentrated at a single site, a condition particularly hard to meet in Europe given three significant
bottlenecks: 1) power (slow infrastructure buildout and insufficient power at any single site) ; 2) compute
(a small and fragmented compute stock); and 3) politics (difficulty of political coordination across member
states).
Distributed training, a set of techniques allowing geographically separated datacentres to function as a single
training resource, has matured rapidly since 2023. This paper assesses its relevance to European frontier AI
capability, drawing on a literature review and expert interviews, a s well as quantitative estimation of
compute and power requirements. The analysis finds that distributed training offers relief on the power and
political coordination bottlenecks. As for compute, it cannot solve the lack of chips itself, but what it does
change is whether Europe’s existing and planned compute can support frontier training at all: without
distributed training, the same chips remain fragmented across clusters individually too small for frontier
work. The report ends by setting out policy recommendations in two areas: preparing European physical
infrastructure for distributed training and simplifying and harmonising cross-border regulation.

Summary
Frontier AI systems are developed primarily in the United States and increasingly in China. If Europe is to
influence how AI is governed internationally, it will need sufficient domestic capability to warrant a seat at
the table where the relevant rules are written and norms decided.
While Europe’s lag in frontier AI has multiple underlying causes, with a lack of European capital chief
among them, this paper focuses on a more specific challenge: to train the most advanced AI models,
compute has until recently had to be concentrated at a single site, with hundreds of thousands of specialised
chips operating in close proximity. This condition gives rise to three types of bottlenecks which stand in the
way of building frontier-scale training capability in Europe: power, compute and politics. Connecting large
datacentres to European grids currently takes seven to ten years, delivery times for critical equipment such
as transformers and turbines have risen substantially, and no single European site currently supplies, or is
on track to supply by 2030, the 5 to 10 gigawatts projected to be needed to train a frontier AI by then.
Coordinating any concentrated buildout of this scale would also require political alignment across the EU’s
member states, a historically time-consuming endeavour.
Distributed training could offer relief : it allows datacentres in different locations to work together on the
same training run, coordinated by a central entity, without needing to be physically co -located. Research
progress since 2023 has substantially reduced the bandwidth between sites these techniques require, making
them increasingly practical at scale. This paper examines whether distributed training can support European
frontier AI capability, given the conditions shaping Europe’s compute buildout, d rawing on a literature
review, expert interviews, and quantitative estimation of compute and power requirements.
Findings
Distributed training offers substantial relief for Europe’s power and political-coordination bottlenecks but
does not narrow Europe ’s underlying chip gap. What it does change for compute is whether the chips
Europe has can be pooled into a single resource for frontier training, rather than remaining fragmented
across individual clusters too small for frontier work. The following paragraphs examine each bottleneck in
turn.
Power. Distributed training offers substantial relief in terms of Europe ’s power constraints. Utilities are
typically more willing to commit power to a portfolio of smaller sites than to a single multi-gigawatt facility,
where the scale of the commitment creates qualitatively different infrastructure requirements and a much
larger planning risk. Connection requests for smaller sites can also proceed in parallel through different
national queues, compressing overall calendar timelines compared to a single ve ry large grid connection
request. Spreading compute across grid regions furthermore allows workloads to be matched to local energy

profiles. Because the underlying algorithms for training frontier AI tolerate individual sites pausing and
rejoining, distributed training may also let operators curtail load during grid peak hours without disrupting
ongoing training runs, freeing additional capacity on already existing infrastructure. Distributing
infrastructure across countries may also reduce concentration risk in equipment procurement and improve
resilience against grid-level disruption.
Compute . Distributed training provides limited direct relief on the compute gap itself, because it does not
add chips. As of the beginning of 2026, European operational capacity stands at approximately 123,000
H100-equivalents (a normalisation expressing chip throughput in units of the NVIDIA H100, the reference
accelerator for frontier-training compute), against roughly 1.4 million in the United States. Incl uding all
confirmed and likely planned capacity, as well as the EU’s flagship gigafactory programme, Europe reaches
around 3.2 million by 2030, while the United States reaches at least 19.4 million. Several AI middle powers,
including Saudi Arabia, South Korea and the United Arab Emirates, are also planning capacity that would
put each ahead of Europe by 2030. Europe is therefore roughly an order of magnitude behind on both
operational and planned compute, and distributed training does not change this. Distributed training does,
however, determine whether the chips Europe build s can be combined into a poo led resource for more
efficient frontier training, rather than remaining fragmented across clusters individually too small for
frontier work.
Politics . A distributed architecture eases Europe’s coordination problem by spreading the benefits of hosting
AI infrastructure across more member states, including energy investment, local workforce development,
and the emergence of AI-adjacent ecosystems around hosted compute. It also lowers the political threshold
for engaging non -EU European countries within EFTA, particularly Norway and Switzerland , because
adding new partners no longer requires negotiating their participation in a single concentrated facility. In
addition, a distributed strategy can build on the existing institutional scaffolding of the EuroHPC – its
procurement pipelines, its multi-sovereign decision-making processes, and its already-agreed site locations
across the continent – more readily than a centralised one.
What would European distributed training look like in practice?
To illustrate what reaching frontier -competitive compute capacity in Europe would require, we set out a
hypothetical scenario built on a target of 10 to 20 million H100 -equivalents, the range needed to match
the order of magnitude of other major and middle -power compute holders by 2030. Translating this into
physical infrastructure yields a power requirement of approximately 17 to 34 gigawatts of total facility
capacity. No single Eur opean site can supply this. A plausible alternative architecture would com bine a
small number of hyperscale clusters of roughly 2 to 3 gigawatts each, sited where large grid connections are
feasible, with a larger number of smaller distributed sites spread across the continent to exploit regional
energy mixes and faster connecti on timelines. This is one configuration among several that could in
principle deliver the target, and the actual split between hyperscale and distributed capacity should emerge
from a detailed siting assessment rather than from policy prescription.
Limitations of distributed training
Several limitations warrant attention. Distributed training does not address Europe’s underlying capital and
financial constraints, which underpin all three of the bottlenecks described above. Nor does it give Europe

side of compute demand, but inference is already the dominant share of AI compute consumption and is
expected to continue growing relative to training.
Recommendations
Map and prepare physical infrastructure for distributed training :
● Commission an assessment of spare grid capacity across European regions, led by the European
Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity (ENTSO-E) in collaboration with the
Directorate-General for Energy (DG ENER) and member state authorities.
● Commission a parallel assessment of existing and required cross-border fibre infrastructure, led by
the Directorate-General for Communications Networks, Content and Technology (DG
CONNECT) in collaboration with member states and the Body of European Regulators for
Electronic Communications (BEREC).
● Publish a European AI Infrastructure Reference, maintained by the European AI Office, mapping
candidate datacentre sites across Europe and tagging each by suitability for different workloads.
● Reduce friction in connecting AI infrastructure to existing fibre backbones through targeted
deregulation of fibre permitting and rights-of-way, and subsidies for last-mile or inter-site links
where commercial provision falls short.
● Monitor trends in distributed training within the European AI Office, drawing on existing
technical monitoring activities and external expertise.
Simplify and harmonise cross -border regulation :
● Accelerate and harmonise permitting and grid connection processes for AI infrastructure across
member states.
● Establish a portfolio of pre-approved candidate sites with environmental assessments, zoning
clearances and grid connections already in place.
● Clarify how existing data protection and sovereignty instruments apply to intra-European Union
training-related data flows, through the Cloud and AI Development Act

1. Introduction
Artificial intelligence (AI) is emerging as perhaps the most transformative general -purpose technology in
history, and at this inflection point, Europe finds itself trailing behind. The technology possesses European
intellectual roots, from Alan Turing ’s foundational concepts of machine intelligence (Turing 1950) to
Hochreiter & Schmidhuber’s (1997) development of Long Short -Term Memory networks in the 1990s,
an early neural architecture for sequence modelling that preceded the transformer-based models dominating
today. Yet over the course of successive AI summers and winters, the centre of gravity has shifted away from
Europe. Now, in the midst of an AI boom fuelled by large language models (LLMs), the most powerful
frontier AI systems are developed not in Europe, but in the United States and increasingly in China.
If Europe seeks to embed fundamental values such as human rights, the rule of law and democratic
accountability into the emerging international AI order, it must have the competitive capacity to warrant a
seat at the negotiating table where that order is being written. Europe does, and will, retain signific ant
leverage through its large single market, regulatory reach and diplomatic weight (Draghi 2024). However,
as AI systems increasingly determine economic productivity, defensive capability and innovative potential
(Stanford HAI 2025), the actors that develop and control these systems will increasingly set the terms on
which others can use them. Without reliable access to frontier AI capabilities, Europe thus risks being
relegated to attempting to regulate a technology it neither builds nor fully understands.
A notable symptom of Europe’s weakness in frontier AI is the lack of compute at its disposal to support
frontier AI training (Fox, Schnitzer & Privitera 2025). This is first ly a capital problem . T he financing
needed to build frontier -scale compute infrastructure has flowed predominantly to US and increasingly
Chinese firms rather than to European ones. This capital problem has deep structural roots: shallow venture
capital markets, an absence of university –venture capital talent ecosystems, and a lack of big- tech
incumbents that could finance frontier capex from cash flow. These conditions in turn drive a steady brain
drain of AI talent towards Silicon Valley, which further reinforces them (Draghi 2024).
A further cause relates to the set of physical conditions needed to build the infrastructure itself. Frontier AI
training requires datacentre clusters drawing multiple gigawatts of power (Sevilla & Troynikov 2025). These
facilities need sites with adequate grid connections, and must be built quickly enough to be commercially
viable, be it by US or indigenous European hyperscalers.

concentrated in a single location, because graphics processing units (GPUs) must exchange information at
every training step – a level of communication that is easy to achieve inside a single datacentre but more
challenging across separate sites. As a result, US companies plan to build individual datacentres the size of

stars to align: sufficient power availability and grid capacity, enough space to avoid the dissent of residents,
and permitting laws that allow fast building timetables. Finding suitable locations is hard enough in general,
and particularly so in Europe, where existing plans for datacentre buildouts tend to be much more
fragmented (European Commission 2025c).
However, a relatively new set of techniques, collectively called distributed training, allow compute to be
spread across multiple sites yet function as a single large pool for training runs (Kryś, Sharma & Egan 2025).
Each site performs computations locally and synchronises results at regular intervals that, depending on the
algorithm used, can range from every training step to every several hundred steps ( Douillard et al. 2023).
This addresses a real constraint on how capacity, once built, can be used, and on the degree to which already
existing infrastructure can be utilised. If distributed training proves viable at the multi -gigawatt scale
required for frontier training, European compute infrastructure being spread across multiple countries from
Spain to the Nordics would cease to be a disadvantage, making Europe a more viable place for frontier AI
development.
Distributed training does not, of course, resolve Europe ’s broader competitiveness problem, nor would it
give Europe a comparative advantage : the United States is already using these techniques in some of its
frontier training, and China is moving in the same direction. Facilitating large training runs is only one
piece of the puzzle, and it does not substitute for ambitious economic policies to enable further datacentre
buildout and attract AI talent back to the continent. But it addresses a constraint that is hard to solve
through investment alone: the requirement that all compute for a frontier training run be concentrated in
a single location, which even well- funded European efforts would struggle to meet given the continent’ s
political, energy and infrastructure situation. Realising this benefit depends on policy choices. Sufficient
connectivity between datacentres is a precondition for distributed training (Sevilla & Troynikov 2025), and
the regulatory, permitting and coordination frameworks needed to enable rapid distributed buildouts are
not currently in place. This paper analyses to what degree the technology ca n be leveraged to facilitate
frontier AI training on European soil, and evaluates which policies need to be in place to enable it.
Our assessment draws on a narrative literature review of recent technical papers and industry reports (2023–
2025), semi-structured interviews with distributed training experts, and quantitative estimation of compute,
power and timeline requirements drawing on the latest available data. Based on this analysis, we present
policy options for a European frontier AI strategy employing distributed training. Section 2 describes our
methodology in detail. Section 3 introduces distributed training and surveys recent technical developments.
Section 4 analyses how distributed training addresses the three principal European infrastructure
bottlenecks: power, compute and political coordination. Section 5 discusses cross- cutting risks and
limitations. Section 6 presents policy recommendations based on our analysis.

2. Methodology
2.1. Literature review
We conducted a narrative literature review spanning multiple domains: technical papers on distributed
training methods (2023–2026), industry analyses of compute scaling trends, energy infrastructure reports,
and policy documents from European and international sources. A narrative rather than systematic review
was chosen given the interdisciplinary scope of the research question and the need to integrate technical,
infrastructural and policy sources that follow different publication conventions. For technic al papers, we
searched arXiv and Google Scholar using terms such as ‘ distributed training ’, ‘ decentralized training ’,
‘DiLoCo’ and ‘geo-distributed machine learning’, filtered by publication date (January 2023–April 2026).
The January 2023 start date was chosen to capture the emergence of recent low -communication training
algorithms, beginning with the original DiLoCo paper (Douillard et al. 2023). For energy infrastructure,
we drew primarily on reports from the International Energy Agency (IEA) and the North American Electric
Reliability Corporation (NERC), which provide institutional analyses of grid capacity, transmission
infrastructure and supply chain constraints. These were supplemented by quantitative projections from
Goldman Sachs and by trade press coverage of specific datacentre projects from outlets such as Data Center
Dynamics, which we used to track site-specific developments and corporate announcements rather than as
primary analytical sources. For European political and coordination dynamics, we consulted policy analyses
from institutions such as the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, the International Monetary
Fund and the Delors Centre. We also monitored news coverage of European AI initiatives. Selection criteria
across all domains p rioritised recen tness, relevance to European infrastructure context and source
credibility.
2.2. Expert consultations
Between September and November 2025, we conducted four semi -structured interviews (follow ing a
flexible topic guide rather than a fixed script) with experts in distributed training research, AI infrastructure
analysis and compute scaling, including researchers with published work on distributed training methods,
practitioners knowledgeable in distributed and decentralised training systems, and analysts tracking frontier
AI compute requirements. We cross -checked recurring expert claims against published sources where
possible. In cases where these claims could be verified, we prioritised referencing published work ; in cases
where verification was not possible, we flag claims as expert assessments in the text.
A number of limitations apply. The sample size is small and weighed towards US-based experts, reflecting
the broader concentration of distributed-training expertise in the United States (as documented elsewhere

2.3. Quantitative estimation
Our quantitative estimates of compute, power and timeline requirements draw on three categories of inputs.
For compute, we use published estimates of recent frontier training runs, alongside projections from Epoch
AI’s analytical reports and GPU Clusters dataset. Where training compute is not officially disclosed, we use
the most widely cited third-party estimate and flag it as such. All compute figures are converted to H100 -
equivalents,
2 to allow comparison across hardware generations. For power, we estimate facility-level demand
by combining two sources: per-GPU power figures drawn from RAND and Epoch AI, and announced or
projected capacities for specific datacentre projects. Where these two methods produce similar figures, we
treat the estimate as relatively robust; where they diverge, we report a range. Regional power demand
projections are drawn from the IEA’s Energy and AI report (IEA 2025). For timelines, we compare publicly
documented recent buildouts against pre -AI-boom industry benchmarks for datacentre construction.
Timeline estimates are particularly uncertain because they depend on jurisdiction-specific permitting, grid
interconnection queues and supply chain conditions. Where possible, we present ranges rather than point
estimates, with bounds reflecting different published assumptions. We state ‘we estimate’ to flag claims that
rest on our own synthesis of sources rather than a single published figure.
2.4. Definition of ‘Europe’
In this report, we focus on the potential of distributed training for Europe’s frontier AI strategy. We
recognise that multiple definitions of ‘Europe’ exist, and that there are several criteria for the inclusion or
non-inclusion of various countries under such definitions. We choose to limit ourselves to the European
Union member states plus the four EFTA countries (Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein and Switzerland). This
makes our analysis primarily EU -focused, but also allows us to include countries that would plausibly
participate in a joint European scaling effort, and many of which have the necessary infrastructure to host
large numbers of data centres.
We have chosen not to include the United Kingdom in our analysis. While geographically and historically
European, the UK’s AI ecosystem has diverged significantly from the EU’s since Brexit. The UK has pursued
a distinct regulatory approach, declining to adopt EU -style horizontal AI regulation in favour of sector -
specific guidance (DSIT 2023, 2024). Its institutional architecture has also developed along different lines.

2.5. Limitations
Several limitations apply to this paper beyond those specific to each methodological component.
Firstly, frontier AI companies treat compute capacity and infrastructure plans as competitive intelligence,
and public data on frontier training runs is both limited and unevenly verified. Expert claims about
corporate infrastructure plans often cannot be independently cross-checked against public sources, and we
have flagged such claims in the text where they affect substantive conclusions.
Secondly, most of the technical literature on distributed training, most frontier AI corporate activity, and
most of the available expert pool are concentrated in the United States, with smaller contributions from
Chinese sources and a sparser set of European-specific institutional analyses. This means that claims about
the US context are better supported than claims about specific European jurisdictions. Where European-
specific sources exist, we draw on them directly (notably Draghi 2024 , IEA 2025 and Fox, Schnitz er &
Privitera 2025); where they do not, we flag the geographic asymmetry in the text.
Thirdly, this is a fast -moving field. Technical developments in distributed training, announcements of
datacentre buildouts, and policy initiatives at the EU and member-state level may render some of the paper’s
specific estimates obsolete within months rather than years. We have attempted to distinguish between
structural claims that are likely to remain valid over time and specific figures that may be superseded.
Fourthly, the paper’ s scope is deliberately restricted to the technical and infrastructural dimensions of
European frontier AI capability and the potential contribution of distributed training therein. We do not
deeply analyse adjacent questions that a comprehensive European AI strategy would need to address,
including talent policy, intellectual property frameworks, semiconductor supply chains and indigenous AI
company creation. Th is limitation define s the specific contribution of this paper rather than it s
completeness as a strategy document.

3. What is distributed training and why is it useful?
The term ‘distributed training’ describes the training of AI models across multiple physically distant pools
of compute, with a central entity coordinating the process (Kryś, Sharma & Egan, 2025). To evaluate the
promise of distributed training for European AI ambitions, we must first understand what it is, how it
differs from other methods, which bottlenecks in building AI infrastructure it addresses, and how recent
developments have influenced the potential of the technology. We do not aim to cover the technical details
exhaustively, but rather to provide the background needed for the policy discussion that follows. For a more
detailed discussion, we recommend Kryś, Sharma & Egan (2025) for a governance -focused overview,
Douillard et al. (2023) for the foundational DiLoCo (Distributed Low- Communication) framework, and
Sevilla & Troynikov (2025) for an engineering analysis of distributed training at frontier scale.

3.1. What is distributed training, and what is it not?
The most common method for training frontier AI models remains centralised training, where all the GPUs
used for a training run are co-located within a single datacentre or campus, connected by high-bandwidth,
low-latency interconnects (Kryś, Sharma & Egan 2025). However, this definition is becoming less clear -
cut; major companies such as Google increasingly use multiple datacentres within a single campus or region,
connected by high -bandwidth links that effectively function as a unified facility (Gemini Te am 2023).
Distributed training, as discussed in this report, refers specifically to training across multiple geographically
separated locations, where lower bandwidth connections necessitate different algorithmic approaches. In
distributed training, the co mputational workload for a single model is shared across these dispersed sites
while maintaining a central entity for coordination (Kryś, Sharma & Egan 2025).
Distributed training should not be confused with decentralised training, which relies on community -
provided compute that is contributed on a peer -to-peer basis with no central coordinating entity (Kryś ,
Sharma & Egan 2025). While the two paradigms differ primarily in coordination structure rather than in
any strict technical requirement, decentralised training as currently practiced depends on low -
communication algorithms, since its community participants typical ly operate over consumer -grade
internet rather than dedicated fibre.

3.2. Why is distributed training useful?
The value proposition of distributed training is that it converts geographically dispersed compute into a
pooled, collectively usable resource for a single training run, thus addressing resource limitations resulting
from location constraints. Hosting vast amounts of compute in close proximity requires physical
infrastructure that few locations can offer.

frontier model training facility, encompassing not only the running of GPUs but also cooling, networking

Distributed training also addresses constraints to infrastructure buildout. Connecting a large datacentre to

Douillard et al. 2026). A distributed approach also allows developers to place different workloads on regions
with different types of power sources. Nuclear capacity, for instance, is suitable for baseload power, while
hydroelectric power plants offer flexibility to absorb demand fluctuations (IEA 2025).

3.3. Recent research advances
Before turning to recent algorithmic developments, it is worth briefly explaining the technical problem they
are trying to solve. In distributed training, multiple datacentres work in parallel on different portions of the
same training run, each making incremental updates to its local copy of the model. To ensure that these
local copies do not drift apart, the sites must periodically synchronise, exchanging information about how
each has updated the model so that all copies converge on the same final result (Duan et al. 2024). This
synchronisation step is what consumes bandwidth between sites. The more frequently sites synchronise, the
more bandwidth-intensive the training run becomes, and the less feasible it is to operate over slow or long-
distance connections. Conventional training methods were developed for single-facility deployment, where
GPUs are connected by high -speed intra -facility interconnects that make frequent synchronisation
inexpensive. Applied across geographically separated sites, however, conventional methods require
dedicated high-bandwidth fibre links to maintain acceptable performance. The recent algorithmic advances
summarised in Table 1 therefore all share a common goal: reducing how often, or how much, sites need to
communicate without degrading training quality (Douillard et al. 2023).
These advances have aimed to improve distributed training ’s ability to operate across large geographic
distances and with reduced communication between sites. Experts we consulted disagree, however, on how
significant they are. Some argue that existing bandwidth infrastructure, including dedicated fibre, is already
sufficient for distributed training across continental distances using conventional methods, and that the
recent development of low-communication algorithms primarily matters for contexts where such dedicated
infrastructure is unavailable. Others posit that recent advances fundamentally expand the range of feasible
distributed training configurations, including those with weaker or shared network connections between
sites. A separate methodological concern, raised by several experts, is that many of the recent studies rely on

4. Distributed training as a potential solution to infrastructure
bottlenecks
To assess the potential of distributed training for European scaling ambitions, we must first consider the
bottlenecks standing in the way. Here w e focus on three principal infrastructure bottlenecks that a public
or private actor would face when seeking to conduct large -scale frontier AI training runs: power
infrastructure, compute availability and political coordination. Sections 4.1, 4.2 and 4.3 address these
bottlenecks in turn. For each, we examine how the bottleneck applies to Europe specifically, assess what
relief distributed training could offer, and acknowledge the limitations of that relief. Our analysis finds that
distributed training offers substantial relief for power constraints and reduces the political friction of
concentrated infrastructure in a multi-state political architecture, but provides only limited direct benefit
for the compute gap.
4.1. Power
4.1.1. The European power challenge
Europe’s power infrastructure may stymie its AI ambitions in four ways: lengthy grid connection timelines,
supply chain delays, insufficient single -site capacity, and reliability requirements that are in tension with
EU energy policy.
Connecting a large datacentre to the European grid can take 7 to 10 years in the FLAP-D hubs (Frankfurt,
London, Amsterdam, Paris, Dublin), compared to 1 to 3 years in the United States. This gap reflects layered
permitting (EU, member -state, regional), longer environmental and consul tation processes, and grid
operator backlogs from renewables integration (IEA 2025). The EU has signalled its intent to accelerate
this process through the proposed Cloud and AI Development Act (European Commission 2025b; 2026),
which is expected to address permitting, siting and grid connection bottlenecks for AI infrastructure. But
the US is starting from a substantially shorter baseline, and is already compressing its timelines further
through measures such as the July 2025 e xecutive order on accelerated federal permitting (White House
2025).
The second challenge is global rather than specifically European: energy supply chain delays. Lead times for
large grid transformers reached 80 to 210 weeks (roughly 1.5 to 4 years) by 2024, approximately double
their 2021 level (IEA 2025a), and have since plateaued year -on-year (NERC 2025). Building new
transmission lines can take four to eight years in advanced economies (IEA 2025). Gas turbines for new
gas-fired power plants now face lead times of several years, potentially delaying their commissioning beyond

2030 (IEA 2025). Battery storage systems, essential for grid reliability and demand flexibility, meanwhile
face acute supply chain pressure as the expanding AI data centre buildout has placed considerable strain on
the supply chain for key grid components, including low-voltage solutions and equipment supporting the
integration of variable energy resources (IEA 2025). These delays are not independent. A disruption to one
component cascades through procurement timelines for the others.
The third challenge is frontier AI training ’s rapidly growing power demand. Frontier AI companies are
outlining plans for datacentres with approximately 1 GW capacity in the near term, scaling to 5 GW by
2030, with aggressive scenarios predicting 10 GW for a single geographically concentrated cluster (Sevilla
& Troynikov 2025). To put that in perspective , France’s peak electricity demand in 2021 was
approximately 89 GW (S&P Global 2021). A single 10 GW cluster would draw roughly 11 per cent of
that, in one place.
Power availability and reliability requirements create the fourth challenge, and together they generate a
direct tension with EU energy policy. Meeting projected AI power demand at multi -gigawatt scale is
difficult without new dispatchable generation, and Goldman Sachs projects that natural gas will provide 60
per cent of (US) expanded datacentre power demand by 2030 (Goldman Sachs 2024). On shorter
timescales, during synchronisation events AI training workloads produce significant transient power spikes,
which require fast-ramping generation or substantial battery storage to manage. Meeting Tier III or Tier
IV reliability standards

redundant grid feeds from independent substations, uninterruptible power supplies to bridge sub -second

directly with the EU’s REPowerEU plan, which aims to end EU dependence on Russian fossil fuels by 2027
and significantly reduce overall gas consumption by 2030 (European Commission 2022).
Taken together, these four constraints mean that any effort to build concentrated multi-gigawatt AI training
infrastructure in Europe would face long timelines, constrained supply chains, insufficient single -site
generation capacity, and a direct conflict with EU climate commitments. These are the conditions under
which distributed training becomes most relevant, as discussed in the next subsection.
4.1.2. Distributed training as a partial solution to the power challenge
Distributed training offers partial relief on all four fronts, though it eliminates none of them outright.
Specifically, it operates multiple levers, as described below.
The first lever is the scale of any single demand point. Instead of forcing one site to shoulder 5 –10 GW,
distributed training lets Europe spread the load across grid regions, with individual sites drawing from
several hundred megawatts to a few gigawatts each. Utilities are often reluctant to sign very large power
purchase agreements for multi -gigawatt single sites for several reasons. First ly, they perceive risks that AI
companies could default or that demand could fall. Second ly, serving larger loads triggers qualitatively
different infrastructure requirements: at the scale of a few hundred megawatts, facilities typically require

facing the longest delays (ENTSO-E 2024; Ember 2024). By distributing capacity across multiple smaller
sites in different grid regions, Europe can pursue parallel buildout tracks that enter separate interconnection
queues, potentially reducing overall calendar time compared to a single massive grid connection request.

one site is delayed, other sites can proceed, whereas a centralised facility faces complete delays from any
single supply disruption. Distributed p rocurement also enables jurisdictional hedging: sourcing critical
equipment across multiple supplier countries reduces exposure to any single export control regime or
bilateral dispute. Partnerships with countries such as Japan, home to Mitsubishi Power, one of the three
main gas turbine manufacturers globally (IEA 2025), become more strategically valuable in a distributed
architecture where procurement is spread across multiple sites and countries. Such partnerships could help
European companies secure priority access to critical equipment and reduce dependence on US-dominated
supply chains, which, as recent analysis suggests, face significant vulnerabilities of their own.
Distributed training also aligns with Europe ’s heterogeneous energy mix and with the decarbonisation
objectives set out in REPowerEU (European Commission 2022) and the Clean Industrial Deal (European

characteristics at one location, likely forcing heavier reliance on gas.
Finally, geographic distribution may provide resilience against grid-level disruptions, which the IEA reports
have increased fourfold between 2020 and 2024 (IEA 2025) across OECD countries (IEA 2025). A targeted
disruption to energy infrastructure serving one or two datacentres could halt a centralised training run,
whereas a distributed system can continue operating with degraded capacity while affected sites recover, a
property that DiLoCo-style algorithms are specifically designed to support (see Section 3.4). An attack or
natural disaster affecting energy infrastructure supplying one or two datacentres would more easily halt a
training run than when capacity is spread across many sites. That said, it should be noted that some
interviewed experts disagree with this view, pointing out that since datacentres are interconnected during
distributed training, a disruption to the weakest link would be enough to halt the training.
4.1.3. Limitations and uncertainties
As demonstrated above, distributed training offers relief on energy infrastructure challenges in multiple
ways. That being said, it does not fully eliminate them, and uncertainties remain. Five limitations in
particular warrant attention: total power requirements are not reduced; gas dependency persists; the IEA’s
flexibility estimates rest on demanding preconditions; coordination complexity grows with the number of
sites; and the resilience benefits of distribution are partially offset by new points of failure.
Firstly, distributed training does not reduce total power requirements. Whether Europe builds one 10 GW
facility or ten 1 GW facilities, the aggregate power demand remains broadly comparable. In fact, distributed
training marginally increases power demand, due to additional inter -site networking infrastructure, and
performance overheads from synchronisation delays may need to be compensated with additional compute
time (Jaghouar et al. 2024b). Whatever the absolute scale of capacity Europe ultimately targets, building it
remains a major undertaking, and distributed training does nothing to reduce the underlying energy
requirement.

Secondly, gas dependency persists. As noted in Section 4.1.1, natural gas is predicted to serve 60 per cent

manage the predictable ramp-up and ramp-down of power demand at the start and end of training runs,
Nordic hydroelectric capacity is limited in total scale relative to projected European AI energy needs (IEA

storage. Battery storage is technically well-suited to absorbing such transients, but current deployment levels
are insufficient and, as noted in Section 4.1.1, batteries face acute supply chain vulnerabilities (IEA 2025).

substituting it for more costly alternatives such as liquid natural gas (LNG) imports from the US. The 2026

capable of dynamically shifting workloads (IEA 2025). A parallel constraint applies on the US side: Norris
et al. (2025) similarly find that the 76 GW figure depends on contractual innovation and activ e
coordination between operators and grid authorities, neither of which is currently widespread. It remains
unclear how rapidly Europe could fulfil such conditions in a coordinated manner, particularly given the
heterogeneity of national permitting regimes and the limited current deployment of grid -scale battery
storage.
Fourthly, experts interviewed for this report have highlighted that adding additional sites would increase
coordination complexity. While parallel buildout shortens the duration of any single connection process
(see Section 4.1.1), multiple grid connections mean multiple permitting processes in parallel, the navigation
of heterogeneous national regulatory regimes, and thus multiple potential delays. Each datacentre location
requires separate negotiations with utilities, environmental approvals, and mobilisation of construction, grid
interconnection and permitting expertise, all of which are in short supply across European markets.
Finally, as pointed out by some of the experts we interviewed, the energy grid resilience benefits of
distribution may be partially offset by the fact that connecting multiple datacentres creates additional points
of failure. While the coordination role in distributed training can be reassigned between sites if one goes
down, the inter -site network links themselves become critical infrastructure, and disruption to key
connections could degrade or halt synchronisation across the network. That said, distribut ed training
algorithms such as DiLoCo are designed to tolerate temporary site dropouts (see Section 3.4), a property
demonstrated empirically at the 10 -billion-parameter scale by the INTELLECT- 1 run (Jaghouar et al.
2024), though tolerance to sustained or coordinated inter-site disruption at frontier scales remains untested,

4.2. Compute
4.2.1. The European compute challenge
Compute is the second major bottleneck for European AI development. Whereas the power challenge
discussed in Section 4.1 typically involves constraints caused by physical infrastructure, grid connection
timelines and energy policy, the compute challenge comprises a different set of factors: access to scarce
frontier chips, the capital required to procure them at scale, and the willingness of hardware suppliers and
host governments to allocate cutting-edge GPUs to European buyers. This section sets out the scale of the
gap between European compute capacity and that of other major actors, examines what closing the gap
would require, and considers two additional challenges that make the gap harder to close: European buyers
being deprioritised by chip suppliers, and shifting US export controls on frontier hardware.

4.2.2. Distributed training as a partial solution to the compute challenge
Unlike with power infrastructure, distributed training offers limited direct relief for the compute bottleneck,
as it does not increase the total pool of compute available. However, it does make Europe ’s dispersed
compute stock more easily and efficiently usable for frontier training. As discussed in Sections 3 and 4.1.2,
distributed training algorithms such as DiLoCo allow multiple geographically separated clusters to function
as a single effective training resource by tolerating long intervals between synchronisation steps (Douillard
et al. 2023; Jaghouar et al. 2024a). Recent systems can also cope with some hardware differences between
sites. Decoupled DiLoCo, for example, completed a single training run on a mix of older and newer versions
of Google's custom AI chips without losing performance (Douillard et al. 2026). A pooled European
compute base – EuroHPC machines, AI factories and commercial clusters – would be more demanding
still, since it would combine not just different chip generations but chips from different manufacturers,
such as NVIDIA and AMD. Training across manufacturers has not yet been demonstrated. This matters
because companies considering large-scale training runs in Europe do not just need chips to exist somewhere
on the continent; they need that compute to be usable together. Without distributed training, the European
compute base remains fragmen ted across individual clusters, none of them large enough on their own to
support frontier training runs. Distributed training also builds naturally on existing European initiatives.
The EuroHPC Joint Undertaking already operates supercomputer sites across multiple countries, and the
AI factory and gigafactory programmes are designed to span multiple member states. This institutional and
physical infrastructure makes distributed training a realistic pathway by which Europe ’s fragmented
compute base can be combined into a single training resource at frontier scale.
Distributed training may also provide indirect benefits for chip procurement. As noted in Section 4.2.1,
European companies currently sit at the back of the queue for GPU allocation from manufacturers. A
distributed infrastructure strategy addresses this by spreading hosted compute across more member states.
When a state hosts part of a distributed training network, it acquires a direct economic and political stake
in the success of European compute procurement, which makes it more likely to back EU -level j oint
negotiation with chip manufacturers. It is worth noting, however, that this argument applies primarily
where Europe seeks to procure chips for European -headquartered companies. US hyperscalers building
European datacentres draw on their own procurement pipelines, in which they sit near the front of the
queue, so the deprioritisation problem matters most where Europe seeks to build indigenous frontier
capability, and matters less where the goal is hosting US-led infrastructure on European soil.
The track record on EU joint procurement is mixed. Joint vaccine procurement in 2020 –21 is widely
considered to have succeeded under pressure (European Commission 2021a), while the joint gas purchasing
platform launched in 2022 faced structural challenges from market liberalisation and limited intra -
European supply, though it has since bee n cited as a blueprint for future demand aggregation (Nicoli &
Beetsma 2024). Chip procurement would likely face similar coordination challenges, but the political logic
favouring joint action is stronger when more member states are direct stakeholders. The $40 billion US –
EU chip purchase commitment announced in September 2025, although negotiated as part of a broader

this scale are now possible (Chavez 2025). Whether such commitments translate into priority access

4.2.3. Limitations and uncertainties
Despite the potential benefits discussed in Section 4.2.2, distributed training does not directly close the
fundamental compute gap, as it does not reduce overall GPU requirements, it provides little relief from
allocation problems, it offers no protection against shifting US export controls, and the coordinated
procurement it enables may slow execution.
Just as distributed training does not reduce total power requirements (Section 4.1.3), it also does not reduce
overall GPU requirements. The technology changes how existing compute is used without changing how
much is needed. Recent demonstrations such as INTELLECT- 1 suggest the efficiency overhead can be
modest at smaller scales ( Jaghouar et al. 2024a), but the picture at frontier scales is less clear. Either way,
the fundamental compute gap is not solved by technology.
Distributed training also offers little relief from chip allocation problems. As discussed in Section 4.2.1,
European companies sit at the back of the queue for GPU allocation from manufacturers. This is a
procurement and commercial relationship issue, not an infrastructure one, and rearranging where compute
is physically located does not directly change which companies sit at the front of the queue.
Nor does distributed training protect against shifting US export controls. The United States has revised its
export control regime on frontier AI chips multiple times since 2022, including the October 2022 Bureau
of Industry and Security BIS rules, the October 2023 update, the December 2024 measures, the January
2025 AI Diffusion Rule, and the re scission of that same Diffusion Rule in May 2025 (Allen & Goldston
2025; BIS 2025). The chips inside European datacentres are subject to the same regime regardless of how
those datacentres are physically configured.
Finally, while coordinated procurement may strengthen Europe ’s bargaining position with chip
manufacturers, as discussed in Section 4.2.2, it carries an execution speed cost. EU coordination has a well-
documented track record of slow decision making; the Draghi report (2024) found that EU legislation
averages 19 months from Commission proposal to adoption. Distributed training therefore creates a trade-
off between bargaining leverage and deployment speed. This takes us to the question of politics.
4.3. Politics
4.3.1. European coordination challenges
Europe’s fragmented political authority introduces coordination challenges and conflicting economic
interests that may bottleneck efforts to scale European AI compute infrastructure.
The European Union has a long history of political resistance to centralised coordination. While much of
this history involves areas such as migration policy and defence , where the EU has limited formal
competence, similar dynamics have emerged in adjacent economic domains where its authority is stronger:
for example, Ireland, Luxembourg, Malta and Cyprus have resisted enhanced authority for the European

Securities and Markets Authority in capital markets integration (Lindner & Mack 2024), and Germany
and Austria have opposed transferring banking supervisory control to supranational institutions
(Balakrishnan & Pradhan, 2025). The economic stakes in AI infrastructure are large enough to intensify
these dynamics. Investment in information-processing equipment and software, driven primarily by the AI
datacentre buildout, accounted for an estimated 92 per cent of US gross domestic product (GDP) growth
in the first half of 2025, even though this category represents only around 4 per cent of total US GDP
(Armstrong & Kim 2025). Without this contribution, US GDP would have grown at just 0.1 per cent on
an annualised basis. This creates powerful incentives for individual member states to compete for
infrastructure placement rather than accept centralised siting decisions.
Even where member states agree on the goal of scaling European compute, regulatory and permitting
timelines pose a distinct coordination challenge that depends on political will at both EU and national
levels. As discussed above, pre-AI boom US datacentre buildout typically took 18 to 24 months from site
selection to operation, while grid connection timelines in parts of Europe currently run to 7 to 10 years
(IEA 2025). The fastest confirmed recent deployment, xAI ’s Colossus cluster in Memphis, reached
operation in 122 days, but only by relying on contested workarounds: xAI hired 25 per cent of the US’s
mobile trailer cooling capacity to manage thermal loads, and used on -site gas turbines to begin operations
before formal grid connection, operating in a regulatory grey area that drew permit compliance challenges
(Reuters 2025a). Whether European member states possess the political will to streamline environmental
permitting and grid connection to a comparable degree, and whether such approaches would survive legal
challenges under existing European environmental and planning frameworks, remains uncertain.
4.3.2. Distributed training as a partial solution to coordination challenges
A distributed AI training strategy aligns with the political and economic interests of a majority of EU
member states and EFTA partners. It would avoid concentrating computing power in a handful of states
and would spread the associated benefits more widely ( energy infrastructure upgrades, foreign direct
investment, local workforce development in AI -adjacent fields, and the gradual emergence of talent and
deployment ecosystems around hosted compute, as researchers, start -ups and enterprise users locate near
priority-access infrastructure). It also aligns with the EU’s Cohesion Policy, which aims to reduce disparities
between regions and member states (European Commission 2021b). A distributed approach could extend
advanced computational infrastructure to countries with limited current AI research and deployment
capacity, supporting the policy’s stated goal of reducing intra-EU disparities.
Non-EU EFTA partners such as Switzerland and Norway would also be easier to engage through a more
distributed approach. Both countries have well- developed innovation ecosystems and significant energy
resources, and Norway in particular is already host to t he Stargate Norway project (OpenAI 2025; Nscale
2025). A distributed strategy lowers the political threshold for adding new partners because it does not
require negotiating their participation in a single concentrated facility, making it easier to incorporate non-
EU European countries into European compute initiatives.
A distributed approach also builds on existing political infrastructure. As noted in Section 4.2.2, the
EuroHPC Joint Undertaking already operates a multi-sovereign governance model: 33 participating states
coordinate on purchasing, deploying and operating high-performance computing systems, with
supercomputers hosted in locations including Finland, Spain, Slovenia and the Czech Republic. What these

4.3.3. Uncertainties and limitations
Despite this promise, a distributed approach carries its own risks and uncertainties. These range from
execution challenges in government-led coordination, to the legal complexity of operating across multiple
jurisdictions, to doubts about whether EU member states can agree on joint financing at the scale frontier
AI infrastructure actually requires.
Government-led coordination carries real execution risks. EU coordination has a well- documented track
record of extended decision-making timelines (Draghi 2024; Kirpsza 2022), and there is a real concern that
heavy public involvement adds bureaucratic fric tion without delivering proportionate results. The AI
Gigafactories programme illustrates the concern: although individual gigafactories would be smaller than
some privately built datacentres completed in a matter of months, the programme remains stuck in approval
processes years after announcement, with the formal call for proposals deferred repeatedly – from Q4 2025
to early 2026 and subsequently to summer 2026, still unpublished at the time of writing (European
Commission 2025c, 2025d, n.d.) and the original delivery timeline no longer achievable (see Section 4.2.1).
A distributed strategy only avoids this trap if the division of labour is clear: private actors should build the
datacentres themselves, with governments focusing on the enabling layer of permitting, grid connections,
and inter -site fibre (Kothuri et al. 2025) . Where that division blurs, and where public financing or
coordination is asked to do work private capital should do, distribution risks compounding the coordination
burden rather than solving it.
Two different actors face regulatory burdens in a distributed architecture, and it is worth separating them.
Site developers face heterogeneous permitting and planning regimes, much of which sits at the regional and
municipal level rather than the national or EU one. Each datacentre site requires separate environmental
review, planning approvals and compliance with local labour rules, even where EU -level frameworks exist
(IEA 2025; European Commission 2025b). The parallel grid -connection-queue advantage dis cussed in
Section 4.1.1 applies specifically to per -site connection timelines; the broader landscape of planning,
environmental review and labour compliance runs in the opposite direction, with each site adding its own
approval stack. AI developers running training workloads across those sites face a different layer of burden:
data residency and localisation requirements that bind the training operator regardless of where the physical
hardware is located. While intra-EU data transfers are protected under GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679,
Chapter V), pipelines that span EFTA partners or non -EU European countries face additional legal
complexity, including adequacy decisions and country-specific residency requirements (EDPB 2021). We
return to both issues in the policy recommendations (Section 6.2.2).
A further uncertainty sits around the financing of the public enabling layer itself. The datacentres at the
heart of a distributed strategy would be privately financed, but the inter-site fibre connections, grid upgrades
and coordinated permitting frameworks that make distribution viable do require public money and multi-
sovereign agreement. EuroHPC, the closest existing model, operates with a 2021 –2027 budget of around
€7 billion (Council Regulation (EU) 2021/1173) – substantial but still an order of magni tude below the

5. Cross-cutting risks and limitations of distributed training
Distributed training offers solutions to Europe ’s compute infrastructure shortfall, as discussed in Sections
4.1.2, 4.2.2, and 4.3.2. It also introduces a set of risks that cut across all three domains (power, compute,
politics), distinct from the domain- specific limitations covered in Sections 4.1.3, 4.2.3, and 4.3.3:
operational complexity, talent requirements, and the fact that distributed training remains unproven at
frontier scale.
5.1. Operational complexity and coordination costs
A distributed training strategy introduces operational complexity that cuts across all three of the bottlenecks
discussed in Section 4. As detailed in Sections 4.1.3 and 4.3.3, this includes the regulatory dimension –
heterogeneous national frameworks, separate utility negotiations, environmental permits, and labour -law
compliance across multiple sites. A further layer, distinct from the domain -specific limitations above, is
engineering complexity. S oftware must detect when individual sites go offline and r edistribute their
workloads to the remaining sites without losing training progress, tolerate higher rates of data loss between
sites, and keep GPUs computing while data is being transferred between sites in order to avoid idle time on
slow inter-site links. The most recent systems address several of these requirements directly. Decoupled
DiLoCo, for instance, isolates site-level failures so that remaining sites continue training, maintained 88 per
cent efficiency under aggressive simulated hardware failure s, and allows sites to join and leave a run in
progress (Douillard et al. 2026). These capabilities have, however, so far been demonstrated only within a
single company's tightly integrated software stack. Multiple experts interviewed for this report identified
these as serious barriers that companies would need to weigh carefully before adopting a distributed
approach.
A distinct source of fragility lies in the inter -site network links themselves. In a distributed architecture,
these links become critical infrastructure: disruption to the connections between sites could degrade or halt
synchronisation across the whole training run, even if every individual datacentre remains fully operational.
This cuts against the resilience benefit noted in Section 4.1.2, where a distributed system can continue with
degraded capacity while affected sites recover. Whether distribution makes a training run more or less fragile
therefore depends on the specific network topology, how many inter-site links exist, how much redundancy
is built into them, and how the training algorithm handles partial network failure. Recent damage to subsea
cables in the Baltic Sea, which the European Commission and High Representative have characterised as
part of larger hybrid campaigns targeting EU critical infrastructure, illustrates that this is not a hypothetical
concern for European cross -border infrastructure (European Commission & High Representative 2025).

Lastly, a distributed approach also sacrifices economies of scale. Centralised hyperscalers benefit from

standardised operational tooling, all of which translate into substantially lower unit costs (McKinsey 2025;

5.2. Talent requirements
Distributed training introduces talent requirements specific to the technology. Algorithms enabling training
across multiple sites and large distances draw on a specialised body of expertise, including asynchronous
optimisation, gradient compression, and i nter-site bandwidth- aware scheduling. This is concentrated
almost exclusively in US frontier AI companies and a small number of academic groups. Even when
underlying methods are published, as with DiLoCo (Douillard et al. 2023) and INTELLECT-1 (Jaghouar
et al. 2024b), the implementation details, tuning and operational know-how that make them work at scale
typically remain proprietary or undocumented.
Several experts interviewed for this report argued that the deeper challenge is not technical talent in isolation
but the absence of an ecosystem that retains it. European researchers with relevant expertise frequently move
to US labs, where the combination of compensation, compute access and career trajectory is difficult for
Europe to match. Closing the talent gap therefore requires not only training programmes but also the
institutional and financial conditions to keep specialised talent in Europe, whether at European firms or at
local operations of foreign ones, a challenge that parallels broader European concerns about brain drain in
frontier technology fields (Draghi 2024).
5.3. Unproven at frontier scale and issues in future-proofing
Distributed training has been demonstrated in smaller research trials and limited production deployments,
but concrete evidence of its use by frontier AI developers at scale remains thin. Current public
demonstrations involve a small number of datacentres and moderately sized models, well below the
parameter counts and compute volumes characterising the latest frontier models (see Table 1). Experts
caution that benchmark performance reported in research papers may obscure broader challenges in training
stability, convergence quality and real-world model behaviour that emerge only at frontier scale.
Industry behaviour, while not conclusive, does offer signals. Companies prefer centralised facilities when
power and grid constraints permit, and pursue distributed models mainly when forced to by external
constraints (Sevilla & Troynikov 2025; Patel et al. 2024). This preference suggests that, despite recent
algorithmic advances, centralised training retains significant operational and performance advantages that
frontier d evelopers thus far perceive as significant. Distributed training is, for Europe, a s econd-best
architecture that circumstance makes hard to fully avoid. The question is not whether it is optimal but
whether it is good enough to make European frontier development viable.

principle adapt to centralised configurations, but communication-heavy protocols developed for centralised

data generation and reinforcement learning fine -tuning, are relatively well -suited to distributed set- ups
because they involve compute -heavy workloads with comparatively modest inter -site commu nication

either direction, and the future trajectory of training methods remains a meaningful source of uncertainty
for any long-term distributed strategy.
5.4. Additional challenges
Beyond the limitations specific to distributed training, Europe faces broader structural challenges that apply
to any AI competitiveness strategy.
Europe lacks both the venture capital depth and the integrated capital markets to mobilise private
investment at the scale required for frontier AI infrastructure. US hyperscalers were projected in mid-2025
to deploy approximately $350 billion in capital e xpenditure in 2025 and around $400 billion in 2026
(Fusion Worldwide 2025), with individual datacentre projects increasingly in the tens of billions of dollars.
More recent estimates place the figure substantially higher, with combined 2026 capital expenditure for the
five largest hyperscalers (Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle) now forecast to exceed $600
billion, a roughly 36 per cent year-on-year increase (IEEE ComSoc Technology Blog 2025). Europe’s largest
single AI infrastructure commitment to date, the gigafactory programme, totals roughly €20 billion across
multiple sites (European Commission 2025e). Mistral’s $830 million debt financing for its Paris data centre
buildout (CNBC 2026), on top of its €1.7 billion ($2 billion) Series C led by ASML in September 2025
(Mistral AI 2025), represents the largest single-company financing round by a European AI company.
The Draghi report (2024) identifies a deeper structural problem: European savings rates are higher than
those in the US, but the absence of an integrated capital markets union prevents these savings from being
efficiently channelled into productive investment, including AI infrastructure. The result is that European
private capital that could in principle finance frontier AI buildout instead flows into less productive uses or
into US markets. Without addressing this structural gap, European-led infrastructure strategies, distributed
or otherwise, will operate under a binding capital constraint , even where foreign -financed alternatives
remain viable. This constraint cuts across each of the bottlenecks discussed in Section 4: financing power
and grid buildout, procuring frontier compute, and building the joint European- level public layer that
distributed coordination requires.
A further structural shift concerns the changing balance between training and inference in total AI compute
demand. Distributed training addresses one side of the workload, but inference is becoming the dominant
share of AI compute consumption and is expec ted to continue growing relative to training (Tanner et al.
2026). To the extent that the European compute challenge becomes primarily an inference challenge, the
relative importance of distributed training as a policy lever will diminish accordingly.

training
Europe is roughly an order of magnitude behind the United States on compute, faces serious power and
grid constraints, and is hampered by slow coordination. Closing this gap requires policies that address these
frictions and hence incentivise compute buildout on the European continent. Distributed training has a
specific role in this picture; it is the technical mechanism that allows compute spread across geographically
separated sites to function as a single resource for frontier training, and could transf orm Europe ’s
fragmented compute base into a unified pool. Infrastructure to facilitate distributed training could make
Europe a more attractive location for companies – both European and non-European – seeking to conduct
large-scale training runs.
The primary role for European policymakers is to ensure that distributed training can be done in Europe
when companies wish to do so. This requires two conditions. Firstly, mechanisms must be in place to enable
rapid distributed buildout, including efficient cross -border permitting, sufficient grid capacity, and
governance structures that can keep pace with private-sector deployment timelines. Secondly, infrastructure
that is already built or planned should be designed from the outset to support distributed training projects,
including through inter -site fibre connectivity and siting decisions that avoid unnecessary geographical
isolation. Retrofitting existing infrastructure for distributed compatibility is significantly cheaper than
building new sites from scratch, and European policy should make such retrofits as straightforward as
possible.
The recommendations that follow build on this framing. We begin with an illustrative scenario that grounds
the discussion in concrete operational terms, then turn to specific policy measures.
6.1. An illustrative scenario
Suppose Europe were to host 10 to 20 million H100 equivalents, the range identified in Section 4.2.1 as
necessary to support frontier -scale training runs at a cadence sufficient for Europe to remain a credible
participant in frontier AI development and regulation. What physical infrastructure and enabling

Translating 10 to 20 million H100 equivalents into power requirements depends on assumptions about
per-chip power draw, supporting hardware, and datacentre cooling efficiency. Recent estimates from
RAND (Pilz, Mahmood & Heim 2025) and Epoch AI (Sevilla et al. 2024) converge on approximately
1,418 W per H100 equivalent at the cluster level, which becomes approximately 1,700 W per H100
equivalent at the total facility level under a central power usage effectiveness (PUE) assumption of 1.2 (see
Annex 1). Under this assumption, 10 million H100 equivalents would require approximately 17 GW of
total facility power, and 20 million approximately 34 GW.
Two sources of uncertainty complicate this central estimate. On the one hand, next-generation hardware
will deliver substantially more compute per watt: for its Blackwell chips, for example, NVIDIA cites up to
25× lower energy per inference for large LLM workloads at the rack level (NVIDIA 2024). By the time
European infrastructure at this scale is operational, the power requirements per unit of compute could be
meaningfully lower than current estimates suggest. On the other hand, the demand -side estimates of
compute required for frontier training in 2030 and beyond also rest on multiple assumptions, and given
the delivery delays many projects are likely to experience, the compute requirements at the relevant time
horizon could equally be higher than current ly projected. Predicting how these effects will balance out is
beyond the scope of this report, but it is plausible that efficiency gains will be partially or fully offset by
rising compute demand. The 17 to 34 GW range should therefore be read as an order -of-magnitude
working estimate rather than a precise forecast.
These power requirements are the central reason for adopting at least a partially distributed approach. No
single European site, current or projected, can provide the 5 to 10 GW concentrated at one cluster that a
fully centralised approach would require: even the largest planned European facility, the 1.4 GW Campus
AI project in the Paris region (École Polytechnique 2025), falls well below this threshold. Grid connections
at the required scale face approval timelines of 7 to 10 years in many European countries (IEA 2025), and
a fully centralised approach would forgo the demand -flexibility benefits discussed in Section 4.1.2, which
could unlock tens of gigawatts of additional capacity using existing infrastructure. Smaller sites in the 300
MW to 1 GW range can connect to grids substantially faster, and parallel buildout across multiple locations
would compress the overall timeline considerably (Sevilla & Troynikov 2025; NERC 2025). A fully
centralised approach would also face political resistance from medium a nd small EU member states and
would risk excluding valuable partners such as Switzerland and Norway, as discussed in Section 4.3.2.

One plausible set -up would involve two tracks. The first would consist of 4 to 6 hyperscale clusters of
approximately 2 to 3 GW each, located where large -scale grid connections are most feasible, for example
near France’ s nuclear baseload or Nordic hydroelectric infrastructure. These provide the concentrated
compute density needed for the most bandwidth-intensive training phases and for large-scale inference.
The second track would complement these with 15 to 25 distributed sites of 500 MW to 1 GW each,
exploiting demand flexibility, faster grid connections, and Europe ’s varied energy landscape (for example
Iberian solar, North Sea wind, or geothermal sources in Iceland and Italy). Combined, the two tracks would
provide approximately 16 to 43 GW of total facility power, bracketing the 17 to 34 GW range implied by
the 10 to 20 million H100 equivalent central estimate.
During a frontier training run, the hyperscale clusters would handle the most communication -intensive
portions of the workload, while the distributed sites contribute additional compute capacity through low-
communication algorithms such as DiLoCo (Douillard et al. 2023), synchronising at intervals rather than
continuously. Between major training runs, both tracks would serve inference, fine-tuning and experimental
workloads, with the distributed sites providing demand flexibility by temporarily reducing load during grid
peaks while the hyperscale clusters maintain baseline operations. The precise allocation of workloads across
the two tracks would depend on bandwidth, training architecture and relative cost at each location, with
these decisions best left to operators rather than prescribed by policy. The scenario shows that a 17 to 34
GW European compute base is constructible by pairing a small number of hyperscale anchor sites with a
fleet of smaller distributed clusters, rather than concentrating frontier training into a single ultra -large
facility. The binding constraints are therefore primarily regulatory and infrastructural rather than
technological, which is what the policy recommendations below address.
Several caveats apply. This is one illustrative scenario, not a blueprint. Investment in energy supply,
semiconductors and talent remains equally critical. The power conversion reflects current -generation
hardware and will shift substantially as more efficient chips reach deployment. The dual-track architecture
assumes continued maturation of distributed training algorithms, which remain largely unproven at full
frontier scale. The precise split between hyperscale and distributed capacity should be determin ed by a
detailed siting assessment, which we recommend commissioning as an early priority.
6.2. Policy recommendations
The following policy recommendations focus on helping to create a landscape that is prepared for the
inclusion of a distributed training strategy into Europe’s frontier AI scaling. The recommendations centre
around two pillars: mapping and preparing physical infrastructure for distributed training, and simplifying
and harmonising cross-border regulation.
6.2.1. Map and prepare physical infrastructure for distributed training
Enabling distributed training in Europe requires the right physical preconditions. This means mapping
current infrastructure, identifying gaps, designing new infrastructure with distributed training in mind from
the outset, as well as tracking technical de velopments closely enough that policy does not lag behind the
state of the art.

Assess spare energy capacity across European grid regions. This assessment could be led by ENTSO -E
in collaboration with DG ENER and member state authorities. The purpose would be to map facilities that
operate below their grid allocations, regions with available headroom, and grid connection timelines by
jurisdiction. The assessment should also identify where flexibility in demand from operators could unlock
additional capacity.
Assess existing and required cross -border fibre infrastructure . This assessment could be led by DG
CONNECT in collaboration with member state authorities and the Body of European Regulators for
Electronic Communications (BEREC), and it should map both existing fibre capacity and the gaps that
would need to be filled to enable a pan-European distributed training architecture.
Publish a European AI Infrastructure Reference. Based on energy capacity and fibre assessments, the EU
AI Office should produce and maintain a public database that contains two components : firstly, a map of
candidate datacentre sites across Europe above 100MW, with each location tagged for the type of workload
it best supports (such as distributed training, hyperscale baseload, inference and so on) ; secondly, a query
function that allows users to filter locations by their own criteria, such as available power capacity, energy
mix and latency to other sites. The reference will serve as a guidance document for companies evaluating
Europe for frontier AI infrastructure buildouts, and as a resource for EU and member state institutions
looking to prioritise investment into enabling infrastructure for datacentres.
Make AI infrastructure easy to interconnect across Europe . EU and member state authorities should
reduce the friction of connecting AI infrastructure to existing fibre backbones through targeted deregulation
of fibre permitting and rights -of-way, and subsidies for last -mile or inter -site links where commercial
provision falls short. Co-financing institutions such as the European Investment Bank (EIB) and EuroHPC
can reinforce this by favouring projects sited near exis ting fibre backbones, without asking ope rators to
build fibre themselves.
Monitor trends in distributed training within the EU AI Office. Distributed training is a fast-developing
technical domain, and as a result associated policy recommendations may quickly become out of date. To
address this moving target problem, the EU AI Office should incorporate distributed training into its
existing technical monitoring activities, drawing on external expertise where needed rather than creating
new dedicated capacity.
6.2.2. Simplify and harmonise cross-border regulation
Companies seeking to operate AI training infrastructure across Europe currently face patchy and slow
regulatory regimes and processes, making it unattractive to develop frontier AI models via distributed
training across European countries. Reducing this burden is valuable regardless of whether distributed
training ends up being used as a main mechanism for training or not. The following actions are
recommended:
Accelerate and harmonise permitting and grid connection processes for AI infrastructure across the
continent. DG GROW and DG ENER, in cooperation with member states and the Agency for the
Cooperation of Energy Regulators (ACER) , should ensure that an AI company can go from investment
decision to construction seamlessly, so that datacentres can go online in internationally competitive
timelines.

Establish a portfolio of pre -approved candidate sites. DG ENER, DG GROW and the EU AI Office
should create ‘ pre-approved’ AI infrastructure sites, where grid connections, environmental assessments,
zoning clearances and other documentation needs are accelerated. Member state authorities and energy
operators could then execute actual site preparation within their jurisdictions. The EU AI Office should
maintain a publicly available database of these sites.
Resolve the legal issues of operating data flows between training sites. During distributed training, a

Annex A. Converting between H100 equivalents and power
capacity
This annex sets out the rough estimates used to convert our suggested 10–20 million H100 equivalents to
total facility power. They are based on two recent ly published estimates that turn out to converge closely
once their underlying assumptions are unpacked.
A.1. Source 1: RAND (Pilz, Mahmood & Heim 2025)
RAND reports a power draw of 1,419 W per H100 equivalent at the cluster level – that is, the GPU together
with supporting hardware including server, networking and other in- rack components, but excluding
datacentre power usage effectiveness (PUE) overhead such as cooling and power conversion. A PUE of 1.25
means the facility consumes 25 per cent more power than the IT hardware draws on its own. This figure is
taken from NVIDIA’ s DGX H100 SuperPOD reference design. To obtain total facility power, RAND
separately applies a PUE multiplier, using 1.25 as a near -term value and projecting improvement to 1.05
by 2030.
A.2. Source 2: Epoch AI (Sevilla et al. 2024)
Epoch AI reports a power draw of 1,700 W per H100 equivalent at the total facility level. Their derivation
is explicit: a 700 W thermal design power (TDP), the chip’s nominal sustained power draw, for the H100,
multiplied by a 1.96× cluster overhead factor (based on the DGX H100 SuperPOD server expected average
power), and then by a PUE of 1.2. Backing the PUE out yields approximately 1,417 W at the cluster level,
nearly identical to RAND’s 1,419 W.
A.3. Averaged estimate
The two sources converge on approximately 1,418 W per H100 equivalent at the cluster level. The
remaining difference between them reflects the choice of PUE multiplier rather than any disagreement
about the underlying hardware. As detailed in Table A.1, applying the central PUE assumption of 1.2 yields
a facility -level figure of approximately 1,700 W per H100 equivalent, which we adopt as our central
estimate; for sensitivity, we also report figures under PUE = 1.25 (near -term) and PUE = 1.1 (improved
efficiency by 2030).

A.4. Caveats
This conversion is subject to several factors including utilisation rates, cooling technology, and next -
generation power-efficient chips (NVIDIA Blackwell delivers roughly 35 per cent greater power efficiency
than the H100, with Rubin expected to deliver a comparable improvement). By the time European
infrastructure at this scale is operational, per -compute power requirements could be meaningfully lower.
On the other hand, demand -side estimates of compute required for frontier training in 2030 also rest on
multiple assumptions, and could be higher than currently projected. The figures given above should be read
as order-of-magnitude working estimates rather than precise forecasts.
