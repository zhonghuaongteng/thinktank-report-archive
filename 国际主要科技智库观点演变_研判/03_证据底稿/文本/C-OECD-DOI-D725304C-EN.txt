Abstract
This study provides a comprehensive understanding of how Korea
implements mission-oriented innovation policies (MOIPs), and offers
guidance on how to design, co-ordinate, and implement MOIPs in the
country.
As part of a series of MOIP national re views, the paper finds that Korea has
made significant efforts to embrace a mission-oriented approach, setting
several overarching MOIP frameworks and specific MOIP initiatives. This
work builds upon a long history of central co-ordination of STI policies
implemented in a number of STI and sectoral public bodies. MOIPs in
Korea are the latest attempt to provide STI with more direction in order to
prioritise technological development that both benefits Korean society and
drive competitiveness. The study concludes with options for change,
including by simplifying prioritisation exercises for sectors and technologies
across government, and exploring how the government can better support
its societal missions to drive more transformative outcomes.

Executive summary
This review, undertaken as part of the OECD Committee for Scientific and Technological Policy (CSTP)
work on mission- oriented innovation policies (MOIPs), aims to i) identify the key elements that foster or
hinder the development and implementation of MOIPs to tackle societal challenges in Korea; ii) review
past and on-going Korean MOIPs; iii) provide evidence- based conclusions and options for improving the
adoption and implementation of this policy approach in the future, building on the Korean STI system’ s
distinctive strengths, and learning from past mission experiences in Korea and internationally.
Does Korea have specific features that are conducive to the design and
implementation of MOIPs?
The Korean science, technology and innovation (STI) system does have specific features that are
conducive to a MOIP approach . The OECD Reviews of Korean Innovation Policy (OECD, 2023 [1])
provided a detailed overview of the evolution of Korean STI governance, which is aligned with the three
dimensions MOIPs. Korea strategically engages an array of public and private actors in agenda setting,
leveraging its history of strategic agendas that direct government support toward specific technologies,
sectors, and societal challenges. Advanced tools like foresight, big data analytics, and citizen sentiment
analysis are employed to identify priorities, though its agendas have primarily focused on technological
priorities. Korea has established multiple institutions to coordinate long-term STI priorities, align plans
across ministries and agencies, and implement joint cross-ministerial initiatives. This coordination is led by
the Presidential Advisory Council on Science and Technology (PACST), which reports directly to the
President, while the Ministry of Science and ICT’s (MSIT) STI Office supports the process, coordinating
STI policies across government. For policy implementation, Korea has a comprehensive set of
innovation policies covering different sectors and stages of the innovation cycle, with interministerial
programmes addressing common challenges. More recently, Korea has adopted challenge- oriented
programmes to foster high-risk, high-reward research, and recent updates to STI monitoring and evaluation
practices should further enable systemic evaluations that consider the collective impact of various
programmes and policies.
Does Korea implement MOIPs?
Korea has launched a series of MOIPs to prioritise technological development in a way that aims to
combine traditional economic priority setting with addressing societal goals. This study focuses on three
programmes that represent the different types of MOIPs Korea is using: the 3 rd Comprehensive Plan to
Solve Social Problems Based on Science and Technology (2023- 27) (CP3), the National Strategic
Technology Policy (NSTP) and the Alchemist. Other MOIPs in the Korean policy mix include the Carbon-
Neutral Strategic Technology Policy (CNSTP), the Fine Dust Management Comprehensive Plan, the
Future Challenge Technology Development Project, ARPA-H, and the Challenging the Limit Programme.

• Strategic orientation - The NSTP, CNSTP and CP3 are overarching MOIPs, which hav e

• Policy co -ordination - Korea's MOIPs are going further than traditional STI co- ordination
mechanisms (mainly efficiency -driven and focused on how to avoid duplication) by effectively
bringing public and private actors together to identify gaps that need to be addressed to achieve
the missions. This co- ordination happens at committees specific to each mission under the CP3
and for each strategic technology with the NSTP, which are comprised of relevant stakeholders
from government, industry, and academia.
• Policy implementation - These MOIPs mobilise a diverse set of policy levers to achieve th e

procurement for innovation. This wide variety of tools is needed to tackle the complex cha llenges
each mission identifies. The governance and implementation following the programme approval

towards the goals through a dedicated portfolio management approach. Some of the MOIPs have
less certainty regarding how much funding their missions will receive, while others have national
funds earmarked to them over several years and are prioritised during the annual budget process

Of the MOIPs this paper studied, only the Alchemist’s has had time to fully offer its programming over
several years. Its challenge- oriented approach has been recognised as a successful programme by
Korean authorities and other ministries are seeking to replicate it. The Alchemist is focused on technology
development for improving society through a portfolio of research projects. It is more focused on R&D than
the other two MOIPs. The latter plan to take more systemic approaches to tackle national missions that
would inspire and engage public and private actors across all departments and sectors. It is too early to
assess the added value of these other initiatives in the Korean STI policy landscapes.
What could be next steps for MOIPs in Korea?
These policies have added more directionality to the Korean innovation system and are helping to
encourage more transformative technological progress and societal change. To help embrace the
transformative nature of missions beyond the strong foundation in place, the OECD has identified options
for change: The overall mission -oriented approach should involve setting a small number of
transformative missions for societal challenges with clear, inspiring, and powerful directions at the highest
level of government to galvanize national support. To strategically orient current missions, Korea
should raise the profile of societal missions to encourage greater support and enable a more systemic
approach at the national level, while also creating more coordinated priority -setting among its various
ARPA-style challenge programmes to complement the overarching mission- oriented framework. For
implementing current missions, Korea should establish a dedicated portfolio management structure to
provide proactive and holistic steering of mission- oriented activities, provide flexible, dedicated, multiyear
funding to unlock their transformative potential, and align STI support across government to enhance the
likelihood of mission success. Additionally, pathways to market should be developed in current and future
challenge-oriented programmes to maximize the impact of developed technologies. Finally, mission
monitoring and evaluation processes should be adopted to capture specific systemic effects and assess
added value compared to traditional STI policies.

The work on mission -oriented innovation policy (MOIP) was launched by the OECD Committee for
Scientific and Technological Policy (CSTP) in 2019. It aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of
how governments try to address societal challenges through various types of MOIPs in different national
and thematic contexts, and offers options for change on how to improve these policies based on research
of international cases.1
Using a dedicated methodological framework, this strand of work conducts two main types of fieldwork
activities:
1. Systematic investigations: identification and characterisation of MOIP initiatives in all OECD
countries and beyond; and
2. In-depth investigations: thorough analysis of mission- oriented policies in a sample of voluntary
countries through national reviews of MOIPs.
To date, four countries have engaged in a partnership with the OECD to conduct a MOIP national review :
Austria, Japan, Korea and Norway.
The present document sets out the results of the Korean review of MOIPs. The main objectives of this
review are to:
• identify and analyse the institutional factors that may foster or impede the design a nd
i
mplementation of MOIPs for societal challenges in Korea.
• systematically scan through the Korean policy landscape to identify and characterise initiatives that
match – to a greater or lesser extent – the MOIP’ definition.
• provide a series of options for change to inform future policy decisions regarding Korean MOIPs.
The final goal of the review is to identify the opportunities for and challenges to mission- orientation in
Korea, derive corresponding conclusions and options for change , and contribute to discussions on how
Korea could, if deemed necessary, strengthen its mission-oriented policy. This should help Korea to more
effectively address societal challenges and meet national priorities.

The definition of MOIPs
The definition of MOIPs used in this project builds upon an extensive literature review and is consistent
with the main research works conducted in this area ( (European Commission, 2018 [2]), (European
Commission, 2018[3]), and (Mazzucato, 2018[4])). A MOIP is defined as a co-ordinated package of policy
and regulatory measures tailored specifically to mobilise science, technology and innovation in order to
address well-defined objectives related to a societal challenge, in a defined timeframe. These measures
possibly span different stages of the innovation cycle from research to demonstration and market
deployment, mix supply-push and demand-pull instruments, and cut across various policy fields, sectors
and disciplines (Larrue, 2021
[5]).
This definition can be broken down to isolate three main types of functions that MOIPs are expected to
fulfil: strategic orientation, policy co-ordination and policy implementation (see Figure 2.1).

3 Framework conditions for mission-

T
he MOIP framework conditions systematically define the national factors that influence the need for and
ability of a country to design, fund and implement MOIPs. This chapter will use the three dimensions that
lie at the core of MOIPs (strategic orientation, policy co-ordination and policy implementation) to explore
how conducive Korea’s existing STI system is to pursue a mission -oriented approach. The main
underpinning hypothesis is that the forms and types of MOIPs in a given country can only be understood
within:
• the national institutional setting in which they are embedded and that largely determines what is
politically, administratively, and socially feasible at one point in time. Countries differ for instance
in their capabilities to steer, fund and implement complex policies (such as MOIPs); and
• the policy trajectory that has gradually formed them, moving forward through experimentation,
negotiation, and learning. These trajectories are driven by several factors, including the general
policy stance toward state intervention, the respective choice of policy instruments (e.g. , generic
vs. specific measures).

Strategic orientation
Korea’s STI policymaking process seems to lend itself well to a mission-oriented approach. Korea uses
central agendas for identifying both technological and sectoral priorities, an approach that has mission-
oriented characteristics. This is epitomised by a series of nested strategies that guide Korean STI policy :
a 10-year long-term vision, the presidential programme agenda, the five- year S&T Basic Plan, the mid-to
long-term National R&D Investment Strategy, and annual plans for each ministry. Ad-hoc strategies (e.g.,
focused on technologies) complement this work. Korea embeds many of these strategies in legislation that
requires them to be updated on regular cycles, which helps continue this work beyond political election
cycles. Similar legislation has been used to ground the mission- oriented policies discussed in Chapter 4
(e.g., the CP3 and NSTP, which both must be updated every five years).
Box 3.1 . Korean green strategies
Korea has had two overarching green strategies with mission- oriented qualities: the Green Growth
Strategy5 ( 2008) and the 2050 Carbon Neutrality Strategy 6 (2020) (Eum, 2023[7]).
The 2008 strategy set out the government’s vision to become one of the top 7 green superpowers in
the world by 2020 and amongst the top 5 by 2050. This work was underpinned by the Framework Act
on Low-Carbon Green Growth 7 (2010). An extensive governance system oversaw cross -government
implementation, headed by the Presidential Committee for Green Growth and the Green Growth
Planning Office, and then ‘Chief Green Officers’ were created within each ministry to facilitate
communication between ministries and committees. The Green Growth Strategy was accompanied by
three directions, with 10 specific tasks that were largely STI focused (Green Growth Committee,
2009[8]).

Policy implementation
Korea’s STI policies are mainly implemented in the form of R&D programmes by around 15- 17 dedicated
R&D agencies , which act on behalf of ministries . A gencies can be mandated to allocate funding and
manage projects (R&D management agencies), while others perform a range of other actions to support
R&D and innovation (R&D promotion agencies). In some cases, the difference between the agency roles
is unclear, with some agencies playing both.
An important aspect of Korean innovation policy is the conditionality it places on its funding. Technologies
that are commercialised from government -funded R&D projects are charged technology fees. This helps
“maintain a virtuous cycle of technology development” by reinvesting the fees in further research and
development through a quasi-dividend arrangement (Related Joint Ministers, 2024
[19]).
Korea has the second- highest R&D intensity among OECD countries ; its gross domestic expenditure on
R&D (GERD) represented 5.21% of GDP in 2022 (OECD, 2024[20]). It is also among the OECD countries
that provide the largest level of total government support to business R&D (BERD) as a percentage of
GDP, at a rate of 0. 35% of GDP in 2021 (OECD, 2024[21]). The allocation of public funds is rooted in the
historical context of a country, including the pace of development, industrial structure, and government
policies. A large portion of Korea's government appropriations dedicated to R&D is thematically oriented
(76% of the total (direct) funding for R&D in 2022, in comparison to 84% in the United States, 65% in
Japan, and 48% in Germany). The country dedicates a relatively small proportion of its government R&D
budget to projects and activities related to health and society issues due to its historical need to invest
more in defence. In 2021, 14% of R&D funds were allocated to health and society, compared to 28% in
Norway, 25% in the United States and 22% in Germany (OECD, 2024
[20]).

priorities has been inter -ministerial programmes that combine resources and capabilities from across
government to tackle common challenges.

The Comprehensive Plan to Solve Social Problems Based on Science and
Technology
The 3rd Comprehensive Plan to Solve Social Problems Based on Science and Technology (2023-27) (CP3)
is an overarching mission-oriented innovation policy that aims to realise a happy society by solving social
problems that matter most to Koreans through science and technology.
It is rooted in the President ial Advisory Council on Science and Technology's 2006 report Technology-
based Quality of Life Enhancement Plan for Vision 2030, which proposed: (1) diagnosing the current
situation; (2) linking the expansion of supply of technology to demand; (3) developing technology and
building infrastructure to improve Korea's quality of life; (4) establishing technology support system for the
socially disadvantaged; and (5) preparing comprehensive policy support plans (STI Office, 2006[24]). A first
attempt at this approach was the 2007 initiative called Comprehensive Measures to Improve the Quality of
Life Based on Technology, which, along with the first two iterations of the Comprehensive Plan to Solve
Social Problems Based on Science and Technology, encouraged ministries across government to prioritise
R&D projects that address specific social problems and sub-priorities for each problem.
This progression in Korea's policy approach reflects a broader pattern seen in mission-oriented innovation
policies globally. The degree to which policies adhere to the three dimensions of mission orientation (i.e.,
strategic orientation, policy co-ordination and policy implementation) often grows over time for mission -
oriented innovation policies around the world. This gradual evolution in Korea from one Comprehensive
Plan to the next follows this pattern, as it has gained more concrete goals (now in the form of missions) ,
formal governance, and tools to deliver on its ambitions of addressing social problems. The five-year S&T
Basic Plan (2023-27) highlights the CP3 as a tool for encouraging public participation and public -private
cooperation to tackle core social problems (MSIT, 2022[13]). The National Research and Development Mid-
to Long-Term Investment Strategy (2023- 2027) also mentions addressing social problems, but does not
mention all of the CP3’s mission areas and prioritises social problems the CP3 has not set missions for
(e.g., disaster safety) (MSIT, 2023[14]). While it has not received the same prominence as other mission-
oriented policies (e.g., the NSTP and CNSTP) in these core documents, including the CP3 in these
documents demonstrates it is still a priority and that Korea is dedicated to delivering on it and addressing
social problems.

missions has a Flagship Programme Planning Committee to coordinate R&D programmes to address the
mission targets. These committees are chaired by a champion ministry (e.g., the Ministry of Health and
Welfare is the lead ministry for bridging the digital divide for the elderly), and their memberships include
relevant ministries and agencies, a representative from the Public-Private Council, and technical and non-
technical experts (including from government research institutes). MSIT work s with these committees to
support the planning of inter -ministerial R&D programmes that align with the roadmap for each mission.
Additionally, the committees serve as consultative bodies to coordinate matters among participating
ministries. It has been more difficult to have ministries commit to supporting the missions through non-R&D
measures to date, but these measures are part of the roadmaps and will be more important as the missions
mature.
MSIT co-ordinates the funding of R&D for the CP3 like it does for every other R&D program. Korea does
not make multi-year budgeting decisions but goes through an annual process described in section 3.2 and
in Annex A in more detail. As part of the broader budgeting process, sectoral ministries submit their R&D -
related proposals to the STI Office for review which, amongst other factors, makes funding
recommendations based on how the proposals are linked to government priorities – including the CP3.
Sectoral ministries develop their proposals based on their own strategic priorities, including the
commitments they make through the CP3’s Flagship Programme Planning Committees. The budget for
the CP3 decreased from 2023 to 2024, but government investment to address the social problems had
been growing since 2018. While the implementation plans for the CP3 do not explicitly identify connections
to the NSTP, many social problems have the potential to utili se and benefit from the technologies being
fostered as part of the NSTP (e.g., social problems such as chronic diseases, rare and intractable diseases,
mental illnesses, and infectious diseases may benefit from advanced bio technologies, a National Strategic
Technology, and sub-technologies including synthetic biology, gene and cell therapies, and digital health
data analysis and utilization).
The Public-Private Council for Social Problem Solving also allows local governments to participate, who
can make proposals aimed at addressing their needs. To enable this participation, the Plan proposes to
work with 10 existing regional problem- solving platforms – which are private sector -led with local
government involvement. These platforms aim to identify and solve regional social problems.
In addition, ‘hub institutions’ (a university or a government -funded research institute) will be identified to
accumulate multidisciplinary research results from government -funded research for each of the core
problems. These hubs will then disseminate the research and encourage co- operation by government,
local government, researchers, companies, and citizens.
The Plan also emphasises the importance of international co-operation for solving some social problems
(e.g., transboundary environmental pollutants and radioactive contamination). As a result, it will expand
international joint research and development projects, and increase bilateral or multilateral co- operation.

Council released the NSTP Basic Plan, which announced an annual implementation plan will be delivered

the technological capabilities to advance each sub- technology. Sub-committees for each of the 12 sub-
technologies can recommend when their roadmaps need to be updated (KISTEP, 2023[27]).

Policy co-ordination
The missions aim to build an inclusive process throughout their lifecycle, and include the participation of
experts from government, industry, academia, research institutes, and civil society in mission setting, R&D
planning, and evaluation. This is formalised through a series of committees.
The National Strategic Technology Special Committee – a special committee under the PACST
Deliberative Council – oversees the NSTP. The Special Committee, and 12 sub- committees focused on
each strategic technology, are comprised of implicated government ministries, diverse experts from
academia, research institutes, and industries. The main committee is chaired by the Vice Minister of
Science and Technology Innovation Office.
The R&D and non-R&D elements of the roadmap are co-ordinated by the STI Office, which promotes co-

academia and the private sector.
The STI Office also uses the budget process to coordinate the programmes from across government meant

Box 4.3 . Co-ordinating missions with structural funding
The US’s Energy Earthshots Initiative was launched in 2021 by the United States. Department of Energy
(DOE) with the aim of catalysing innovative advancements in various energy sector technologies to
significantly reduce emissions in the U nited States and bring down the costs of select energy
technologies. To achieve these reductions, eight Energy Earthshots have been established. They aim
to achieve cost reduction and technical goals over approximately a ten-year period.
The Earthshots’ policy mix includes research at DOE federal labs; funding of university, not -for-profit,
and private research teams; funding the commercialisation of DOE research; challenge prizes ;
innovation for procurement; regulatory and permitting support; and technical assistance. Each
Earthshot has a co-ordination team of both technical experts and leads from diverse teams that oversee
investment tools within DOE. A science and innovation programme manager oversees each Earthshot
co-ordination team, and the portfolio of projects and investments to tackle the mission.
The Earthshots co-ordinate with other initiatives depending on their priorities, and other strategies that
affect their technology. For instance, the Hydrogen Shot has a joint strategy team under a broader
Hydrogen Strategy with two co-chairs – one representing the DOE team responsible for the Earthshots
and one from the DOE team responsible for infrastructure. This approach helps co-ordinate the various
initiatives to support building out the infrastructure to deploy the technologies developed by the
Earthshot. For example, t he Bipartisan Infrastructure Law included $9.5 billion for clean hydrogen
initiatives and the Inflation Reduction Act creates a new Hydrogen Production Tax Credit . These
initiatives all will advance the Hydrogen Shot and help bring the technologies it develops to market.
Another example is the Floating Offshore Wind Shot, which has taken an inter -agency approach,
working alongside the Department of Transportation and Department of the Interior to set a roadmap
and manage the Earthshot. This helps bring together the relevant regulatory reforms and accelerated
permitting approvals that are needed to deploy the technologies developed by DOE’s work.
Policy implementation
The NSTP mainly supports R&D to advance the mission areas, but the NSTP Basic Plan and roadmaps
identify a range of other programmes including skills (including training but also easing visa requirements
and immigration procedures) , commercialisation, entrepreneurship support, clusters, industry-academia
networking, innovation procurement, standard setting, and regulatory changes.
To implement the roadmaps, sectoral ministries are responsible for using their existing programmes, and
developing new ones, to support the roadmaps. This includes R&D, but also the other types of programmes
listed above. Government research institutes will also conduct R&D on core technologies. Funding for
projects with private sector involvement will be largely based on the existing regulations, which has a R&D
matching ratio of 50% for large companies, 30% for mid-sized companies, and 25% for small and medium-
sized companies. The private investment ratio can be adjusted based on industry, field, scope of benefits,
TRL, and spillover effects for the national economy. For instance, Strategic Research and Development
projects, including flagship projects, can incentivize private investment through special benefits, such as
reducing the R&D matching ratio for companies, lowering royalty fees, and providing support for follow-up
projects.

The Alchemist
The Alchemist is a high- risk high-reward challenge-based programme that encourages the development
of technologies in areas that have the dual purpose of improving Korean society and creating new sources
of global leadership. It also seeks to tackle some of the weaknesses of the Korean R&D system, in
particular, the relatively low appetite for pursuing risky projects (OECD, 2023[1]). It is delivered by MOTIE’s
Korea Evaluation Institute of Industrial Technology (KEIT).
As discussed in Chapter 3, Korea’s policy suite is undergoing a broad shift towards more challenge- led
programmes. In May 2019, the MSIT convened a ministerial conference on science and technology
development where relevant ministries agreed on the Measures to Strengthen National R&D Innovation,
and the Alchemist Programme was nominated as a pilot project. It was one of several similar programmes
that were piloted, including the Global-Future Innovation Research Science Technology project (G-FIRST),
a colla borative programme by MSIT and MOTIE, and the Innovation Challenge Programme, another
ARPA-style approach from MSIT (see Annex H for information on this program) . However, both of these
programmes failed their pre-feasibility tests.
The Alchemist's initial success has inspired other programmes that plan to use its methodology – notably
the Defence Administration's Future Challenge Technology Development Project, the Ministry of Health
and Welfare's ARPA -H and MSIT's Challenging the Limit Programme. Building on the success of the
Alchemist Project, KEIT is currently planning follow -up challenge- oriented R&D initiatives, including
projects like the Purple Ocean Project and the (tentatively named) Global Paradigm- Shifting Project.
Strategic orientation
The Alchemist programme sets themes, and then it supports a series of projects under each theme through
a stage-gated approach – starting with six projects in year one of a theme, three projects in year two, and
then one project is funded for five years. The Alchemist has set 19 themes so far (see Annex G). It will
develop approximately three or four themes each year until 2025. Each theme is funded over a seven-year
time-horizon. It has a budget of 414.2 billion won over 10 years (~280 million euros), starting in 2022, which
will allow it to fund all of the stage gating for its final round of themes.
The programme uses a ‘moving target approach’, where each project under the theme is planned initially
for seven years, but the projects are allowed to change as long as they still align with the themes. The
programme also allows teams to merge if they find their research is converging, or for early termination if
it is deemed that the project is not able to achieve its goal.
To set the themes, KEIT conducts desk research on the current state of technologies, and then what the
future societal challenges will be. It also uses its big data platform to explore quantitative trends behind

This work culminates in 20- 30 draft themes. These draft themes are presented to the Alchemist

Box 4.5 . Overarching MOIPs using challenge-oriented policies in their policy mix
The United Kingdom’s 2017 Industrial Strategy identified four overarching ' grand challenges' for the
United Kingdom, with an accompanying set of targeted and timebound missions under the grand
challenge areas. For instance, under the grand challenge related to ageing society, it set a mission to
“ensure that people can enjoy at least five extra healthy, independent years of life by 2035, while
narrowing the gap between the experience of the richest and poorest ”. This now withdrawn strategy
was an overarching mission-oriented innovation policy.
The grand challenges were supported by a suite of initiatives. The healthy ageing mission established
a Longevity Council to help the U nited Kingdom seize the economic opportunities of ageing societies ,
funded healthcare innovations through the UKRI’s Strategic Priorities Fund, and launched a joint United
Kingdom–Japan competition to support British and Japanese businesses to harness AI and robotic
assisted living products.
Each mission was also supported by the Industrial Strategy Challenge Fund (now the UKRI Challenge
Fund), which is a more challenge- oriented policy and its work has continued even after the grand
challenges were withdrawn. For the Challenge Fund, a challenge director oversees a suite of
collaborative research projects between businesses and other firms or academia . It set 23 challenges,
three of which were created for the ageing society challenge:
• Accelerating detection of disease - Building a national resource of volunteers to support th e
development of new diagnostic tools and technologies
• Healthy ageing - Developing products and services that help people remain independent a nd
active into older age
• Data to early diagnosis and precision medicine - Supporting the development of precisi on
medicine for improved early diagnosis and treatment
This nested structure, where an overarching mission —oriented innovation policy uses a challenge-
oriented policy to help in the delivery , is also being used in the United States’ Energy Earthshots
Initiative. Here the United States Advanced Research Projects Agency–Energy (ARPA-E) (a challenge-
based organisation) advances high-risk, high-reward research under the mission.
Policy implementation
Non-profits or research organisations are leaders for each of the themes to oversee the work from a
technical perspective. The participants in the programme are found by the research agencies. Themes are
managed by a dedicated a group of about three Project Managers who oversee the projects step- by-step
and review their progress. Individual organisations and consortia then apply for funding, which are often
led by non- profits or research organisations. A company must be involved in every project team to
encourage commercialisation, and technology transfer of the intellectual property they create. There is a
suggested government cost sharing rate for the participants: large companies (<50%), medium- sized
companies (70%), small companies (<75%), and research institutions (<100%). The cash ratio of
contributions should be not less than 30% for all types of companies. There are additional incentives for

public research institutions should invest.
KEIT selects the winning projects for each round of the contest, based on the advice of the Grand

other forms of support from MOTIE instead – including for commercialisation or further R&D. KEIT operates
its own programmes to encourage follow -on R&D and commerciali sation from technologies developed
under the programme.
The Alchemist’s slogan is “we accept failure”, as it wants researchers to take risks and not be afraid of
failure if they do not succeed or do not make it to the final round of funding. This approach is in contrast to
other R&D programmes delivered by MOTIE, where there are penalties for researchers who do not achieve
their goals – such as having to repay part of the funding they received – which discourages researchers
from pursuing ambitious and high-risk projects.
For all the project teams, KEIT provides IP support, including patent lawyers to carry out analysis on global
patent trends, and to coach each project on how to increase the value of their IP. It also offers networking
between the members to encourage the dissemination of ideas, and once a year the participating
researchers and members under each theme hold a forum to talk about the project .
The Alchemist has a special membership programme, where companies who do not participate in the R&D
projects directly can monitor their progress for free through the first two stages of the programme, and then
pay a subscription fee at the third stage to gain priority access to the IP. These members sometimes also
provide facilities to the research teams. The membership system encourages competitors to be involved
in like-minded projects, and both benefit at the end.
For the programme at large, the stage-gated approach builds in opportunities to monitor progress towards
the goal. The Evaluation Committee evaluates results at each stage of the stage-gated funding, deliberates
and then adjusts the scale, budget, and timeline for each theme. Instead of determining if a project is a
success or failure based achieving the goal alone, a new evaluation system was established to factor in
industrial impact (PACST, 2021[31]).
The programme has been such a successful model for supporting innovation to date that other ministries
and regional governments are creating their own versions. KEIT is helping them by sharing their corporate
documents.

5 Conclusions and options for the
future of the mission-oriented policy
agenda in Korea
Korea’s STI institutional setting is conducive to mission-oriented policies
Korea has a long- established strategic agenda setting model for giving its STI system direction, which
includes 10-year, five-year, and annual STI action plans, as well as sector- and technological-specific ones.
This approach already provides Korean innovators with a level of directionality that is above and beyond
what many countries offer. It is clear by the sheer number of mission- oriented programmes , and more
technology-driven challenge- oriented ones, that the country is committed to providing even more
directionality to its innovation system. In particular, a number of mission-oriented plans have been put in
place, particularly since 2018, to prioritise technological development that benefits Korean society and
drive competitiveness . While Korea has several different MOIPs, this study focuses on three: the 3rd
Comprehensive Plan to Solve Social Problems Based on Science and Technology (2023- 27) (CP3), the
National Strategic Technology Policy (NSTP), and the Alchemist (highlighted in Chapter 4). This provides
a representative sample of the different types of MOIPs Korea is using. Annex I summarises the main
characteristics of these MOIPs.
Leading foresight capabilities are another strength in the Korean STI system, which helps the government
to identify what issues matter most to Koreans, and what technology and societal trends are emerging,
which may in turn require a government response. The use of big data analytics for identifying thematic
areas under the Alchemist program me, and the use of citizen sentiment analysis for identifying priority
social problems under the CP3, are two examples of how this is already being applied in a MOIP context.
By taking such an evidence- based approach to these priority -setting exercises, Korea’s priorities are
sometimes able to transcend political cycles, where other countries again have struggled to maintain
momentum from one election to another. Again, the CP3 is a testament to this, where only four of the 43
social problems are new under the 3rd plan compared to the 2nd (see Annex C).
In addition, the STI Office’s strong co-ordinating role, including in the budgeting process, ensures there is
a moderate level of coherence across the government’s STI policies, and R&D projects. While it has
traditionally focused on how to prevent duplication of STI supports across government, MOIPs are
providing new opportunities for the government to collaborate on identifying and addressing gaps in the

STI support syst em to help achiev e their missions. Thi s co-ordination has bee n formalised throug h new
committees overseeing each MOIP initiative, and then at each mission.
The MOIPs have mapped these programmes to strategic roadmaps on how they plan to reach each
mission area. This includes a diverse set of policy levers, including R&D investments in key areas, skills
development, regulatory sandboxes, and procurement for innovation. N ew programmes that address
missions under the NSTP and CNSTP have been prioritised through Korea’s budgeting process, which
has mobilise d government- wide resources . In addition to funding for R&D , these mission- oriented
innovation policies are deploying a range of policy instruments that even initiatives within Korea could learn
from each other. This includes c hanging the rules behind the funding (i.e., removing technology fees and
not penalising failure), and using more demand- side instruments (e.g., using existing procurement of
innovation programmes or granting regulatory sandboxes for technologies developed through some of the
MOIPs). The Alchemist’s challenge-oriented approach has also been recognised as a successful approach
for driving technological advancement to address societal challenges. In contrast to the overarching-
MOIPs, it is more focused purely on technology development. It uses a stage- gated approach to funding
and better embraces failure than traditional STI programmes. Finally, recent changes to monitoring and
evaluation of STI policies is also allowing the government to consider the collective impact from
programmes, as work towards missions is the sum of many parts.
Options for change
Korea has engaged in reforms and strategic initiatives that have embedded MOIPs in its national system
of innovation. Notably, it has used the mission- oriented NSTP and the CNSTP as central pillars for the
five-year S&T Basic Plan, and included them in its nested set of strategies to deliver S&T programming. In
addition, the CP3 has a mission layer to concentrate work on addressing five core societal issues , which
goes beyond what previous iterations took to provide directionality . The CP3 is also included in the five -
year S&T Basic Plan. This work is in addition to a series of ARPA-type challenge programs the government
has launched to drive ambitious technological change (e.g., the Alchemist launching challenges on areas
such as reverse aging).
These policies have added more directionality to the Korean innovation system and are helping to
encourage more transformative technological progress and societal change. At the same time, they are all
relatively new and will require time to establish themselves before being evaluated in depth.
To help embrace the transformative nature of missions beyond the strong foundation in place, the OECD
proposes one overarching recommendation for setting future missions in the mid- to long- term
(Recommendation 1) and several additional recommendations to improve existing missions in Korea in
the coming years (Recommendations 2 to 8). The achievement and lessons learned in the second set of
shorter-term recommendations will set the conditions for the implementation of the Recommendation 1.

challenges such as global warming (IEA, 2023[33]), wider changes that combine technological, behavioural
and societal changes, will be necessary to successfully conduct the necessary transitions and create

Anchoring whole-of-government, transformative, missions above the traditional STI strategic and policy
frameworks such as Basic Plan is a challenge in many countries. Options include creating an executive
body at the centre of government (e.g. missions led by the SGPI under the Prime minister office in France)
or mandating a high- level cross -ministerial body (e.g. missions managed by a high -level Committee in
Japan, with strong support from the Cabinet Office). The United-Kingdom has established five overarching
national missions to drive the interventions of its “mission-driven government” elected in July 2024. A cross-
ministerial platform taking responsibility of the missions, based on their broader remit and stronger
systemic responsibilities across the government structure, a dedicated agency linked to several ministries
or a large public-private partnership/platform or an autonomous organisation with foundation status could
also lead such missions (Larrue, Tõnurist and Jonason, 2024
[34]).
Strategic orientation of current missions
Recommendation 2. Raise the profile of Korea’s societal missions to encourage more support for
them and allow for a more systemic approach at the national level. Korea has a well -established set
of processes and strategies for identifying government STI priorities and encouraging sectoral ministries
to align their supports – notably the 5-year STI Basic Plan , the R&D Medium- to Long-term Investment
Strategy, and the annual programme review and budgeting process. The NSTP and CNSTP are prioritised
in these processes and strategies. Furthermore, their integrated governance and mission- oriented
roadmaps builds even more coordination towards their priorities than traditional policies. This approach
provides these policies with a high degree of status and ensures there is buy -in to deliver on their
accompanying technology priorities from across government. The CP3 does not have the same profile,
and sometimes faces challenges to build the same level of buy -in as these other MOIPs. While countries
have different perspectives on the importance of policies elevating the CP3 to be at the core of the five-
year S&T Basic Plan and prioritised through its budgeting processes (e.g., the five-year Investment
Strategy), alongside the NSTP and CNSTP, would further encourage buy -in to achieve transformative
societal change.
Elevating the status of the CP3 missions will be particularly instrumental in the lead up to the definition of
the whole-of-government missions proposed in the Recommendation 1.
Recommendation 3. Create more co -ordinated priority setting amongst its various ARPA -style
challenge programmes to complement the overarching mission- oriented frameworks. Some other
countries have used a nested approach where challenge- oriented programmes have supported broader
overarching mission-oriented innovation programmes (e.g., ARPA-E being one tool supporting the Energy
Earthshots in the US). The various ARPA-style programmes Korea has underway and under development
could look to align some of their technology priorities with that of the NSTP, CNSTP, and CP3. While the
aim is not to align them perfectly, this could help leverage areas for complementarity and would bring more
consistency overall.

Implementation of current missions
Recommendation 4. Establish a dedicated portfolio management structure and related practices to
provide proactive and holistic steering of set of activities contributing to the missions. Missions are
similar to programmes, as they have boundaries regarding what projects and initiatives are helping achieve
their goals. ARPA -style challenge programmes (e.g., the Alchemist) have narrower boundaries with a
programme managers assigned to each challenge area to manage the portfolio of projects. This role
involves monitoring progress towards collectively -developed roadmaps, directing resources towards
promising lines of research and away from those with less promise, and identifying complementarities
between projects so they can collaborate to achieve the mission goals. Portfolio management for
overarching mission- frameworks requires similar levels of oversight but at a much larger scale. The
governance and implementation following the programme approval stage in Korea could go further in
ensuring projects and initiatives are more aligned towards the goals through a dedicated portfolio
management approach. This hands-on support aligning, monitoring and steering activities to advance the
mission can build momentum and help bring more coherence to individual programmes and projects that
are often monitored individually across different ministries.
Recommendation 5. Provide societal missions with flexible, dedicated, multiyear funding to
embrace their transformative potential. In providing a dedicated, multi -year budget for missions ,
governments demonstrate their priorities. This provides stakeholders with confidence to invest in these
areas. MOIPs also require strategic and operational flexibility in how they fund projects and initiatives within
their portfolio, as the path to achieve their mission goals may evolve and require different types of
interventions over time. Some countries (e.g., France 2030 – see box 3.2) use a central pot of funding that
can be distributed to various activities (R&D and non -R&D activities) in support of the missions across
different missions and ministries (horizontally), as well as other funding for the work of specific missions
and ministries (e.g., vertically). A case for a mix of vertical and horizontal funding was made in Korea’s 5th
Science and Technology Basic Plan 2023- 27. The CP3 also seeks to establish a new budgeting system
as one of its ten key tasks that can be used to fund initiatives across the social problems, instead of relying
as heavily on contributions from the sectoral ministries. In addition, while the National Fiscal Ac
t does not
allow Korea to implement multi -year budgeting at this time , its 5- year R&D Medium- to Long- term
Investment Strateg ies provides notional benchmarks for how much funding will be dedicated towards
government priorities. The latest Investment Strategy sets a benchmark to fund the NSTP and CNSTP and
a similar commitment could be made for the societal missions under the CP3.
Recommendation 6. Align STI support along the innovation chain from across government to
improve missions’ chances of success. There are many different technology priority exercises across
the Korean government, which are used to provide preferential government funding and other supports at
different points along the innovation chain (e.g., R&D tax credits from the MOEF, and preferential direct
funding for applied R&D and commercialisation from MOTIE). While there is a large overlap amongst these
priorities, they are not perfectly aligned and many MOIPs (e.g., MSIT’s NSTP) only have some of their
missions qualify for preferred support. Aligning these technology priority exercises and the supports that
target different stages of technology development would ensure more types of programmes are available
for each MOIP along the innovation chain. This alignment between the mission -oriented programs and
other departments will be particular ly important as missions mature and focus more on technology
deployment than development.

procurement and regulatory mechanisms into the challenge-oriented policies would better steer innovation
and create new markets for the technologies developed under the challenges . A major reason for the

Annex A. Detailed framework conditions for
mission-orientation in Korea
This annex provides a synthesis of the evolution of the broader Korean STI governance system, which is
discussed in more detail in the OECD Review of Korean innovation policy (OECD, 2023[1]).
The development of the Korean STI governance system
There are three main periods for how Korea’s STI governance has evolved:
1962-2000: The emergence of an autonomous national innovation system – Korean STI policy in this
period was guided closely by five- year national economic development plans that focused on acquiring
and learning from foreign technologies in targeted industries. Government research institutes (GRIs)
played a major role in absorbing key foreign technologies and transmitting them to large Korean
conglomerates. Private firms also received significant guidance and support from state authorities via
subsidised loans, specific licences to enter targeted industries and protective regulations.
In the 1980s, more autonomous STI policies emerged to incubate national S&T capacity, encourage the
formation of industry consortia, and promote the development of core technologies, such as
semiconductors and various information technology (IT) technologies. This approach was then applied in
other sectoral ministries.
In the 1990s, the focus turned to fundamental science and its commerciali sation. The first mid- to long-
term national R&D plan was released in 1992 and is seen in retrospect as being instrumental in fostering
industries where Korean companies are now global leaders: semiconductors, various IT segments,
electronics and automobiles. This period also saw an increase in complexity for the Korean STI support
system, as the scale and scope of STI support expanded rapidly across sectoral ministries (agriculture,
transport, health, land and construction, etc.) that started to invest significantly in R&D activities at this
time.
2001-22: The maturing of the STI governance system – This period started with a radical change in the
structure of STI governance to respond to the challenges of c
o-ordination across the Korean government
and the more robust suite of supports for STI, which had to be planned, managed and evaluated. New
frameworks and organisational structures were established at this time, including the creation of the
Ministry of Science and Technology in 1998; the establishment of a new, more powerful, co-ordination
council in 1999 (the National Science and Technology Council, NSTC); the enactment of the Framework
Act on Science and Technology in 2001; the launch of the first five-year S&T Basic Plan in 2002; and the
creation of an executive office to take charge of an increasing range of STI governance functions (notably,
the Science, Technology and Innovation Office; hereafter).
There were also attempts to merge or reorganise ministries and agencies to improve co-ordination and/or
streamline structures. For instance, in 2008, the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) and the
Ministry of Education (MOE) merged to become the Ministry of Education, Science and Technology
(MEST). Also significant was the creation in 2008 of the Ministry of Knowledge Economy (MKE), integrating
parts of the former Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Energy (MOCIE), the Ministry of Information and
Communication (MIC) and MOST. The aim of this “super ministry”, active until 2013 when it was disbanded,

Strategic Orientation
This section explores the first dimension of mission- oriented policies, strategic orientation, in the Korean
context. It includes the relevant strategies, priority -setting activities and trends in the Korean STI system
and how effective they are in setting directions that concretely influence the various actors’ own agendas
and actions.
Overarching STI agenda-setting in Korea
The Korean government has a cascading system of well- elaborated whole-of-government strategies for
providing long-term direction. For encouraging strategic orientation in Korean STI policy, it has several
long-standing mechanisms (as outlined in Figure A.1): the long-term vision, the presidential programme
agenda, the five-year S&T Basic Plan, and annual plans for each ministry. Ad-hoc strategies complement
this work.

Strategic policy intelligence, consultations and monitoring
Strategic policy intelligence is an important aspect of Korean policymaking, and the country is broadly
recognised as being at the forefront of foresight and technology forecasting. It has not only devised new
methodologies and tools but also ensured that their results are used to inform strategic decisions, notably
in the context of the S&T Basic Plan development process. Foresight systematically informs whole- of-
government and sectoral strategies and plans – including the mission- oriented policies discussed in
Chapter 4. These exercises create opportunities to debate and make better policy decisions, especially
where these involve societal judgements or that have a systemic character. Foresight is particularly well
connected to the process of developing the Basic Plan.

Policy co-ordination
Korea has a well -established three-tiered governance structure composed of dedicated institutions with
clear legal mandates to perform the essential functions necessary to steer, co-ordinate and implement STI
policies. As is typical of advanced economies, the Korean STI system is composed of three main levels of
governance:
1. Strategic orientation/agenda setting : the level of the executive and legislative branches of
government where the strategic framework guiding STI activities is developed.
2. Co-ordination and programming: the level of the individual ministries or administrations.
3. Policy implementation: the level of funding agencies (also known as intermediary or management
agencies) which implement policy on behalf of the ministries.
The organisations that perform R&D or innovation (universities, public research institutes, companies and
others, such as hospitals) form a fourth level that receives funding and conducts activities to generate
impact.
Horizontal co-ordination by the President
The president is a powerful actor elected for a single and non- renewable five-year term. Members of the
National Assembly serve for four years. The President appoints the Prime Minister as his/her deputy. The
President appoints the other ministers, and the Prime Minister runs the Prime Minister’s Office
(Chancellery).
The PACST, currently chaired by the President, was created in 1991 to provide strategic, medium- to long-
term advice on S&T, and co-ordinates R&D policy across the ministries (except defence, which is treated
separately from the rest of the system). It focuses on national tasks that require comprehensive co-
ordination across ministries and relevant stakeholders, and that require a decision from the highest level
of the S&T community, namely, the President. The PACST has an advisory body, which provides high-
level strategic advice to the President, and a deliberative body, which focuses on planning, budget
formation and monitoring of R&D programmes. Both are chaired by the President of Korea and the PACST
Vice-Chair. All ministries are expected by law to implement the PACST’s decisions. At the same time,
because Korea has a number of high-level councils, there is inevitably some overlap between the PACST
and other councils. The PACST is supported by the PACST Support Group (about 25 staff) , GRIs and
other agencies that have significant analytical capabilities. This group supports both the advisory and
deliberative bodies. It is comprised of public officials from relevant S&T ministries, and experts from GRIs
and agencies.

secretary to the President as part of a reorganisation of the Office of National Policy in 2024. This Senior

The STI Office and the PACST oversee multiple cross -government co- ordination processes in close
collaboration. The STI Office often develops S&T strategies or reviews work from sectoral ministries, which
is then validated or given formal approval by the PACST.
For instance, sectoral ministries submit their annual R&D action plans to the STI Office for review. The STI
Office integrates these inputs into an annual Basic Plan, which it submits to PACST’s Steering Committee
under the Deliberative Council for review. This plan is both a monitoring (activities of the past year) and an
action plan (activities planned for the next year) covering the whole government. It does not include budget
figures. After identifying overlaps, the STI Office encouraged the ministries concerned to reorganise their
programmes. In addition, some sectoral ministries develop annual plans and submit them to the PACST’s
Deliberative Council via the STI Office.
In a similar process, each relevant ministry also submits a mid- term action plan to the STI Office, which
reviews them to ensure they align with the Basic Plan and do not overlap with other ministries’ plans. The
STI Office can interact directly with the ministries when changes are needed. At the end of the review
process, MSIT delivers a list of recommendations that may require the ministries to amend their plans and
take them into account in their annual action plans. Every year, the results of this review process are
submitted to the PACST Deliberative Council for validation and shared with the MOEF and other ministries
for information.
The STI Office annually reviews R&D -specific programmes with support from eight expert committees
comprised of about 20 scientific and technological experts from the public and private sectors in specific
areas (energy, ICT, and convergence technologies, among others). These experts assess the technical
soundness and feasibility of the different programmes submitted by the ministries. These assessments are
complemented by the STI Office’s analysis of the coherence of the different programmes, both between
themselves and with the priorities outlined in the Basic Plan. In addition, the STI Office can provide
additional budgets to some programmes if deemed necessary, although this is relatively rare.

a main test. The first stage pre-test is based on four criteria: 1) necessity and urgency considering recent

STI Policy implementation
Overview of Korean STI support
Korea not only has a very high BERD, but it is also among the OECD countries that provide the largest
level of total government support to BERD as a percentage of GDP, at a rate of 0. 35% of GDP in 2021
(Figure A.8). Most of this public support for business R&D is directed at SMEs. In 2022, the government
financed 16.0% of all R&D conducted by SMEs. In contrast, the government financed only 2.13% of the
R&D expenditures of large firms (MSIT and KISTEP, 2024[39]). This heavy focus on SMEs in public support
for business R&D applies not only to direct funding but also to tax support. Specifically, tax credits for large
firms are capped at 2% of R&D spending, while there is no ceiling for the R&D tax credits for SMEs. As a
result, the implied tax subsidy rate for profit -making SMEs was 26% in 2020, in contrast to 2% for large
firms. Nonetheless, due to the dominant role of large firms in R&D spending in Korea, the share of the total
amount of R&D tax credits given to SMEs among all R&D tax credits was only 40% in 2018 (OECD,
2023
[1]).

Inter-ministerial programmes
A number of broad and complex (“wicked”) issues require resources and capabilities that go beyond the
portfolio of any single ministry or agency. In this case, co- operation between different ministries and
agencies pertaining to different policy fields can prove beneficial either to cover different stages of the
innovation cycle (from research to deployment), different areas of competencies (for instance, support to
technological development and support to skills development) and/or different thematic areas (for instance,
energy and transport).
Large programmes are one of the main mechanisms for collaboration among ministries and agencies
across policy silos. The ministry-led inter-ministerial programmes tend to be longer-term and have a larger
scale and scope, and therefore subject to pre- feasibility tests (KISTEP, 2019
[45]). The government

Annex E. Reducing fine particle matter
Fine particle air pollution is a long-standing issue in Korea. It had improved from 1995 until 2002 following
the introduction of environmental standards, before deteriorating again and reaching a low in 2012. The
government tried to address this through the 10- year Comprehensive Plan for Air Quality Improvement
(2006-2015), but a 2016 public opinion poll by the Korean Medical Association indicated that fine particle
matter was still the “most feared public health risk” (Related Joint Ministers, 2016[47]).
In response to this concern, in June 2016, the Korean Prime Minister and the Office for Government Policy
Co-ordination announced the Special Countermeasures for Fine Particle Management, with the mission to
improve fine particle matter (PM10) concentration to the current level in major European cities within the
next 10 years (from 23 ㎍/㎥
 in 2015 to 18 ㎍/㎥ in 2026). The Ministry of the Environment has led on the
policies to address final particle matter. The Strategic Council on Science and Technology included the
Countermeasures for Fine Particle Management as one of the nine national strategic projects in August
2016 (Related Joint Ministers, 2016[47]). This is an example of an inter-ministerial R&D programme, where
national strategic projects are jointly delivered by actors across the innovation system, including
companies, universities, research organisations, and multiple government ministries (see section 3.3 for
more information on these types of programmes). Unfortunately, it did not proceed because it failed the
pre-feasibility test. However, ten ministries have supported various fine particle matter projects since 2019.
The first stage of the fine particle matter project was carried out based on the Science and Technology -
based Fine Dust Response Strategy from 2017 to 2020. It conducted considerable work to educate citizens
on their role in addressing fine particle matter.
A new phase was announced in 2019: the Fine Dust Management Comprehensive Plan (2020- 2024). It
focused on a new mission to reduce the annual average concentration of ultrafine particle matter (PM2.5)
by more than 35% (from a national average annual concentration of 2 6 ㎍/㎥ in 2016 to 16 ㎍/㎥ in 2024)
(Fine Dust Special Countermeasure Committee, 2019 [48]). This was rooted in the Special Act on the
Reduction and Management of Fine Dust (2019), which requires a new plan be announced every five years
and was mentioned in the 4th S&T Basic Plan – which demonstrates the important emphasis the
government put on it.
The Fine Dust Special Countermeasure Committee oversees the Comprehensive Plan, which consists of
36 people, half of whom are government officials and half are from the private sector or academia. The
National Council on Climate and Air Quality under the President's Office functions as an advisory body,
involving local governments, academia, industry, civil society, and political parties. The Office for Clean Air
supports the Committee and monitors STI-related projects.
The National Air Emission Inventory and Research Centre provides policy support and surveys fine particle
matter air emission. Because fine particle matter does not have boarders, the Fine Dust Management
Comprehensive Plan emphasises working with China and other East Asian countries to reduce their
emissions. In addition, local strategies aligned with the national-level Plan provide direction at the city and
regional level and help reduce fine particle matter from their local emission sources.
Fine particle matter is still a priority for Korea, as a 2nd Fine Dust Management Comprehensive Plan will
be released in 2024 to cover 2025-29, and it is one of the five core problems under the CP3. Because the
Fine Dust Management Comprehensive Plan includes “R&D for emissions reduction and management of
fine particle pollutants and other air pollutants” as one of its main pillars, it will reflect the R&D directions

Annex H. The Innovation Challenge Programme
T
he Innovation Challenge Programme was a challenge-based programme designed by the STI Office and
Delivered by an internal organisation in KISTEP called the Korea Advanced Research Program me
Accelerator (KARPA). The programme worked by setting themes, and then a series of research projects
under each one. Project managers were in place to manage the projects in a more agile approach, winding
down projects that were unsuccessful, and scaling-up those with more promise.
Each year, new themes were selected through a process that involved an extensive consultation: a ministry
demand survey was conducted to identify research themes from within government; the review committee
could put forward their own research themes; and the Crazy Idea Accelerator was created for researchers
across the country to present their ideas. An advisory council of industry, academics and research
organisations narrowed down these proposed themes, then the implicated ministries reviewed the themes
and selected the first batch, and then the 'promotion team leader and division heads’ decided on a second
shorter list. There was then a negotiation with the selected candidate teams on the proposed research
agenda. The research themes were then externally reviewed by the Innovation Challenge Project
Promotion Committee (consisting of academic and industry experts) and a final research agenda was
established (KISTEP, 2021
[49]).
The promotion teams ensured a diverse set of people reviewed projects to determine which ones
warranted more funding. The programme sought to assign individuals to head this team who had not
worked as government officials in the past and focused on individuals who had private sector technical
expertise, participated in large- scale R&D projects, and had large networks domestically and
internationally. The head of a promotion team was the general project manager (PM), who led the R&D
project planning and managed an overall innovation challenge project (Lee et al., 2023
[50]). The Innovation
Challenge Programme established three research areas to generate innovative outcomes that can bring
about socio-economic transformations.
• Happy and healthy ageing
• Safe and pleasant society
• Sustainable growth of Korea’s economy
Within these three areas, each year several challenges were selected based on five criteria: clarity,
challenge-orientation, innovativeness, originality, and impact. The challenges were technical in nature, and
their timeline ranged from 4-7 years. It launched 19 challenges from 2020-2023. The research themes fell
into two types. Firstly, “exploratory” projects were funded for about four years from ideation until the proof-
of-concept phase (short term). Secondly, the “package” projects funded the whole cycle from R&D to
demonstration phases for a maximum of nine years (longer term) (OECD, 2023[1]). A mid-term evaluation
was conducted after the initial two years to decide whether to continue funding based on the pilot projects
(Lee et al., 2023[50]). The programme was able to select multiple research institutions to achieve the same
or similar research goals. The projects funded universities, research institutes, and companies. Unlike
traditional programmes, it could also fund overseas research teams (KISTEP, 2023[51]).
The Innovation Challenge Project Promotion Team and the supervising ministry reviewed projects to
determine which ones warranted more funding. Where annual evaluations are conducted for traditional
programmes, the project managers would conduct regular progress inspections, in addition to stage
evaluations and final evaluations at the end of a project. Evaluation also centred on looking at what was
