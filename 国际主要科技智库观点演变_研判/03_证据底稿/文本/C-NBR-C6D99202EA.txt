T
he Online Platform Fairness Bill proposed in the Republic of Korea (ROK) National Assembly is less a
targeted fix for demonstrable market failure than a sweeping expansion of regulatory authorities that
would hand the Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC) significant powers to police routine platform
business decisions, including for many leading American firms. 1 This proposal, which continues the on-again,
off-again saga of ROK platform regulation proposals over the past several years, deepens existing U.S. government
and industry concerns that KFTC enforcement lacks basic procedural safeguards and is discriminatory in
practice. 2 The bill combines broad “unfairness” standards with significant financial penalties, while leaving key
questions about scope and implementation to presidential decree. The result, if this bill is enacted, is a highly
uncertain compliance and enforcement environment for platform policy that chills innovation. Moreover, the
bill clearly goes against South Korea’s commitments to avoid unnecessary digital trade barriers and ensure
nondiscriminatory treatment of digital service providers. 3
On December 9, 2025, Representative Lee Jeong-moon, a member of the National Assembly, introduced the
Online Platform Fairness Bill, the latest in a series of platform-specific legislative proposals. Representative
Kim Nam-geun cosponsored the legislation, which is important to note, as he had sponsored prior versions
of the bill that explicitly targeted U.S. firms. The Online Platform Fairness Bill would impose a wide set of
obligations on online platforms, including settlement-related rules, contracting and transparency requirements,
and open-ended conduct prohibitions. Many of the decisive details will be specified later through presidential
decree, making it difficult to assess ex ante coverage and compliance burden.
Nigel Cory is a Director at Crowell Global Advisors and a Nonresident Fellow at the National Bureau of Asian Research.

A NEW DIGITAL NONTARIFF BARRIER IN U.S.-ROK TRADE
BRIEF BY NIGEL CORY, CROWELL GLOBAL ADVISORS

South Korea’s stop-start efforts to regulate
platforms over the last five years help explain why
the U.S. government and technology sector are
concerned about the Online Platform Fairness
Bill. Early proposals were heavily shaped by the
European Union’s competition regulations under
the Digital Markets Act (DMA) and then repeatedly
revised amid domestic and U.S. pushback as well as
high-profile corporate incidents that policymakers
use to revise or reframe platform legislation. The
EU’s DMA is a problematic model as it shifts from the
traditional, evidence-based antitrust standard that
is based on proving consumer harm to a proactive
“fairness” standard that disproportionately targets
U.S. companies and imposes restrictive, ex ante
regulations that stifle innovation. 4 Consequently,
South Korea’s platform policy target drifted over
time. What began as a competition-adjacent
response to alleged bargaining-power imbalances
now reaches into practices that fall under the
categories of financial settlement, custody, and
merchant contracting—areas typically handled
through financial supervision and sectoral rules
rather than antitrust enforcement. Lawmakers
also have not demonstrated that key precipitating
(corporate) incidents cited to justify new legislation
reflect a recurring pattern of platform governance
failure rather than firm-specific liquidity and
controls breakdown. The result is a regime that
increasingly resembles a solution in search of a
stable problem, which leads to increased expenses
and heightened uncertainty for businesses.
Even if framed in terms of “transaction fairness,”
the law’s real impact will be driven by the KFTC’s
enforcement posture and by Article 13’s open-ended
conduct rules. This is where uncertainty becomes
consequential from a commercial and trade
perspective. Many covered practices can be either
abusive or efficiency-enhancing (e.g., fraud controls
linked to payments, fulfillment bundles, advertising
products tied to placement, or differentiated service
tiers), depending on how presidential decrees
define “unfairness” and how the KFTC applies
those standards on a case-by-case basis. That
ambiguity is compounded by punitive penalties and
due-process concerns. 5
Flawed from the Start: The Long,
Inconsistent Track Record of Platform
Regulation Proposals
South Korea’s turbulent, nearly six-year effort to
enact platform fairness legislation helps explain
why the current proposal is seen as so concerning. 6
The policymaking process matters. Unlike the EU,
the United Kingdom, Australia, Japan, and others
that published analytical reports to support public
debate, South Korea has repeatedly introduced,
withdrawn, amended, and reintroduced proposals
as political winds have changed and high-profile
incidents have emerged and faded. Japan’s mobile
ecosystem legislation offers a sharp contrast. From
late 2021 through mid-2023, Japan conducted a
structured competition assessment—publishing
an interim report for public comment (April
2022) and a final report (June 2023) after extensive
stakeholder e ngagement—before drafting legislation,
which helped narrow the scope and improve
predictability. 7 Even if you disagree with Japan’s
decision to enact this new legislation, the process
reduced arbitrariness and improved predictability
for businesses. This is exactly what South Korea’s
current approach lacks.
The KFTC first proposed the Online Platform
Fairness Act in 2020, framed around platform
bargaining power and unfair conduct toward
business users. 8 The move was abrupt and proceeded
without a thorough consultation process. Much of
the urgency appeared driven by senior policymakers
seeking to track overseas models—particularly the
EU’s platform-to-business regulation and Japan’s
partial incorporation of platform-to-business concepts
through its Act on Improving Transparency and
Fairness of Digital Platforms. 9
The 2021 Merge Point incident sharpened
debate over intermediary platform accountability,

especially where platforms are deeply involved
in ordering, payment receipt, and refunds. This
crisis stemmed from the decision by Merge Plus
(a digital coupon business) to restrict its Merge
Point prepaid discount service (following regulatory
concerns), leaving users unable to make payments
and triggering a refund crisis. 10 Policymakers
treated Merge Point not only as a prepaid-finance
problem but also as a platform responsibility case
study, arguing that existing rules were unclear
about intermediary duties in payment and refund
workflows. That pathway led to concrete legislative
action in 2023, when the National Assembly
enacted amendments to the Electronic Financial
Transactions Act. Meanwhile, the KFTC’s softer
approach—platform guidelines—briefly advanced.
The commission finalized draft guidelines in 2022,
and they entered into force in early 2023 with
relatively little controversy or revision, aside from
the removal of a section on unfair trading practices.
The guidelines largely reflected existing precedent
rather than imposing new DMA-style obligations.
From 2022 to 2024, however, the policy focus
swung back toward new legislation. The EU’s DMA
began applying to designated “gatekeepers” on
May 2, 2023, and domestic events in South Korea,
including the major KakaoTalk outage and scrutiny
of Kakao T fee policies, intensified political pressure.
Then the July 2024 TMON-WeMakePrice settlement
crisis—where the firms held consumer payments
without a third-party escrow-style structure and
later faced insolvency—became a focal point of
policy debate. Importantly, both firms were already
supervised as payment gateway providers by South
Korea’s Financial Supervisory Service, which had
signed agreements with them two years earlier
due to concerns about deteriorating financial
conditions.11 That episode maps most naturally to
financial and insolvency design—settlement custody
and segregation rules, audits and disclosures,
capital/escrow requirements—rather than classic
fair-trade conduct issues. Consistent with that, in
2024–25 the National Assembly proposed further
amendments to the Electronic Financial Transactions
Act targeting payment gateway providers (though
they were not enacted), and the E-Commerce Act
was amended to address platform accountability. 12
Yet lawmakers still used the TMON-WeMakePrice
crisis to justify a new platform statute with the
KFTC in the lead, even though settlement oversight
sits closest to the Financial Supervisory Service and
the Financial Services Commission.
The KFTC then attempted to revive the legislative
initiative through the Platform Competition
Promotion Act, explicitly citing the EU’s DMA and
Germany’s Section 19a as reference points.13 Like the
DMA, Section 19a is a problematic model because it
lets regulators designate a handful of “paramount”
firms and then impose ex ante conduct bans with
wide discretion and a lower effects burden, increasing
uncertainty and spillover risk. 14 The KFTC proposal
would have created presumptions of dominance
across six categories (online intermediaries, search,
digital content services, social networks, operating
systems, and online advertising) using thresholds
such as 60% market share and 10 million users, or
85% combined share among two or three firms with
each exceeding 20 million users, with exemptions
for firms under 4 trillion won turnover (around $3
billion). In South Korea, these triggers would likely
have captured a handful of firms—Google, Apple,
Kakao, Naver, and possibly Microsoft and Meta. 15
The process was again top-down. The KFTC did
not publish the text of the bill or conduct broad
stakeholder engagement. It also did not reflect
the views of the KFTC’s internal task force, which
reportedly did not endorse DMA-style regulation.
Korean and U.S. stakeholders criticized the process
and outcome. 16 On September 9, 2024, the KFTC
announced it would abandon the proposal .17
How the Online Platform Fairness Bill
Could Be Quite Unfair
Scope and focus. The Online Platform Fairness Bill
adopts a platform-only scope (like the DMA) rather

4
than an activity-based one. It applies to platforms
with annual revenue of 10 billion won (approximately
$6.9 million) or more from intermediary, advertising,
or payment-related services and firms with annual
sales of 100 billion won (approximately $69 million)
or more by participating merchants. It would
cover many Korean firms (e.g., Naver, Kakao, and
Baedal Minjok) and foreign firms (e.g., Coupang,
Google, and Apple). The threshold is broader than
prior proposals, but the conduct standards remain
open-textured and discretion-heavy (see Article 13).
The Online Platform Fairness Bill does not focus on
market structure but on specific payment-settlement
and merchant-contracting practices:
• Settlement discipline. The bill makes platforms
use statutory payout deadlines (generally
within twenty days, with shorter timelines
for certain services) where platforms manage
sales proceeds.
• Ranking and exposure transparency. The bill
requires platforms to specify in merchant
contracts the standards governing product
exposure order and format.
• Transaction terms and unilateral changes.
The bill strengthens procedural safeguards
for how key transaction terms are set and
modified, including requirements that paid
advertising and promotions be governed
by separate written agreements, and allows
merchants to organize collectively to better
articulate their interests.
Unlike activity-based regulation, which targets
specific functions and risks across comparable
actors, a platform-only approach concentrates
compliance burdens on large platforms that sit
at the center of cross-sector and cross-border
commerce. Even if facially neutral, the practical
impact is predictable: the largest platforms, many
of them headquartered in the United States, are
likely to bear the overwhelming share of the burden
from operational and financial compliance, while
functionally similar intermediaries outside the
statutory definition are unaffected. 18
Large punitive fines. The Online Platform Fairness
Bill allows for fines of up to 10% of sales revenue
if platforms engage in unfair practices. At an event
in December 2025, Kim Tae-oh, a professor at
Changwon National University’s Law School, noted
that the legislation—specifically the maximum 10%
of sales and the fixed minimum fine of 5 billion
won—“has a strong punitive character and carries a
high risk of conflicting with the principles of liability
and proportionality.” 19 This is especially sensitive
from a trade perspective because U.S. firms appear
to bear outsized penalties in practice. Although
Korean National Assembly data shows that domestic
firms recorded more violations from 2022 through
the first half of 2025 Coupang paid the most fines,
and Google’s penalty for 2019–22 was comparable to
(or exceeded) fines imposed across multiple Korean
firms for more egregious cartel conduct. 20
Lack of judicial review of fines and corrective
orders. The Online Platform Fairness Bill applies
surcharges of up to 10% of relevant sales through
broadly framed “unfairness” standards (enforced by
the KFTC), combined with expansive authorities to
issue corrective orders. In some respects, this even
goes further than the EU’s DMA—where penalties
apply to a narrowly designated set of gatekeepers
under a defined obligations framework with clear
judicial review—by creating comparable exposure
to penalties under broader standards without a
tailored appeals architecture for this new platform-
specific regime.
The bill’s penalty-and-remedy architecture also
raises a practical judicial-review problem that
reinforces existing concerns about the KFTC.
Under South Korea’s current framework, challenges
to KFTC corrective orders and surcharges are
generally litigated after the KFTC issues a final
administrative disposition. This means judicial
review is largely ex post and typically arrives only
after the investigation has run its course. In short,
the regime risks combining broad, decree-defined
obligations with heavy penalty exposure while
leaving firms with limited ability to obtain timely
judicial clarification before commercial and
reputational harms materialize.

Broad and vague prohibitions of conduct beyond
competition. The Online Platform Fairness Bill goes
beyond the sorts of conduct rules found in the EU’s
DMA and instead directly regulates things like
settlement and custody mechanics. These obligations
are unprecedented globally for nonfinancial
platforms, especially when compared with the
United States and EU (where similar requirements
apply only to licensed financial institutions).
Typically, digital antitrust regulations are designed
to address potential exclusionary conduct, such as
self-preferencing, that could distort competition.
They do not prescribe market outcomes through
measures like mandating settlement timelines,
liquidity management, or safeguarding rules, which
are not ordinarily addressed by competition law
regimes. 21 In addition, even though the prohibited
forms of conduct could be actionable under South
Korea’s existing competition law, this ex ante
identification shifts the burden of proof to platforms
and enhances the administrative power of the
KFTC. The broad discretion the KFTC will possess
to prosecute Article 13 violations will inevitably
raise questions of discrimination—for instance,
regarding why it chose to pursue certain practices
from some platforms but not others.
Article 10 of the bill requires that, where a platform
receives and manages sales proceeds, including
where management is outsourced to an electronic
payment processing entity, the platform must pay
out merchants within a statutory deadline (20 days).
The bill includes a phase-in period that temporarily
extends the baseline payout deadline to 40 days in
the first year after enforcement and 30 days in the
second year.
Article 13 prohibits an online platform
intermediary from engaging in (or inducing others
to engage in) conduct that is likely to undermine
“fair” transactions in platform intermediation.
Unfair conduct includes unreasonably suspending,
refusing, or materially restricting access to the
platform’s intermediation services (or related
information/management support); unfairly
discriminating among business users; unfairly
coercing a business user into transactions it does
not intend; unfairly demanding money, goods,
services, operational data, or other economic
benefits for itself or affiliates; setting, changing,
or implementing transaction terms in ways that
disadvantage business users; improperly interfering
with users’ business activities; and unfairly imposing
exclusivity or obstructing users’ ability to transact
with rival intermediaries. The detailed criteria and
specific types of prohibited “unfair trade practices”
are to be further defined by presidential decree.
Article 13 functions less like a targeted “platform
to business fairness” rule and more like a
general-purpose rule that gives the KFTC discretion
over enforcement while leaving key boundaries to
presidential decree. This combination is a recipe for
legal uncertainty, with chilling effects on legitimate
platform governance. Article 13’s open-textured
unfairness standards invite ex post second-guessing
of routine governance choices about pricing/fees,
access decisions, ranking, and risk controls, unless
presidential decree supplies narrow criteria and
clear safe harbors.
Article 13’s provisions on forced transactions
and exclusivity are aimed at problematic tying
(conditioning access to a necessary platform
function on buying another service) and lock-in
(designing contractual/technical constraints that
increase switching costs), but the same language
can sweep in efficient integration that is central to
platform performance. For example, this could be
characterized as additionally covering payments
linked to fraud/risk controls, fulfillment bundles
that improve reliability, advertising products tied to
placement, or standard commercial terms such as
volume rebates and program tiers. As prohibitions
are framed in open-ended unfairness terms and the
boundary-setting is pushed to presidential decree,
this becomes an area of high regulatory uncertainty:
the law can end up treating normal competition on
business models as suspect. 22
The uncertain application of future presidential
decrees. As noted above, the Online Platform
Fairness Bill’s real-world scope and impact will

be determined largely through future presidential
decrees because the statute delegates core definitional
and operational content to subordinate rulemaking.
This is most consequential in two areas: scope and
obligations. On scope, the coverage thresholds
rely on revenue categories and ancillary services
that will be further specified by decree, meaning
the regulated universe can expand or contract
depending on technical choices. On obligations,
critical compliance mechanics are also left to decree,
raising risks of moving goalposts, uneven sectoral
tailoring, and recurring compliance friction. Article
13 is the clearest example: the statute delegates the
operative unfair-practice criteria to decree, making
enforcement exposure difficult to price ex ante.
The KFTC’s uneven enforcement record compounds
concerns about the Online Platform Fairness Bill.
Articles 31–35 of the Online Platform Fairness
Bill grant the KFTC broad enforcement powers,
including corrective orders, daily penalties, and
administrative surcharges. Articles 45–48 add
criminal sanctions and other liabilities and fines.
The KFTC’s enforcement record compounds
concerns about the Online Platform Fairness Bill
because the legislation would expand the number of
day-to-day business judgments that can be reframed
as unfair. U.S. firms already view KFTC enforcement
as selective and heavy-handed. Prior research
published by the National Bureau of Asian Research
documented that U.S. firms’ central concern is not
simply what South Korea regulates but how the
KFTC enforces regulations, especially given the lack
of due process and avenues for legal redress. 23 Firms
consistently described a compliance environment
marked by a low threshold for opening investigations,
limited transparency about the underlying theory
of harm, aggressive investigative tactics (including
raids), and unusually broad information demands
and data seizures—paired with the frequent use (or
threat) of criminal referral as an enforcement lever.
These are not abstract process critiques; they are
precisely the kinds of features that convert an already
broad fairness mandate into a highly uncertain and
high-cost regulatory risk for U.S. firms operating in
fast-moving digital markets .
The Impact of the Bill on U.S.-ROK
Trade and Innovation in South Korea
The Online Platform Fairness Bill risks becoming
another major irritant in U.S.-ROK digital trade by
layering broad prohibitions on “unfair trade practices”
onto cross-border platform activity and then vesting
primary enforcement in a regulator that U.S. firms view
as unusually aggressive and procedurally imbalanced.
Some U.S. firms already see South Korea as a high-risk
compliance market, leading them to delay new product
entry and launches. This bill, if enacted, is likely to
reinforce this perspective.24
In trade terms, the Online Platform Fairness Bill
operates like a digital nontariff barrier. Even where
the statute is facially neutral, uncertainty, coupled
with discretionary application of broad conduct
rules, creates asymmetric compliance and litigation
risks that are likely to fall disproportionately on large
U.S. platforms. There is an uncertainty premium
as platforms cannot reliably predict what conduct
will be deemed unfair, how compliance will be
measured, or how penalties will be calculated until
after presidential decrees and enforcement cases. That
uncertainty increases legal exposure to complaints,
investigations, and corrective orders, which in turn
drives precautionary compliance (rewriting and
executing merchant terms and conditions, redesigning
integrations, and expanding internal review) and
could deter the rollout of new features or business
models in Korea. This leads to disproportionately high
fixed compliance costs and expected enforcement
risks for foreign suppliers at the center of cross-border
commerce. The result is a market-access constraint “in
effect,” even without an explicit nationality-based rule.
This is exactly the kind of regulatory friction that trade
policy treats as a nontariff barrier.
The innovation costs flow from the same mechanics.
The Online Platform Fairness Bill contributes to
a business environment in South Korea in which
regulatory outcomes are less predictable and

commercial decisions are more exposed to ex post
second-guessing. When core business judgments
(e.g., integrations, ranking and access decisions,
differentiated terms, and fraud controls) can be
reframed as unfair under decree-defined criteria, firms
will rationally slow iteration, reduce experimentation,
and default to more conservative, uniform designs,
while shifting resources from product development
and security improvements toward process
compliance, documentation, and legal defensibility.
That tradeoff weakens service quality and localization
and reduces the pace at which Korean users and small
and medium-sized enterprises benefit from better
trust-and-safety tools, payments innovation, and
commerce-facilitating tools.
The same uncertainty also changes how firms
allocate investment and operational attention across
markets. Elevated regulatory risk raises the cost of
treating South Korea as a launch and testing market,
encouraging platforms to delay rollouts, narrow
third-party access, or centralize decision-making in
jurisdictions with clearer rules and more predictable
enforcement. Over time, these ecosystem effects will
slow the diffusion of frontier services into South Korea
and reduce complementary innovation by Korean
developers, merchants, and advertisers. The net result
is that a law motivated by legitimate fairness concerns
could still degrade the overall investment climate for
digital services, which would erode trade confidence
and the innovation dynamism that underpins South
Korea’s competitiveness and U.S.-ROK economic and
technology cooperation.
Options for the Trump Administration
The Online Platform Fairness Bill fits a pattern
in South Korea’s platform policy: ambitious,
high-discretion proposals that later get narrowed,
delayed, or abandoned after domestic pushback,
market realities, and external scrutiny. The priority
for the Trump administration should be to elevate
the bill as a top-tier digital trade irritant in ongoing
bilateral engagement to help ensure this becomes
another instance where South Korea reverses course.
The policy objective is not to “win” a platform fight
for its own sake; it is to prevent a high-uncertainty
and discriminatory regime from hardening into
enforceable rules that will predictably chill innovation
and distort market access for globally integrated U.S.
digital services.
This strategy also directly addresses the
credibility gap created by South Korea’s recent
digital trade assurances. The Lee Jae-myung
government committed in bilateral talks with the
Trump administration in November 2025 to avoid
discriminatory or unnecessary barriers to digital
services. Almost immediately after that commitment
was made, however, the National Assembly (in which
Lee’s Democratic Party of Korea maintains a sizable
majority) restarted a process to enact legislation that
would create precisely the kind of decree-defined
compliance burden and enforcement discretion
that firms experience as a de facto barrier. That
juxtaposition underscores the core problem with
the South Korean business environment: U.S. firms
cannot plan investment, compliance, or product
rollout when major regulatory obligations can be
introduced abruptly, defined later by decree, and
enforced aggressively, while firms have limited
practical ability to obtain timely judicial review,
assert legal defenses, or secure effective redress before
commercial and reputational harm occurs.
The Trump administration should continue to
treat the Online Platform Fairness Bill as a live
trade irritant and keep it at the top of the digital
trade agenda in ongoing negotiations. Both the U.S.
Trade Representative (USTR) and U.S. Congress
have made public statements that underscore how
this bill contravenes South Korea’s commitment to
not enact discriminatory digital regulations. 25 The
USTR should make clear that a final regime that
discriminates in design or effect—or predictably
burdens foreign suppliers through open standards
and punitive penalties—could justify escalation,
including a Section 301 response (as the USTR has
already threatened).26 •
