Azerbaijan, located at a strategic point along the coast of the Caspian Sea, is the centre of
science and technology in the Eurasian region and has great importance for cooperation in
the international energy market as a production and transit hub for crude oil and natural
gas. Azerbaijan is hugely reliant on the oil and gas industry, which accounts for more than
90% of its total exports, and has an economic structure trade that is contingent upon three
neighbouring countries, namely, Russia, Iran, and Turkey. In other words, it is a country that
is economically and socially affected by unstable fluctuations in international oil prices and
by changes in the circumstances of neighbouring countries.
Recently, Azerbaijan’s government resolved to move away from excessive dependence on
the oil industry and make the transition to a knowledge-based economy, in a bid to avoid a
financial crisis caused by unstable oil prices and to create a democratic political environment.
It is in this context that the government established 'Azerbaijan 2020: Look into the Future’
and 'Azerbaijan 2030: National Priorities for Socio-Economic Development' with the aim of
achieving sustainable economic development based on innovation in the field of science
and technology. The creation of the Azerbaijan National Cooperation Strategy (2016) shows
that the government is making efforts to strengthen the country’s science and technology
innovation capacity under the national development plan and to implement industrial
diversification policies aimed at building national competitiveness. In addition, it
emphasizes the need to increase investment in science and technology innovation and to
improve the operation of its system by increasing the efficiency of knowledge creation,
technology transfer, and the application and commercialization of technology through the
'Azerbaijan 2030 Strategic Roadmap'.

 Project Background
According to the definition provided by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO),
technology transfer plays a pivotal role in facilitating the entire life cycle of a technology,
encompassing its inception, diffusion into the market, and eventual commercialization1.
Within this context, the commercialization of technologies entails a process in which an idea
transitions from a knowledge base to the market, thereby creati ng financial value.
Technology commercialization (TC) also enhances economic efficiency by offering
consumers a diverse and improved range of goods and services, resulting in the generation
of value-added revenue streams. Consequently, investment in technology and research and
development (R&D)-related activities, along with the subsequent commercialization of their
outcomes, necessitates the involvement of a wide array of institutions, programs, and
mechanisms to achieve the optimal results.
In the context of Azerbaijan, technology commercialization (TC) has recently emerged as a
prominent topic of discussion within its innovation ecosystem. This report is an integral part
of a collaborative project between the Azerbaijan High Technologies Park (HTP) and the
Science and Technology Policy Institute (STEPI) spanning 2022 to 2024. The report's primary
focus is to delve into the nuances of technology transfer and commercialization that are
specific to Azerbaijan. To accomplish this objective, our research will explicitly centre on the
developments occurring in Azerbaijan post -July 2022, coinciding with significant
institutional reforms in the aforementioned domains. We anticipate that this report will
contribute significantly to enhancing the ef fectiveness of the STEPI -HTP partnership's
operational programs.

Consistent with the WIPO's delineation, effective technology transfer requires universities
and research institutions to operate within a well-structured innovation ecosystem2. This
ecosystem comprises an interconnected network involving governmental bodies, industry
stakeholders, research institutions, and various enabling factors, including human capital,
technology transfer infrastructure, and the sophistication of businesses and markets. Within
such an ecosystem, these entities pool their resources and expertise to collaboratively drive
innovation, ultimately serving regional and economic development objectives.
Recognizing these imperatives, Azerbaijan has established a comprehensive framework of
institutions and infrastructures to regulate and bolster TC activities. For instance, the
"Social-Economic Development Strategy of the Republic of Azerbaijan, 2022-2026" outlines
a strategic framework, part of which is dedicated to enhancing the competitiveness of the
country's human capital and positioning it as a hub for innovation. Consequently, Azerbaijan
aspires to create a "Science-Industry Cluster 4.0" concept, establish the "Absheron Valley"
innovation cluster, and greatly expand its research capacity and commercialization
initiatives.
The K-Innovation Partnership Program, a collaboration between the Science & Technology
Policy Institute (STEPI) of the Republic of Korea and the High Technologies Park (HTP) of the
Republic of Azerbaijan, spanning 2022 to 2024, is primarily geared towards capacity building
and fostering cooperation in the realms of effective technology transfer and
commercialization. Within the ambit of this project, this document has been tasked with
delivering a status report concerning TC policies, programs, and projects in Azerbaijan. To
fulfill this mandate, the report will serve as a comprehensive reference, providing in-depth
insights into governance mechanisms, institutional and legal frameworks, intellectual
property rights, and a concise overview of Azerbaijan's national innovation ecosystem. Its
overarching objective is to make a substantial contribution to the K-Innovation Partnership
Program by offering a comparative analysis and timely updates on the pertinent reforms
and policies in Azerbaijan.

 Project Framework
The 2023 project is focused on improving the technology commercialization system in
Azerbaijan and developing startup and incubation programs for the High Technologies Park
(HTP).
This project began with a kick-off seminar and an extensive current status analysis. During
this phase, we formed a dedicated research team and outlined the scope of our research.
This part of the project consisted in investigating the local landscape by conducting a
thorough assessment of the existing startup and incubation regulations in Azerbaijan, while
simultaneously examining the startup and incubation services offered at HTP, pinpointing
areas that required further attention.
In the pursuit of enhancing capacity, a collaborative effort with diverse experts and
organisations in South Korea was initiated. This collaboration included inviting Azerbaijan
experts for training and organi sing specialized workshops on technology transfer and
commercialization. These initiatives aimed to foster exchanges of knowledge and share
Korea’s experience and methodologies.
An integral aspect of the 2023 project involved the development of an online platform for
technology transfer and commercialization. This platform was designed to enhance the
ecosystem of technology transfer and commercialization in Azerbaijan by offering
information, incorporating enhancements in content and technological functionality, and
ensuring that it would serve as a valuable resource for stakeholders.
Finally, a forum has been conducted to disseminate the outcomes of the 2023 project. This
forum played a pivotal role in providing policy recommendations concerning technology
commercialization governance in Azerbaijan and boosting the roles of the HTP. The aim was
to ensure that the insights gained from capacity-building activities were not only shared but
also effectively disseminated to the relevant stakeholders, thereby contributing to the
broader ecosystem of technology transfer and commercialization in Azerbaijan.

 Project Team
The project team is composed of four key groups: the project management team, Korean
consultants specializing in policy recommendations, Korean experts focusing on the TCK-Lab,
and a team of Azerbaijan experts. The project management team oversees overall project
coordination, resource allocation, and timeline management; the Korean consultants offer
policy insights, aligning international standards with local needs; the Korean TCK-Lab experts
facilitate knowledge transfer through seminars and workshops; and, last but not least, the
Azerbaijani experts contribute local insights and conduct situational assessments to tailor the
project activities. In summary, this multidisciplinary team combines international expertise
with local insights to enhance Azerbaijan's technology commercialization system and foster
innovation-driven growth.

 (Capacity-Building) TCK-Lab: IP Silk Road Azerbaijan

In the current landscape of policy -making, the demand for innovative approaches to
enhancing capacity-building is evident. Various learning methodologies have emerged as
viable options, one of which is the “learning lab” approach. The concept of a learning lab
pertains to a physical or virtual environment in which individuals engage in both academic
and practical learning activities.
The inception of the Technology Commercialization Knowledge Lab (TCK-Lab) was inspired
by the principles of the learning lab approach. Its primary mission was to align with the
objectives of the 2023 project, which sought to bolster the operational capabil ities and
competitive edge of the Azerbaijan National Academy of Sciences High-Tech Park (ANAS
HTP). This endeavour aimed to achieve these goals through the implementation of essential
training programs designed to enhance operational expertise and foster exchanges of
knowledge with local stakeholders in the realm of Intellectual Property (IP) Management.
The TCK-Lab for the year 2023 constitutes an integral component of the capacity-building
initiative undertaken under the purview of the K-Innovation Partnership Program. Notably,
this year's TCK -Lab featured the participation of a distinguished expert in the field of
Intellectual Property (IP), who shared his invaluable knowledge and insights. The course's
overarching objectives were to impart the fundamental legal principles and to illuminate
the commercial dimensions of patents to a diverse audience comprising current and
prospective inventors, entrepreneurs, technology commercialization and transfer experts,
and Technology Transfer Organization (TTO) professionals among others.
Through the auspices of the TCK-Lab, a multifaceted approach was adopted to contribute
to the capacity-building efforts of policy-makers. This approach encompassed learning,
practical application, rigorous evaluation, innovative discovery, and the sharing of ideas.
Consequently, the anticipated outcome of this initiative is the enhancement and
advancement of Azerbaijan's institutional framework and conditions within the domain of
Technology Commercialization/Technology Transfer (TC/TT), as viewed through the prism
of Intellectual Property considerations.

 (Capacity-Building) Open Innovation Platform Workshop

In light of the increasing demand for innovative policy-making capabilities, a broad spectrum
of diverse learning methodologies has emerged as a viable option in contemporary contexts.
One such methodology that warrants consideration is the workshop approach.
The Open Innovation Platform Workshop was conceived and executed with the overarching
objective of aligning with the objectives of the 2023 project. This project's primary mission
was to fortify the operational prowess and compet itive standing of the HTP (High
Technologies Park) through the implementation of indispensable training programs aimed
at augmenting operational expertise. Additionally, it sought to facilitate the exchange and
dissemination of knowledge among local stakeholders within the realm of TTCP (Technology
Transfer and Commercialization Platform) Management.
The Open Innovation Platform Workshop for the year 2023 represents an integral
component of the capacity -building initiative within the purview of the K -Innovation
Partnership Program. The workshop's ultimate aim was to furnish the participants with
foundational operational principles and illuminate the commercial dimensions of
technology, specifically pertaining to the TTCP platform. The diverse participant base
included individuals from various professional backgrounds, including current and
prospective inventors, entrepreneurs, experts in technology commercialization and transfer,
as well as professionals affiliated with Technology Transfer Organizations (TTOs), among
others.
Through the strategic deployment of this workshop, it is anticipated that a multifaceted
approach encompassing learning, practical application, rigorous evaluation, innovative
discovery, and the exchange of ideas will contribute significantly to the capacity-building
efforts of ecosystem stakeholders. As a result, this initiative is poised to catalyse
improvements and advancements within Azerbaijan's Technology Transfer (TT) and
Technology Commercialization (TC) landscape through the digital platform provided by the
TTCP.

 Research Framework

A systematic review of existing academic literature on technology commercialization can
demonstrate the ambiguous role of the capabilities of technology transfer offices (TTO) in
transferring technologies from a university/research institute to industry. For example, Lee
and Jung (2021) identified three distinctive capabilities of technology commercialization (TC)
while analysing longitudinal data from South Korea for the period 2009-2015. These
capabilities include academic research capability, applied res earch capability, and TTO
operation capability. Their analysis of the effects of these capabilities on TC performance
reveals that, unlike the other two capabilities, the TTO operation capability has no significant
effect on TC performance. Therefore, establishing an effective operational capability alone
for a high-technology park cannot solve the challenges associated with TC activities. Min,
Kim and Vonortas (2020) also analysed the conditions for TC and commercial exploitation
of public technologies in Korea. Their analysis of 514 cases of TC by major public research
institutes and universities underline that market competition is a key determinant of
successful TC, and that it also affects business growth in general.
Moreover, regardless of the market situation, effective partnership is critical for successful
TC. Recently, Belitzki, Aginskaya and Marozau (2019) also identified the role of TTOs and
direct industrial funding in TC in the transition economies of Azerbaijan, Belarus and
Kazakhstan during 2015-2017. Interestingly, their research reveals that successful research
commercialization is neither associated with the existence and awareness of TTOs, nor with
their contracts with partners. However, direct industrial funding of research is a significant
factor.

 Macro-level analysis of the current status: a view of the TC

◼ Policy-making issues
A successful innovation policy for a developing country requires careful consideration of a
wide range of activities. To achieve better TC results, policy makers should develop a
comprehensive innovation policy and then consider certain key issues when implementing
that policy. Such issues may include, but are not limited to, the fol lowing: the state of
innovation; strategic priorities; access to finance; intellectual property protection; capacity
building; and collaboration and cooperation.

◼ Institutions
TC is a process by which necessary measures are undertaken in order to pass a given
research output through the relevant procedures and make it beneficial for
commercialization in various applications. For TC to act effectively, several types of
institutions play important roles: government agencies; research centres and universities;
business incubators and accelerators; venture capital firms; and associations and consortia.

◼ Funding mechanisms
TC requires several funding mechanisms to be available. The range of such funding schemes
may vary depending on the stage of technology development, or on the goals of the
stakeholders wh o seek to commercialize such technologies. Various major funding
mechanisms also play an important role, including government grants, equity financing,
debt financing, corporate partnerships, and crowdfunding.

◼ Regulatory frameworks
Complying with legislation and regulations is a must-do requirement for TC. Therefore, it is
critical to assess regulatory frameworks that may impact it. There are several regulatory
frameworks that are particularly important for TC, and such frameworks - encompassing
intellectual property, taxation, international trade, consumer protection, data privacy, and
environmental, health and safety regulations among others - may vary depending on the
specific industry and technology involved.

A successful innovation policy for a developing country requires careful consideration of a
wide range of activities. In order to achieve better TC results, policy makers should develop
a comprehensive innovation policy and then consider certain key issues when implementing
that policy. Gaining added value through TC plays a key role in economic development. The
rapid development of technology and increasing competition are among the main factors
that justify the commercialization of science, as well as the development of innovative
entrepreneurship. There are complex infrastructural factors in Azerbaijan to regulate the
process of TC. At the same time, there are factors that restrict this process and partially
hinder it, such as legislation, the weakness of university-industry cooperation, incomplete
formation of the intellectual property ecosystem, and other factors. Also, the lack of
research that meets the needs of the industry can be considered as a factor that slows down
the commercialization process.
Regarding the state of innovation in Azerbaijan, the country generally performs below
expectations for its level of development, relative to GDP. According to the 2023 Global
Innovation Index, it ranked 93rd among the 132 economies featured in the GII 2022, a poorer
ranking than it achieved in the previous two years, i.e. 80th in 2021 and 82 nd in 2020.
Compared to its level of investment in innovation, it produces fewer innovation outputs.
Furthermore, it performs relatively poorly in various domains. 3 For example, its best
performing sub-index is “Institutions” while its weakest performance is in “Knowledge and
technology outputs”. Particularly, it underperforms significantly in the “creative outputs”
and “knowledge and technology outputs” categories. As regards the “knowledge and
technology output” indicator, Azerbaijan ranks 107 th in knowledge creation, 120 th in
knowledge diffusion, and 106th in knowledge impact. These indicators give a clear picture of
Azerbaijan’s innovation output.

Regarding institutions, TC is a process by necessary measures are carried out in order to
ensure that a given research output passes through the relevant procedures and to make
them beneficial for commercialization in diverse applications. However, there is no central
organization in Azerbaijan that is engaged in technology commercialization. Usually, all
ministries include their right to conduct scientific-technical projects within the scope of their
statues as a standard clause. However, the activities of the Ministries of Digital Development,
Transport, Economy, and Science and Education, as well as the Intellectual Property Agency,
partially coincide with this area.
There is no single ministry dedicated to technology commercialization in Azerbaijan.
Therefore, each related authority has its own TC approach. However, the Centre for
Commercialization and Transfer of Technologies (established in 2021) under the IP Agency
is the only dedicated institution, although it is not a central body tasked with carrying out
country-level TC policies. It has no authority to lead the TC policies by itself, nor order other
ministries to conduct TC functions as it sees fit. In order to achieve its objectives, this centre
will support the provision of incentives for invention activities, and the integration of science
and industry, as well as supporting “innovation economy” policies by promoting IP activities.

◼ Improvement of operational and managerial issues
Since its establishment in 2016, the HTP has gone through various stages of development
and experienced fluctuations in its fortunes . Fortunately, it provides strong research
opportunities and fiscal incentives for its residents. On the other hand, the frequent changes
in leadership, sluggish and old-fashioned image of ANAS, inappropriate location in a factory

the path of the HTP. Additionally, the HTP is one of the m ost passive members of the

Considering the status reported above, as well as the real challenges lying ahead, the HTP
must improve its operational and managerial capabilities by focusing on the following
critical dimensions:
◼ Needs assessment
Ever since its inception, there have been continuous discussions about the necessity for the
HTP to succeed in the ecosystem and achieve its intended targets. However, a detailed
needs assessment, ideally conducted by an independent third -party, has never been
conducted to grasp the real picture and calculate the appropriate budget and allocation of
resources. A needs assessment would enable identification of specific operational issues
that need to be improved to ensure for a better TC. A comprehensive needs assessment
may focus on infrastructure, services, management, and other areas. A needs assessment
should also be interpreted from the viewpoint of the HTP’s strategic vision.

◼ Improvement plan
Although the HTP has prepared a Road Map for future growth, this document consists of a
list of targets that might seem rather vague and ambitious while lacking a proper initial
assessment of its needs. Therefore, based on the results of a needs assessment, a detailed
improvement plan that outlines the specific actions required to address each issue is critical
for successful TC. Implementation plans also allocate tasks and set timelines and milestones
for their completion.

◼ Infrastructure and services
Successful TC requires state-of-the-art infrastructure to support the needs of stakeholders.
Such an infrastructure would include facilities, equipment, manufacture spaces, and work
spaces, etc. Moreover, services such as networking events, mentorship programs, and
access to legal and financial advice are also crucial for TC. From this point of view, the HTP
faces huge challenges. For example, its location is less interesting for private firms and
individuals; and although its infrastructure is sufficient for experimental-production works,
it is not attractive as a modern business incubation and acceleration centre; nor does it have
a reputation for strong mentorship and networking capabilities. Therefore, depending on

its improvement plans, the HTP should consider how to become an attractive hub equipped
with modern infrastructures and services.
◼ Monitoring and evaluation
Assessing needs and then developing implementation programs is not a sufficient way to
proceed. Therefore, regular monitoring of the progress of the improvement plan and an
evaluation of its effectiveness is critical for TC, as this would allow stakeholders to adjust
their plans as necessary to ensure that the intended outcomes are achieved. The HTP needs
to establish an effective monitoring and evaluation system for its own scope and, perhaps,
for its residents as well. This would allow it to become more flexible and agile and to improve
its growth strategy in a timely manner.

◼ Timing
The HTP has already been in operation since 2016, and this period has witnessed massive
changes in both industries and technologies. Therefore, in order not to miss critical
opportunities, timing is a critical factor for the purposes of TC. It is important to assess the
market trends and the readiness of technolog ies before entering the market. Time is
pressing upon the HTP, and it may also affect its political credibility as a reliable and efficient
public entity with a broad range of fiscal incentives. Therefore, it should immediately assess
the market potential locally and globally, connect with potential customers, and compete
against other providers of TC.

◼ Business model
A good business model that evaluates the market, competition and other factors is essential
for the successful commercialization of technologies. Because of the abovementioned
factors that the HTP needs to improve, there is no clear business model for its TC activities.
The founding purpose of the ANAS HTP (currently, the total budget amounts to about USD
300 000) was to gradually decrease its dependence on the state budget so that it could
become a self-financing, not-for-profit entity in the long term. To achieve that goal, however,
it needs to build diverse and sustainable revenue streams.

 Conclusion

This Report on the Status of the K-Innovation Partnership Program 2022-2024 between
STEPI and the HTP can be considered a follow-up to the previous status report submitted in
2022. This report mainly concentrates on the updates that have occurred since July 2022,
while considerable institutional reforms have been implemented within ANAS. Numerous
institutions, mostly in the field of natural and technical sciences, have been transferred
within the structure of the Ministry of Science and Education. As for ANAS, it mostly
manages institutes in humanitarian and social sciences. Most importantly for this project,
the ANAS HTP is no longer a unit of ANAS, although the interagency transferring process is
still ongoing and needs to be finalized.
Another significant event entails strengthening the authority of the Ministry of Transport
and Digital Development as a central power to govern the innovation ecosystem, digital
transformation, and e-governance. All these responsibilities are currently assigned to the
Innovation and Digital Development Agency. The HTP would benefit from closer
collaboration with the IDDA and active participation in the innovation ecosystem.
So far, Azerbaijan has not introduced any legal acts on competition, venture capital, etc.
Moreover, a draft of the national innovation strategy needs to be approved in order not to
miss the right timing or lose out on the market potential. Equity and debt financing, and
crowdfunding are underperforming forms of funding mechanisms in the country.
Furthermore, the unavailability of venture capital firms in the domestic market makes the
creation of more dynamic markets and financing an even more challenging task.
We believe that the process of re-formation of HTP will take place after it becomes part of
the Ministry of Science and Education. The visits of the minister and other management
staff to the HTP, and their respective ideas, indicate that the HTP will once again be formed
into a modern technology park.
Regarding the need to improve the HTP’s operational and managerial issues, it will soon
conduct a needs assessment and develop improvement plans based on the findings of the

1.2.2.2 Manpower Certification System
South Korea has a ‘technology trader’ system that certifies qualifications in the relevant field

To promote the technology transfer and commercialization of home-grown technologies
and their intellectual properties (IPs), such as patents, the Azerbaijan government needs to

 Intellectual Property (IP) Ecosystem
Innovations, inventions, and new technologies do not appear from nowhere. That is,
innovations, inventions, and new technologies are neither created from scratch nor
obtained at no cost. On the contrary, they come from an ecosystem comprising several
layers.
It has been noted that different people who are equipped with different skill sets usually

===== PDF_PAGE 91 =====
Chapter 3. Policy Recommendations
81
play a major role in each such layer. In addition, these people tend to work in a sequence so
that, for instance, people in the second layer cannot start to perform their tasks until the
people in the first layer have completed their tasks, while people in the third layer cannot
start to perform their tasks until people in the second layer have finished their work, and so
on. To reflect these characteristics, one can assume a hypothetical IP ecosystem, and then
use that IP ecosystem to analyze the characteristics of each layer as well as the various
interactions among the different layers.

2.1.1 The Upstream Segment of the IP Ecosystem Requires Innovators
and Inventors
The upstream of the IP ecosystem is characterized by the creation of innovations by
innovators or the conception of inventions by inventors. More particularly, the upstream
starts when an innovator or an inventor comes up with an idea or an innovative technology.
The innovator or inventor can then modify his or her idea and can also end up with lots of
related ideas or ideas for improvement. Eventually, the innovator or inventor can sublimate
the best idea into an invention (i.e., the inventor conceives the invention). Thus, there is no
doubt that the inventor or innovator is the main player in the upstream of the IP ecosystem.
When the midstream and downstream of the IP ecosystem of a certain country are fully
developed, innovators or inventors may only need to master their technological expertise.
However, when the midstream or downstream is not fully developed, it may be worthwhile
to educate innovators and inventors with knowledge related to patents, technology transfer,
and commercialization. Once equipped with such knowledge, innovators and inventors may
be able to pursue their R&D, while manipulating their R&D in the direction of increasing the
chances for potential technology transfer or commercialization.

2.1.2 The Midstream Segment Requires Patent Law Professionals
The midstream element of the IP ecosystem consists in filing a patent application and
getting the patent application issued as a patent. More particularly, an inventor hires a
patent lawyer (or a patent agent), who then prepares and files a patent application to a

===== PDF_PAGE 92 =====
2023 K-Innovation Partnership Program with Azerbaijan
82
patent office in the country (or another country) of the inventor.
After filing the patent application, the patent lawyer deals with a patent examiner at the
patent office, and assists his client in getting the patent application issued as a patent.
Therefore, the patent lawyer or the patent agent is the main character in the midstream.
When the upstream and downstream segments of the IP ecosystem are well developed,
legal professionals may only need to know the patent law itself. If the midstream or
downstream segment is not fully developed, however, it may be worthwhile to educate
legal professionals in the various fields of knowledge related to R&D, the invention process,
and technology transfer or commercialization.
Equipped with such knowledge, legal professionals can better understand and efficiently
communicate with innovators and inventors, and may then draft patent applications while
increasing the chances for potential technology transfer or commercialization.

2.1.3 The Downstream Segment Requires Various Technological or Business
Professionals
Finally, the downstream of the IP ecosystem is characterized by different modalities of
monetizing or commercializing a patent. Accordingly, the inventor (or patent owner) can
resort to at least three different modalities to reap benefits from his invention.

2.1.3.1 1st Modality of Commercialization ~ Self-Practice
The 1st modality of commercialization is self-practice, i.e. the inventor applies his invention
and makes his own product using his or others’ manufacturing facilities. To this end, the
inventor may have to set up a startup. It would be even better, however, if the inventor
himself owned his own factory.
In this first modality of commercialization, the inventor can generate sales revenue by
selling his product in the market, and then take a portion of the sales revenue as his profit.
Of course, the commercialization may fail if the inventor cannot sell his product in the
market or if the balance stays in the red even after the inventor has generated sales
revenues.

===== PDF_PAGE 93 =====
Chapter 3. Policy Recommendations
83
2.1.3.2 2nd Modality of Commercialization ~ Technology Transfer
The second modality of commercialization is that the inventor may transfer his technology
and patent to a licensee for profit. Scientific or technological innovators are not usually
entrepreneurial, preferring to work in the lab rather than on the manufacturing front.
It is not surprising to find that most of these innovators tend to be constantly on the lookout
for a potential licensee -manufacturer. When an inventor finds and negotiates with a
licensee-manufacturer and signs a licensing contract, he or she can earn royalties in one or
more of the following ways: (1) an upfront royalty, (2) a running royalty, or (3) a milestone
royalty. The upfront royalty generally refers to the royalty which an inventor receives upon
signing a contract for technology transfer. Conversely, the running royalty refers to a portion
of the licensee’s revenue.
In other words, as the licensee generates sales revenue, the licensee shares a certain portion
of the revenue with the inventor, and that portion corresponds to the running royalty. The
milestone royalty is the one which the licensee pays to the inventor w hen a certain
condition is met or when a certain event happens. It should be noted that the various types
of royalty discussed above reflect different features of technology transfer and technology
commercialization.
For example, a technology transfer which includes the upfront royalty, but not the running
royalty, means that the real fruit of technology commercialization is kept solely by the
licensee. Accordingly, once the technology transfer contract is signed, there will be very little
(if any) incentive for the inventor to assist the licensee in carrying out the actual
commercialization.
Conversely, a technology transfer that includes the upfront royalty as well as the running
royalty means that the inventor and the licensee agree to share the actual fruits of
technology commercialization. Therefore, compared with the upfront -only licensing
outlined in the above paragraph, the latter technology transfer can provide plenty of
incentives for an inventor to assist the licensee in carrying out and successfully completing
the actual commercialization.

2.1.3.3 3rd Modality of Commercialization ~ Patent Assertion
Circumstances may force a naive inventor to go to court to file a suit against a competitor
for patent infringement. For example, there have been many cases in which an inventor has
self-practiced his patent, manufactured the products, and sold them in the market, but
where a competitor has made a dead-copy product and sold this counterfeit in the market.
And, due to the lack of patent protection in the inventor’s country, the inventor has lost his
market.
In some cases, although an inventor has proposed to license the patent, the competitor
refuses. In this case, the inventor’s only remaining option is to take the case to court, file a
suit against the copying competitor, win the lawsuit, and obtain damages from the copying
manufacturer.
In other cases, however, an inventor may not be interested in self-practicing his own patent
at all. Instead, the inventor meets lots of manufacturers, advertises his new technology and
patent, and proposes a licensing deal with those manufacturers.
However, the manufacturer may still choose not to sign the contract and instead copy the
patented technology of the inventor. Similar to the above example, the inventor’s only
remaining option in such a case is to take the case to court, sue the copying competitor, win
in the court, and then obtain damages from the copying manufacturer.
Because the only right awarded to the patent owner is the right to exclusive competitors
from practicing his patent, asset the patent in a court is the only right owned by the patent
owner. There is nothing wrong in suing the infringer.

2.1.4 Virtuous IP Ecosystem vs. Vicious IP Ecosystem
As shown in Figure 1 -1, an invention is transformed into a patent through a series of
processes, wherein different people with different types of expertise play their roles in each
of the upstream, midstream and downstream elements of the IP ecosystem.
Some IP ecosystems flourish, while others perish. The main difference between flourishing
and failing ecosystems usually lies in their efficiency or productivity in generating profits and

in distributing such profits to the different players in its different layers.

Ecosystem

 Source: Shim (2016), p. 898.

[Figure 3-5] is a graphical representation of an IP ecosystem that flourishes in a virtuous
cycle (see the left-hand side of the figure), and another IP ecosystem that operates in a
vicious cycle and perishes (see the right-hand side of the figure).
As depicted in the figure, new technologies and their patents are commercialized (or
monetized) by various professionals in the downstream element of the IP ecosystem. When
a technology or its patent is successfully commercialized, the entrepreneur-inventor (of the
1st modality) can make profits from the sales revenue of the product which embodies the
patented technology.
A licensor-inventor (of the 2nd modality) can make profits from the royalty he receives from
the licensee-manufacturer, while the plaintiff-inventor (of the 3rd modality) can make profits
from the damages which a jury or judge awards to the inventor.
According to the incentive theory of the patent system and, also, our common sense, once
an inventor receives his share of profits, this financially rewarded and emotionally charged
inventor will go on to make the next generation of innovations, inventions, and patents.
In short, an IP ecosystem operating according to a virtuous cycle always has channels in
which profits generated in the downstream can be funneled back to the upstream. As a
result, inventors are motivated to make inventions, to obtain patent protection, and to

commercialize their technologies and patents.
Because an IP ecosystem operating in such a virtuous cycle has a larger pool of serious and
qualified inventors, the patent attorneys or agents serving those inventors can also make
profits while assisting them in getting their inventions issued into patents. As this virtuous
cycle goes on, all players in each layer of the IP ecosystem can make profits. In addition, such
players can invest at least a portion of their profits back into the IP ecosystem. Accordingly,
the IP ecosystem flourishes while acquiring additional width and depth.
Conversely, in an IP ecosystem which operates in a vicious cycle, everything goes in exactly
the opposite direction. Without knowing about the “vicious feature” of the system, an
inventor invests her time, effort, and money in coming up with a decent invention. Then,
the inventor spends more money in filing a patent application and acquiring a patent.
However, the inventor cannot make any profits through commercialization due to , for
example, a lack of (or lax) patent protection. Some persistent inventors may go through this
vicious cycle a few more times, but it will not take long for an inventor to realize that
generating inventions and innovations and obtaining patents only help s his copycat
competitors. The inventor will then determine to join the pool of copiers and, ultimately, there
will be no one in the pool of inventors and innovators. As a result, the system will have far
fewer innovations and inventions, and the public will not be able to reap any benefit from
technology.

2.2.2 Extra knowledge required for innovators and inventors in the
upstream segment of the IP ecosystem
Inventions can be conceived in many fields of life. More often than not, inventors in the
fields of science or engineering perform research and conceive inventions. As a result,
inventions are very closely related to various areas of science, such as chemistry, physics,
biology or geology, or various branches of engineering, such as chemical engineering,
biochemical engineering, electrical or electronic engineering, mechanical engineering,
computer science, or material science, etc. Of course, the inventors who conceive such
inventions obtain the required background knowledge through collegiate or graduate
studies.
Although not as common as inventions in the fields of science and engineering, inventions
can also be conceived in non-scientific or non-engineering fields. Typical examples include
inventions related to methods of doing business. Such inventions can be conceived not only
by scientists and engineers but also by ordinary people.
Inventors typically obtain proper background knowledge through collegiate stud ies or
graduate education. Typically, a government may or may not do extra work to prepare such
inventors to generate more inventions.
However, the Azerbaijan government needs to train and equip such inventors with extra
knowledge about the dynamics of the IP ecosystem and the basic nature of the patent
system. For example, inventors need to know all about (1) the difference between R&D (and
academic papers) and inventing (and patents); (2) inventorship (e.g. who the sole inventor
is or who the joint inventors are); (3) ownership of the patents (this is crucially important in
conducting co-R&D with other domestic or foreign partners); and (4) how competitors can
detect weaknesses in inventors’ inventions or patents, etc.
It has been noted that the R&D-IP strategy or the IP-R&D strategy, as described in Section

2.2.3 Extra knowledge required for patent law professionals in the
midstream segment of the IP ecosystem
Patent law professionals are those persons who are well versed in the domestic patent laws
and systems. In most countries, they have to pass a domestic patent bar examination to
become a patent agent or patent attorney . Accordingly, there will be no need for the
Azerbaijan government to do extra work to improve the legal skills of such patent law
professionals.
However, the Azerbaijan government may need to assist such legal professionals in
becoming better versed in the patent systems of strategically important countries. Because
the patent law is a territorial law and because Azerbaijan (like Korea) is a small country, it
would be far better if the valuable inventions created by Azerbaijan’s inventors were to be
patented in such strategically important countries as the US, certain EU nations including
the United Kingdom, France and Germany, and China and Japan among others.
Azerbaijan’s patent law professionals could upgrade their skills by themselves and acquire
the capability to upgrade the quality of Azerbaijan’s patent applications, which in turn could
be translated into English, German, French, Chinese or Japanese, and then converted into
US, British, German, French, Chinese or Japanese patents.
In practice, however, it would cost much time and money for Azerbaijan’ s patent law
professionals to learn about the patent systems of diverse foreign countries by themselves.
In addition, even if these professionals were prepared to do all this, they would not be able
to recoup their time and resources unless Azerbaijan’s inventors were interested in filing
patent applications in those countries.

2.3.1 Selected Topics from IP Lecture 1
2.3.1.1 How to Invent? Working vs. Hypothetical Examples
When an inventor conceives an invention, he or she can prove that invention by
constructing a working example. The working example may include a mock-up model or a
physical proto-type, and so on. Based thereon, the inventor can hire a patent attorney who
will then prepare a patent application and file the application to a patent office. Of course,
the patent attorney includes the working example in the patent application. However, the
inventor may take an alternative route. That is, instead of constructing a working example,
the inventor can purely rely on his imagination, and come up with an example which he or
she does not actually test in the real world. This example is referred to as a prophetic
example. Because it has not been proven, the prophetic example may not be properly
included in an academic paper. However, because the patent aims for a purpose (profit
generation) which differs from the purpose of the academic article ( i.e. the pursuit of a
universal truth), the prophetic example can be used in a patent application. For example,
the MPEP (Manual of the Patent Examining Procedure) of USPTO allows an applicant for a
patent to use a prophetic example in order to improve the enablement of the invention. As
a result, the patent applicant should not be shy of using such prophetic examples.

2.3.1.2 Which is More Important? The Claim or the Specification?
It is the patent claim itself which is analyzed against various requirements of the patent law,
for example, in determining its novelty or non-obviousness. Accordingly, the patent claim
must be written to survive these requirements. In addition, the scope of the claim also
determines the scope of infringement. Therefore, a competitor may easily infringe a claim
with a broader scope, or may easily get around and escape infringement when a claim has
a narrower scope. In this regard, the claim is a very important part of a patent. However,
when a competitor finds a hole in the scope of a patent, it can attempt to escape the scope
of the claim. A patent owner (or a patentee) may file a continuation or divisional application

===== PDF_PAGE 109 =====

while claiming the earlier filing date of the parent patent, and may trap the competitor. To
do this, however, all elements and limitations of the claims of the continuation or divisional
application must have been included in the specification of the parent patent. In this context,
the specification of the patent may be deemed to be more important than the claim itself.
In other words, the claim of the patent may be regarded as “a soldier who goes to the
battlefront and fight against the enemy”. Contrary to this, the specification of the patent
may then be deemed “an arsenal” from which the soldier can come and pick the proper
weapon of his or her choice.

2.3.1.3 The Patent System Promotes Innovation
Some scholars argue that the patent system hinders or even prevents innovation. The major
argument of these scholars is based on the fact that the patent only guarantees a negative
right. When a patent is issued, the patent system guarantees a patent owner (i.e., a
patentee) the right to exclude others from practicing the patented invention without the
consent of the patentee. However, the patent system does not guarantee the patent owner
to practice his own patent. The main reason for this is that the patent system allows a later
improver (such as an inventor of an improvement invention) to obtain an improvement
patent whose scope may be only a part of the pioneer patent of an earlier pioneer (such as
an inventor of a pioneering invention). That is, all patentees, regardless of whether their
inventions are pioneer inventions or improvement inventions, have the (negative) right to
exclude others from practicing their patented inventions , but the patentees of the
improvement inventions do NOT have the right to practice their own patents (positive
rights).
Anti-patent scholars focus on these problems and refer to the pioneer patents and the
improvement patents as “blocking patents.” They further argue that, due to the blocking
nature of patents, the more patents there are, the fewer innovations there will be. However,
this blocking patent conspiracy theory is counterintuitive at best. Just like patents, other
properties provoke conflicts whenever their property rights overlap. Just like other
properties, conflicts surrounding patents can be resolved as well. Let us suppose that
Howard has a nice house with a beautiful lawn, and that Tom has a nice tent. Tom yearns

===== PDF_PAGE 110 =====

100
to sleep for a night on Howard’s lawn while gazing up at the Milky Way. But Howard does
not allow Tom to do so. Accordingly, one night, Tom brings his tent to Howard’s lawn, erects
his tent on the lawn, and hops into it. Howard finds Tom’s tent, and calls the police. The
police officer kicks Tom off Howard’s lawn, and Tom has no means to stay on Howard’s lawn.
In the middle of this mess, however, Howard finds that Tom’s tent is awesome and decides
he wants to keep it. But the police officer orders Howard to return the tent to Tom. As one
can see, this dispute is attributed to the overlapping property rights of Howard and Tom.
However, our capitalistic society offers different ways to solve this dispute. One such way is
that Tom buys Howard’s house and lawn or that Howard buys Tom’s tent. Another way is
that Howard receives money from Tom and allows him to stay on his lawn for one night.
Similarly, the owner of a pioneer patent (i.e. a pioneer patentee) and the owner of an
improvement patent (i.e., an improvement patentee) can also resolve their dispute in
various ways. The first such way is that the pioneer (or improvement) patentee can sell his
patent to the improvement (or pioneer) patentee. The second way is that the improvement
(or pioneer) patentee can license his patent to the pioneer (or improvement) patentee. The
third way is that the pioneer patentee and the improvement patentee can collaborate and
set up a joint venture company. The fourth - but not the last - way is that the pioneer
patentee and the improvement patentee can together sell their patents to a third party.
In other words, the disputes surrounding patents are no different from the disputes
surrounding other personal properties recognized in the capitalistic society. As a result, the
blocking nature of patents is no different from the same blocking nature of other personal
properties of the capitalistic society.
The patent law belongs to the intellectual property law, which in turn belongs to the
intangible property law, which in turn belongs to the property law. The property law of the
capitalistic society defines which goods have economic value, and how such goods can be
generated, kept, and exchanged. In short, In short, patents are all about money. Accordingly,
just like other financial incentives which lure numerous entrepreneurs into the business
marketplace, the patent system can successfully lure numerous inventors and innovators
into creating more inventions and innovations.

2.3.1.4 When to File a Patent Application? Grace Period
Who can obtain a patent when two different inventors conceive the same invention at
different times? Two systems used to exist: the first-to-invent system and the first-to-file
system.
The first-to-invent system awards the first inventor and allows him or her to obtain a patent,
but it does not give a patent to the second inventor. This system makes a lot of sense as it
is better that the patent be given to the first, earlier inventor. However, the main problem
with the first-to-invent system is that some inventors are inclined to forge a document to
prove the earlier date of their invention. Accordingly, the courts have often had to rule on
who is the first inventor in a dispute, while validating each and every piece of evidence
related to the date of invention. Accordingly, lawsuits surrounding the date of invention can
be costly in terms of both resources and time.
Conversely, the first-to-file system awards the inventor who files a patent application first,
and allows this inventor to obtain a patent, but it does not give a patent to another inventor
who files a patent application later on, regardless of whether this late-filing inventor is in
fact the first to conceive the invention. This system may seem not to make much sense, for
it seems more reasonable to give the patent to the first and earlier inventor of the invention.
However, the principal merit of the first-to-file system lies in the fact that the court does not
have to rule who is the first inventor in the case of disputes. The reason for this is that the
court under the first-to-file system does not have to review any documents about the date
of invention, and requires nothing more than the date of filing, which is well documented
by the patent office.
Despite all the inefficiencies attributed to the validating of the date of an invention, the US
maintained the first-to-invention system up until 2013, and then adopted the first-to-file
system, just like the rest of the world. Therefore, as far as we know, the entire world is now
running the first-to-file system.
It is appreciated, however, that the first-to-file system may not be beneficial to the public in
general. For example, when an inventor conceives an invention, he has to rush to file a
patent application, as otherwise he may lose the chance to get a patent if a competitor files

===== PDF_PAGE 112 =====
2023 K-Innovation Partnership Program with Azerbaijan
102
a patent application for the same invention at an earlier date. As a result, the inventor may
not have enough time to hone his invention or to find more valuable improvements of his
original invention, and the public may not be able to reap more benefits from the same
invention. In addition, the first-to-file system may place unwary inventors at great risk, as it
does not allow an inventor to disclose an invention publicly before filing a patent application.
However, many scientists who are not versed in patent law may publish an academic paper
and disclose their inventions publicly, well before filing a patent application. In such a case,
the inventor may lose the right to get a patent, as his own publication may serve as a prior
art, negating the novelty and non-obviousness of his invention.
To alleviate this problem, the patent law provides unwary inventors with a certain period
of grace, for example, 6 months or 12 months. In other words, even if an inventor happens
to disclose his invention publicly before filing a patent application, as long as the inventor
files a patent application within 6 (or 12) months of its disclosure, he will obtain a patent
provided that his patent application satisfies various requirements.
Most countries now provide a grace period of 6 or 12 month. It has been noted, however,
that China and many European countries previously adopted the absolute novelty system,
in which no grace period was offered. Under such a regime, naï ve inventors who
accidentally disclosed their precious inventions in academic papers often lost the
opportunity to convert the inventions into patents. However, those countries have started
providing a grace period of 6 to 12 months. Accordingly, whatever the grace period may be,
inventors have many reasons to file a patent application before disclosing their inventions
publicly.

2.3.2.2 Novelty Requirement
In order to obtain a patent, an invention which is recited in a patent claim must be novel,
i.e. new compared to all prior art throughout the world. If the invention recited in the claim
is not new, i.e. the same invention has existed before the inventor’s patent, the claim is not
novel. As a result, the claim included in the patent application will be rejected, or the claim
included in the issued patent will be invalidated for not being novel over the prior art.
Why does the patent system require novelty? The answer is simple. Society does not have
to protect an invention which has already become known publicly. If the patent system
protects such a non-novel invention, then that non-novel invention, which has been in the
public domain (i.e. the invention which has been publicly known and which the public has
been able to use free of charge), suddenly becomes the personal property of the inventor,
and the public cannot practice that invention without the consent of the inventor.
In this context, protecting an inventor’s invention that is not novel does not provide any
benefit to the public and society. Accordingly, such a non -novel invention must not be
protected by a patent.
Because all countries have adopted the first-to-file system, “prior art” is defined as anything
which is known to the public before the date of filing a patent application (i.e. the filing date).
Such prior art may include a printed publication, others’ published patent applications,
others’ issued patents (whether expired or not), merchandise, contents on the web, and
such like.

2.3.2.3 Non-Obviousness Requirement
Even if a claim satisfies the novelty requirement, i.e. when at least one claim element or at
least one claim limitation is not disclosed by a single prior art, it does not mean that the
claim is patentable. To be patentable, a claim must also not be obvious. Why, then, does
the patent system require non-obviousness in addition to novelty? The answer is a little
complex compared to the novelty requirement. The assumption is that the public has the
ability to improve or modify knowledge in the public domain. Accordingly, even when an
invention claimed by an inventor has not become known to the public and has not been
disclosed by a single prior art, i.e. the invention is novel, there is a chance that the public will
be able to modify the existing prior art and easily arrive at the claimed invention of the
inventor. Thus, there is no need to protect a inventor’s invention that is obvious. The net
result is that even a novel claim cannot be patented when the claim is obvious in view of the
prior art, and this result can be represented graphically as shown in [Figure 3-11]

However, when a claim does not include within its scope any part of the prior art, but is very

patented. Thus, qualitatively speaking, it may be said that this non -obviousness
requirement is building a buffer zone around the prior art. For the inventor to obtain a
patent, his or her patent claim must not include any portion of the prior art and, in addition,
must maintain a “certain distance” from the prior art.
It is appreciated that the all-element rule is not used to determine non-obviousness. Rather,
determining non-obviousness requires one to asks whether or not the difference between
the claim and the prior art, when viewing the claim as a whole, would be obvious to a person
possessing ordinary skills in the relevant art at the time of filing the patent application.
When the answer is yes, then the claim is obvious over the prior art; whereas, when the
answer is no, the claim is not obvious over the prior art.

As described in the above paragraph, the standard for determining non-obviousness is that
of a person equipped with ordinary skills in the art (referred to as a POSITA). As is manifestly
clear, this hypothetical person, or POSITA, is a fictitious person who is traditionally thought
to have some knowledge of the relevant art. Accordingly, if a claimed invention is obvious
to the POSITA, the claim is obvious. However, the POSITA has long been at best a grey area
in the patent world. In addition, the US courts have issued opinions that have greatly
changed the background or capacity of the POSITA. 35 Accordingly, when compared with
determining novelty, determining non-obviousness is more objective and difficult to predict.
In other words, compared to the determination of novelty, the determination of non -
obviousness is a question that lacks predictability. Given the murky atmosphere
surrounding the determination of obviousness, it is no wonder that determinations of

2.3.2.4 Pitfalls in Prior Art Search
As discussed above, the claim of an issued patent can be invalidated when the claim is found

both practical and legal limitations.

amount of money spent and the number of man-hours involved in such a search. Any
attempt to search all the databases in the world will require large amounts of time and
money. In addition, such a search requires human professionals who can read the prior art,
compare the patent claim with the prior art, and determine whether the claim has been

2.3.2.5 Literal Infringement
To prevent or at least disincentives a competitor from copying the patented invention of a
patent owner, the owner can exercise his or her right to exclude others from practicing the
patented invention without first obtaining the consent of the patent owner.
However, the greater the expected profit is, the more competitors will attempt to copy the
patented invention of the patent owner. A smarter competitor may attempt to patent
around the scope of the patent and thereby avoid infringement.

following: i)direct infringement, ii)indirect infringement, iii)contributory infringement, and

product (or service) that embodies a patented invention without obtaining the permission

provided that the accused product (or service) meets all of the elements and limitations of

2.3.3.1 The Patent System Guarantees Exclusive Rights but May Not
Guarantee Use Rights
A capitalistic society grants many different rights to almost every kind of personal property.
For example, the owner of a personal property has a “use right,” i.e. the right to use his or
her own property. Otherwise, there would be no need to purchase such a property in
exchange for money.
Another important right is the “exclusive right,” i.e. the right to exclude others from using a
given property without the consent of the owner. Otherwise, such a property would be a
community property to be shared by the owner and others. In addition, the owner of
personal property is accorded an alienable right, i.e. the right to sell or lease one’s property
to others. Otherwise, the owner would have to own that property until he or she dies.
Without any of the above bundle of rights, the value of the property would drop significantly.
Like the rights described above, the patent system awards or guarantees a patent owner
the exclusive right. Therefore, a patent owner can exclude others from practicing a patented
invention without his or her consent. Such a right is the basis of patent rights in the US41,
China42, Korea43, Azerbaijan44, and many other countries around the world. However, almost
all (if not all) patent systems do not guarantee use rights, i.e. patent system s do NOT
guarantee the owner of a patent the right to practice his or her own patent. The main reason
for this is that most patent systems allow a later inventor (improver) to obtain an
improvement patent whose scope is a subset of the pioneer patent of an earlier pioneer
inventor.

2.3.3.4 Inventing, TRIZ Principles, Patenting, and Lessons
As proven in the real world, various TRIZ inventive principles are extremely helpful tools in
providing new and creative perspectives for the inventor. With these principles, inventors
may easily find certain features which they had previously ignored or to which they did not
give due attention.
However, the above examples clearly show that none of the TRIZ inventive principles can
help inventors to convert their non-novel claims into novel claims, and that they cannot help
competitors to convert a literally infringing product into a non-infringing product.
In other words, inventing can be one thing, while patenting is another. As a result, the
renders have to be aware of these pros and cons and be very careful in applying such TRIZ
principles to both the inventing processes and the patenting processes.

2.3.4.1 Claim Structure and Its Parts
A claim is structured as a (claim) preamble, a transition phrase, and a (claim) body. In the
US, the transition phrase includes the terms “comprises,” “consists of,” and “essentially
consists of.” When a claim employs “comprises” as the transition phrase, it is called an
“open-ended claim,” whereas it is called a “closed claim” when it uses “consists of” as the
transition phrase, and a “partially closed claim” when it uses “essentially consists of.”
Claim Preamble
The claim preamble is the beginning part of a claim. The preamble typically describes the
technological or legal category or usage of the claimed invention. It is appreciated that the
scope of the claim is determined by the elements and/or limitations included in the body of

===== PDF_PAGE 146 =====

136
the claim. Accordingly, the preamble does NOT usually affect a claim’s scope.
Of course, there are exceptions to this rule. Sometimes the words in the preamble are
construed as the elements or limitations which restrict the scope of the claim. When a part
of the preamble assigns minimal meaning or importance to a claim element or limitation,
that portion of the preamble is deemed to limit the claim’s scope. However, when a part of
the preamble is necessary in construing the claimed invention, that portion may be deemed
to limit the claim’s scope.
In giving their opinions, the US courts have repeated these exceptions using different words.
For example,
① A term in a preamble is a claim limitation when it gives “life and meaning” to the claim.

necessary for the claim’s construction. 48
③ A term in a preamble used to be a claim limitation if it describes the intended use or
purpose of the claimed invention. 49

2.3.5 Selected Topics from IP Lecture 5
2.3.5.1 Why Claim Construction is Required
A claim recites numerous elements and limitations, all of which determine the scope of that
claim. Accordingly, the claim defines the metes and bounds of its exclusivity. For this reason,
a patent owner and an accused infringer always fight about the right meanings of the claim
itself, the claim elements and the claim limitations in the court room day in and day out. The
patent owner always argues that his patent is valid and has been infringed, and presents his
version of the claim’s meaning to the court. Conversely, the accused infringer always argues
that the claim is invalid or, even when the claim is valid, that he did not infringe the claim,
and presents his version of the claim’s meaning to the court. The court then has to decide
which of the conflicting versions embodies the claim’s true meaning. However, this decision
is very difficult to make because the claim has to be compared with the prior art, as well as
with the accused product or service, so various experts come and give their opinions about
the technical meaning of the claim element s and limitations and the like. Accordin gly,
interpreting the claim properly is not an easy task. Due to this reason, the US courts have

developed their own rules for the interpretation of claims, and such rules are conveniently
referred to as the “claim construction.”

3.1.3 South Korea’s technology startup education and promotion system
South Korea's R&D investment budget amounted to about KRW 89.04 trillion as of 2019, of
which the government's R&D investment has continued to increase each year, from KRW
20.5 trillion (23.0% of the total R&D budget) in 2019 to KRW 24.1 trillion in 2020, KRW 27.2
trillion in 2021, KRW 29.8 trillion in 2022, and KRW 31.1 trillion in 2023.
However, in terms of performance, the success rate of commercialization in South Korea is
around 20%, which is significantly lower than in the United Kingdom (70.7%), the United
States (69.3%), and Japan ( 54.1%), ranking 27th worldwide. In particular, in the case of
universities, research productivity - which represents the technology transfer revenue per
billion won of total university research expenditure in South Korea - was just 1.41% as of
2019, which is less than one-third of the 4.66% recorded in the United States (2017).
The situation in the U.S. was not much different ten years ago, with around US$7 billion (8
trillion won) worth of funds being invested in basic research by the National Science
Foundation (NSF) in 2010 during the Obama administration, which invested even more
federal funding, i.e. about $172 billion (180 trillion won) per year in R&D , and yet the

In this regard, Professor Kim Jong-sung (2017) of Boston University introduced Professor

Education and Training Program succeeded in establishing 1,036 startup teams as public
technology-based startups through a cumulative training course involving about 2,241
prospective startups from 2012 to 2020.

3.1.4 Overview of the I-Corps TTT program for Azerbaijan
The NSF I-Corps and Lean Startup curricula promote early-stage startup techniques which
identify customers, validate value propositions, and build successful business models for
commercialization. The I-Corps at the national node university in the US is normally open to
faculty, students, staff, and regional science and technology entrepreneurs with innovations
or inventions that have commercial potential.
The I-Corps Team’s program aims to develop and nurture a national innovation network,
and to support researchers interested in entrepreneurial education and mentoring, with the
goal of reducing the time it takes to bring technologies from the laboratory to the
marketplace.
I-Corps provides real-world, immersive instruction that allows scientists and engineers to
evaluate a technology’s commercial potential, a critical first step toward s successful
translation into products, processes and services that will benefit society.
This starts with the formation of a team. Each team is composed of three to five members
and includes a technical leader, an entrepreneurial leader, and an industry mentor.
Each team must first submit an executive summary via the application portal. The executive
summary must include information about each team member, the proposed technology,

of R&D expenditures, which inevitably involve capital risk because the return on investment
is uncertain. Capital risk increases when more funds are allocated to research and
development. There are also companies that outsource their research and development for
