About This Paper
How does private industry approach the challenge of ensuring the cybersecurity and performance
of the computer networks they rely on? This paper describes how private companies commonly
structure, organize, and staff network and security operations centers, highlighting best practices
applicable to the Department of the Air Force’s approach to protecting its own networks as it
modernizes command and control. We also describe how advances in cybersecurity technology can
reduce manpower requirements when properly implemented.

Private Industry Approaches to
Network and Security Operations
Centers
The U.S. Department of War (DOW) began developing the concept of Combined Joint All
Domain Command and Control (CJADC2) in 2019 as an effort to improve command and control by
connecting systems across the land, air, sea, space, and cyberspace domains.1 The Advanced Battle
Management System (ABMS) division within the Department of the Air Force’s (DAF’s) Program
Executive Office for Command, Control, Communications and Battle Management funds the
development of architecture and systems engineering, digital infrastructure (DI), software and
applications, and aerial networking required for the DAF’s role in CJADC2. ABMS Digital
Infrastructure (ABMS-DI) is a key component of this effort, developing a suite of fixed and
deployable DI solutions that provide lightweight and scalable secure processing, connectivity, and data
management for critical command and control functions. This new network infrastructure must be
both efficient and resilient, requiring dedicated personnel and resources to manage the infrastructure,
troubleshoot problems, and provide cybersecurity. The DAF has proposed standing up a command-
and-control combined network and security operations center (C2NSOC) to support DAF
operational elements using ABMS and ensure that ABMS performance and security meet the
demands of CJADC2.
In contrast to the DAF, which focuses on mission objectives, private industry is looking to engage
customers and maintain services that sustain revenue, and it depends on DI to do so. Organizations
generally operate two core functions to maintain digital reliability and security: These are a Network
Operations Center (NOC) and a Security Operations Center (SOC), respectively. This paper details
common approaches used by NOC and SOC organizations in private industry, highlighting (1) which
practices could improve efficiency in the DAF C2NSOC and (2) situations in which a different
approach might be needed. We survey both NOC and SOC functions and responsibilities,
organizational structures commonly used by private companies, and the corresponding manpower and
staffing requirements. We also describe how advances in cybersecurity technology might reduce
staffing requirements if properly implemented, and we conclude with lessons learned that the DAF
could apply as the C2NSOC and ABMS-DI mature.

Functions and Responsibilities
The NOC ensures network availability and performance across on-premises, cloud, and remote
environments. The SOC safeguards corporate assets from cyber threats that could disrupt operations,
compromise data, or damage brand reputation.
Private-sector NOCs are primarily tasked with ensuring that services remain available to
customers and employees. NOC analysts continuously monitor switches, routers, application servers,
and cloud services for outages or performance degradation. Performance is usually measured in terms
of network uptime, throughput, and latency, which directly affect availability of critical business
services. Network disruptions can also expose the business to financial risk. When disruptions occur,
NOC teams restore service through root-cause analysis and coordination with infrastructure and
vendor support.
Cost-efficiency strongly shapes the private industry NOC. Staffing models often rely on lower-tier
analysts supported by automation platforms; only problems that exceed routine troubleshooting are
escalated to senior engineers. In efforts to reduce redundancy and optimize the use of potentially
limited numbers of staff, many firms also consolidate NOC operations across business units or
outsource them to managed service providers (MSPs) to reduce overhead while maintaining coverage
24 hours per day, 7 days a week (24x7).
2 In-house NOCs in private industry are therefore often as
lean as possible, focusing tightly on availability and performance of business-critical systems.3
Private-industry SOCs focus on protecting data, intellectual property, and customer trust. SOC
analysts monitor logs and alerts from across the enterprise to detect intrusions, malware, and insider
misuse. SOC performance is evaluated against detection times, incident resolution speed, and
compliance outcomes. For firms in regulated industries—such as finance, health care, or retail—SOC
operations also must demonstrate compliance with regulatory and industry requirements, such as the
Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability
Act of 1996 in the United States, and the General Data Protection Regulation in the European
Union.
4
Budget realities influence private-industry SOC operations similar to the ways those realities
affect NOCs. Many companies adopt hybrid models in which routine alert monitoring is outsourced
to managed security service providers (MSSPs), while in-house teams retain control of incident
response, forensic investigations, and communication with executives. Private firms also are
increasingly using automation platforms—such as security information and event management
(SIEM) platforms and security orchestration, automation, and response (SOAR) tools—to
compensate for staff shortages,
5 although overreliance on tools and inadequate tuning can create blind
spots in the security system and create opportunities for cyber threats.

we have outlined. The specifics of each center’s operation (and corresponding size) are largely driven

Centralized Models Versus Distributed Deployment Models
The deployment model chosen reflects trade-offs among cost, efficiency, responsiveness, and
control. A centralized model is one in which a single NOC or SOC handles operations for the entire
firm. The NOC or SOC in this model usually operates from in a single physical location and might
share space with other corporate information technology (IT) functions.
10 This approach is common
in industries in which economies of scale are important and network infrastructure can be monitored
effectively from one location. According to the 2025 SANS SOC survey, the centralized model is still
the most common, used in more than one-third (38 percent) of private firms.
11 Centralized operations
reduce duplication of effort and simplify management oversight, but they also create potential single
points of failure.
12 A localized disaster or system outage at the central facility could significantly hinder
the ability to monitor and respond to incidents. Furthermore, a single-center approach can complicate
responsiveness across global enterprises in which employees and customers span multiple time zones.
Nonetheless, centralization remains attractive for many private firms because it is cost-efficient and
provides the ability to maintain consistent procedures and toolsets across the enterprise.
A distributed model, in contrast, establishes multiple NOC or SOC facilities across regions or
business units. This approach usually entails a higher cost to the organization but enables coverage
across geographies and time zones, can more easily support local regulatory requirements,
13 and
enhances responsiveness in the event of a local facility outage. Distributed operations are common in
multinational corporations with diverse business lines or strict regional compliance obligations, such
as those in financial services and health care. However, distributed centers can also introduce
coordination challenges and potential inefficiencies. For example, a distributed SOC or NOC in a
midsized to large company can have less visibility into relevant data and wield less agency or authority

internal business units, and facilitate rapid decisionmaking. However, the financial cost of maintaining
a fully staffed NOC or SOC is prohibitive for many firms.

models: Tier 1 analysts handle alert triage and escalation, Tier 2 staff conduct deeper investigations,
and Tier 3 (or specialized roles) manage advanced incident response, digital forensics, or tuning of
specialized tools.

30 This formulaic approach to staffing provides a consistent baseline but must be adjusted

Other staffing models focus on maintaining an appropriate workload per analyst, using internal
data on the average time that an analyst requires between opening and closing a service ticket and how

estimates than the 24x7 multiplier but requires more data on frequency of incidents or service tickets

In a NOC, operational complexity is driven by network topology, automation maturity, and the
proportion of outsourced monitoring. Heavily automated environments that use advanced network
analytics and self-healing mechanisms might reduce the number of Tier 1 analysts required, as
described in more detail in the next section. Conversely, organizations managing legacy systems or
complex network architectures often need additional engineers to handle manual troubleshooting and
configuration management. The operational complexity for a SOC depends on the organization’s
threat exposure and regulatory requirements—that is, the perceived risk. Firms responsible for
handling highly sensitive information (such as financial records, health care data, intellectual property,
or government classified information) will likely want a more robust SOC staffing model to provide
more redundancy and to reduce risk. SOCs that perform proactive threat-hunting, intelligence
analysis, and Red- or Purple-team functions could also require additional FTEs beyond the baseline
model.
Staffing for NOC and SOC operations in private industry ultimately must be treated as both a
mathematical and strategic exercise. The 24x7 multiplier can provide a baseline manpower estimate,
but the actual staffing requirement can be affected by multiple factors, such as use of automation,
outsourcing, and organizational risk tolerance. Well-designed staffing models not only ensure
operational continuity but also protect profitability and compliance posture, balancing the demands of
constant vigilance with the realities of limited human capital.

Staffing Challenges
Staffing shortages present a major challenge to NOC and SOC organizations in private industry.
Lack of experienced staff has historically been a significant challenge to improving NOC capabilities.32
Twenty-eight percent of survey respondents in 2024 named either high staffing requirements or a lack
of skilled staff as their greatest challenge to the full use of their SOC capabilities.33 Staffing shortages
can be the result of several issues, such as the difficulty in training junior talent. Many of the skills
required for higher-tier analysts in both NOCs and SOCs are more of an art than a science, requiring
job experience and taking time to acquire. Furthermore, the lean posture taken by private industry
leaves senior analysts with little time to step away from their core duties and train junior talent.
Attracting and retaining already capable talent is also challenging; demand has historically outpaced
the available talent pool. This demand makes it easier for high-performing NOC and SOC analysts to
switch employers in pursuit of higher compensation, more-interesting work roles, and more-
accommodating working conditions.
Technological Advancements’ Impact on Manpower
Advancements in technology—specifically automation, integration, and artificial intelligence (AI)
enhancement—have reshaped the staffing landscape of private industry, including the manpower
requirements for NOC and SOC functions. The adoption of SIEM platforms, SOAR tools, and AI-
enabled analytics has altered how operational coverage is maintained and how personnel are
distributed across job roles. These technologies enable organizations to process vast volumes of data
using fewer analysts, which increases efficiency and scalability, reduces errors, and saves costs.
34
Automated monitoring tools in NOCs can track network performance, identify anomalies in
traffic behavior, and detect faults.35 These tools allow network engineers to focus on remediation
rather than detection. Automated configuration management tools and patching mechanisms can
resolve minor outages without human intervention, thereby reducing the need for Tier 1 operators.
These efficiencies do not eliminate the need for skilled personnel, however. Service outages caused by
automatic patch updates show why NOCs must retain network engineers who can develop, maintain,
and validate automated workflows, manage platform integration, and ensure that automated corrective
actions do not introduce new faults.
SOCs using only manual processes struggle to detect and respond to incidents in a timely manner.
Modern SOCs rely on endpoint detection and response systems and firewalls that use automated and
AI-enabled tools to detect incidents. This approach helps solve the detection challenge but sharply
increases the volume of security alerts that they need to address. SIEM platforms can leverage AI to
filter, correlate, and prioritize alerts, increasing the efficiency of analysts and ensuring that they focus

Developments in AI and machine-learning over the past decade have the potential to further

validating using updated data on attack signatures to avoid bias or false positives.
Although these benefits bear consideration, overreliance on or the misuse of automation can pose
significant risks for private firms. A commonly reported issue with automated detection tools is
excessive alerts resulting from too many tools, redundant alerting mechanisms, or poor tuning; these
alerts create more work for analysts rather than reducing their burden.
39 When tasks and processes are
fully delegated to automated systems, human expertise can atrophy, leaving organizations unprepared
to respond effectively when automated workflows fail. Automated response systems could also amplify
errors when misconfigured or improperly tuned, potentially escalating benign anomalies into large-

visibility gaps and erode institutional knowledge, particularly when outsourced providers control the
underlying models and data integrations.
Advances in cybersecurity technology thus pose a paradox for NOC and SOC organizations.
Automation reduces the number of Tier 1 personnel needed for continuous monitoring, but it
increases the need for technically proficient engineers capable of managing and validating those
systems. The optimal manpower structure in highly automated environments might resemble an
hourglass rather than a pyramid: a smaller base of Tier 1 operators, a narrow but highly skilled core of
senior Tier 2 and 3 analysts, and a deep bench of engineers and architects responsible for the
integration, tuning, and validation of automated systems.
The adoption of SIEM and SOAR platforms and advanced AI-enabled tools will not eliminate
the requirement for human analysts in either NOCs or SOCs. Instead, this evolution should be
viewed as a complement to human expertise to reduce repetitive tasks and augment the ability of
humans to detect and respond to incidents faster and more effectively.

Conclusions
NOC and SOC organizations across private industry are designed to optimize performance while
reducing cost. Best practices involve tiered staffing approaches that concentrate expert talent where it
delivers the highest value. Operational efficiency is typically achieved through tight integration
between cybersecurity technology and workforce planning. NOCs and SOCs are structured to
minimize redundant functions, using automated monitoring tools to consolidate alert streams and to
route incidents based on severity and type. Regular assessments of both operations and tools are used
to improve performance, tune automated systems to reduce false positives, and maintain alignment
between network performance and security objectives. Many organizations leverage hybrid staffing
models and outsource some functions to maintain worldwide operations without incurring excessive
personnel costs. The result is a lean, metrics-driven operational environment in which every analyst’s
time is optimized.
However, direct transposition of some private-sector practices to DAF C2NSOC staffing requires
significant caution. Private industry and the DAF share the technical challenges of continuous
monitoring and incident response, but their operational priorities, risk calculus, and governance
frameworks diverge sharply. Private firms operate under profit motives and optimize practices for
efficiency, scalability, and compliance with regulatory standards. DAF operations, by contrast, are
mission-driven, emphasizing network resilience and mission assurance over cost-efficiency. Practices
that are optimal for a commercial enterprise—such as heavy outsourcing of NOC and SOC functions,
aggressive automation of decisionmaking, or lean Tier 1 staffing—might introduce unacceptable risks
in a military command-and-control context, such as the C2NSOC.
One critical difference lies in risk tolerance and operational authority. Private companies accept
measured levels of downtime and incident exposure if the cost of full mitigation outweighs potential
loss. DAF missions, by contrast, often operate under zero-tolerance conditions in which network
degradation or delayed response can directly affect outcomes. Automation tools that make
independent mitigation decisions without human validation, although efficient in industry, could
conflict with the DAF’s requirement for positive human control and chain-of-command
accountability. Private industry will often outsource monitoring or incident response functions to
external vendors as a way of reducing costs; this approach introduces security complexities for DAF
networks that cannot rely on third-party access.
Personnel dynamics on the staffing side also differ substantially between the DAF and the private
sector. Staff turnover in private industry is expected and offset through standardized training
pipelines; direct hiring of needed knowledge, skills, and experience levels; and automation that
captures institutional knowledge in workflows. Each organization has differences, but the functions
that a NOC or SOC analyst is expected to perform and the skills they need are common across
different private firms. DAF operations—and specifically C2NSOC functions—instead rely heavily
on specialized, cleared personnel whose subject-matter expertise and skills are not easily replaced or
automated.
