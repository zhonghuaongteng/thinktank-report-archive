Drivers of South Korea’s Digital
Regulatory Approach
South Korea’s approach to digital policy is
shaped by a combination of political, economic,
and strategic factors that distinguish it from
regional peers and Western markets. Unlike many
countries in the Asia-Pacific, South Korea has a
highly developed technology ecosystem anchored
by globally competitive firms in semiconductors,
telecommunications, consumer electronics, digital
platforms, cybersecurity, and online services. Its
strength in semiconductors provides a strategic
advantage and leverage for negotiating investment,
trade access, and ICT collaboration. These structural
characteristics help explain why South Korea
increasingly treats digital technologies not simply
as commercial markets but as strategic assets linked
to economic security, national competitiveness, and
technological sovereignty.
At the same time, policymakers remain deeply
sensitive to concentrated economic power because of
South Korea’s historical experience with the chaebol
system. This creates a regulatory environment with
contradictory objectives. The government values
close public-private cooperation to advance national
technology objectives, but it also maintains strong
political support for oversight of large companies
and protections for smaller market participants.
The tension contributes to South Korea’s more
interventionist use of competition and consumer
protection law. While the government wants
national champions, it remains wary of the influence
of large firms.
South Korea’s regulatory culture is also
characterized by a strong emphasis on privacy
and data protection. Public sensitivity regarding
the collection and use of personal information
has contributed to one of the region’s more robust
data protection frameworks, comparable to the
European Union in its scope and enforcement, as
evidenced by the recent significant fine imposed on
Coupang. Privacy is widely treated as a consumer
protection issue, a matter of public trust, and a
component of sovereignty. As a result, regulatory
debates surrounding AI, cloud services, and digital
platforms frequently intersect with broader concerns
about data governance and result in additional
regulation. South Korea continues to support
innovation and AI development, but policymakers
generally view privacy as a prerequisite for public
trust in emerging technologies. 1
Technology ambitions are political, public, and
increasingly global. South Korea stands out in the
region because its technology ambitions are explicit,
politically salient, and tied to national economic
strategy. President Lee Jae Myung has made AI,
advanced manufacturing, semiconductors, and
technology competitiveness central economic
priorities, building on objectives stated in the 2024
National AI Strategy for South Korea to be “one of
the top three AI powerhouses.” President Lee has
intensified and personalized this agenda, treating
AI and sovereign technology capabilities as central
to his administration’s economic program. 2
South Korea’s geopolitical position reinforces this
approach. The country remains heavily dependent
on the United States for security cooperation
and relies extensively on U.S. technology and
investment. At the same time, strategic competition
with China and concerns about economic and
technological dependence could limit adoption of
Chinese technology solutions at scale. As a result,
South Korea actively seeks to reduce external
dependencies. Korean policymakers increasingly
frame the country’s technology strategy as a pursuit
of distinctly Korean leadership and as a means

of exercising middle-power influence globally
rather than as a way to remain aligned with the
United States or any other foreign model. South
Korea’s “responsible global power” framework, 3
its cohosting of the 2024 AI Seoul Summit, and
its participation in international AI governance
discussions all demonstrate an ambition to shape
technology governance beyond the country’s
borders, not merely to regulate within them. 4
The regulatory approach is shifting. All of this has
contributed to a shift in regulatory approach. Today,
digital infrastructure is increasingly viewed through
the lens of broader industrial policy objectives,
including economic security, national resilience,
and digital sovereignty. As a result, regulations
affecting AI, cloud computing, platforms, and
digital infrastructure are evaluated not only on
traditional regulatory and economic grounds but
also for their contribution to South Korea’s strategic
competitiveness and global ambitions.
This shift does not replace traditional market
regulation; instead, such regulation now operates
alongside a broader government objective
of strengthening oversight of critical digital
infrastructure. From the perspective of many
foreign technology firms, these measures create
friction with globally integrated business models.
From the Korean government’s perspective, they
are safeguards that support domestic technology
development, public trust, and resilience.
Operational Resilience Regulation:
Digital Technologies as Quasi-Critical
Infrastructure
Although triggered by failures at major digital
platforms, South Korea’s digital disaster framework
increasingly applies to the broader infrastructure
stack supporting cloud services, telecommunications,
and data centers. Following several high-profile
service disruptions, most notably the 2022 Kakao
outage, Korean policymakers expanded their focus
from cybersecurity toward operational resilience
and continuity of service. This shift is reflected in
the government’s Digital Disaster Prevention and
Response Plan and related legislative initiatives.
Together, these measures seek to strengthen
oversight of major digital platforms, cloud providers,
telecommunications operators, and data centers by
imposing requirements related to business continuity
planning, redundancy and backup systems, incident
reporting, disaster response procedures, and service
restoration capabilities. 5
The policy shift reflects a growing acknowledgment
among policymakers in South Korea that large
digital platforms and infrastructure providers
perform quasi-public functions and therefore
should carry obligations beyond those traditionally
imposed on private firms. Industry stakeholders
generally support efforts to improve resilience,
but they have expressed concern that broad
preparedness, reporting, and infrastructure
requirements could impose significant compliance
costs, expand government oversight of privately
operated digital services, and ultimately prevent
governments from using the most effective digital
services available. The debate illustrates South
Korea’s increasing willingness to treat digital
infrastructure as a matter of operational resilience
and public safety, recognizing digital services
as essential infrastructure rather than simply
commercial platforms.
In regional comparisons, South Korea’s approach
is more regulated than others. For example, Japan
has also strengthened operational resilience
requirements for critical digital infrastructure, but
its approach has generally emphasized cybersecurity
preparedness, public-private coordination, and
voluntary guidance rather than sector-specific
regulation of digital platforms and cloud providers.
While both countries increasingly view digital
infrastructure as essential to national resilience,
Japan has relied more heavily on existing critical
infrastructure and cybersecurity frameworks,
whereas South Korea has developed more targeted
regulatory obligations for digital service providers. 6
For foreign providers, the concern is that operational

AI: Building Capability While
Expanding Governance
At the center of South Korea’s broader technology
strategy is AI, which the country increasingly
views as both an economic opportunity and a
strategic national capability. The government’s AI
ambitions are buttressed by a strategy that supports
semiconductors, computing infrastructure, data
centers, cloud services, and domestic AI model
development. Accordingly, South Korea’s AI policy
overlaps with its cloud, data center, semiconductor,
and platform policies: each forms part of the digital
infrastructure stack needed to support South Korea’s
AI G3 ambitions.
At the same time, Korean policymakers have
established a comprehensive AI governance
framework. South Korea’s AI Basic Act and
implementing regulations seek to balance rapid
deployment of AI technologies with requirements for
accountability, transparency, safety, and public trust.
The framework includes obligations related to AI
ethics, verification and certification, transparency,
safety, and high-impact AI. Key unresolved issues
include the treatment of high-impact AI systems,
disclosure of AI-generated content, copyright
questions related to training data, and the
interaction between AI governance obligations and
privacy regulation under the Personal Information
Protection Act. Industry groups are concerned that
compliance burdens could slow innovation. 7
This AI strategy differs from that of both Europe
and the United States. Unlike Europe, South
Korea is actively balancing the building of globally
competitive AI companies while establishing a
comprehensive governing framework. It is also
pursuing a more active government role in shaping
market outcomes and directing investment. The
resulting framework reflects the country’s broader
ambition to create domestic AI capabilities while
maintaining public confidence in the technology.
This could also position South Korea as a potential
case study of balancing innovation-focused
and risk-focused approaches to AI governance,
particularly if it can demonstrate that the
development of domestic capability can coexist
with rules for credible accountability. Whether the
country can successfully maintain that balance as
its digital regulatory framework evolves remains an
open question.
In the region, South Korea’s approach to AI
development and governance is different from that
of Japan. Japan also has exercised global influence,
positioning itself internationally as a champion
of the Hiroshima AI Process, international
governance, voluntary commitments, and risk-
based approaches. In doing so, it has generally
avoided a comprehensive approach to the regulation
of AI that would discourage investment. By contrast,
South Korea’s AI Basic Act integrates governance
with industrial policy, infrastructure development,
and national competitiveness. Japan’s approach is
more focused on interoperability, risk management,
and guidance. In other words, South Korea puts the
government more distinctly in the role of industrial
planning, driving development of the AI ecosystem.
Online Platforms: Regulating Market
Power and Behavior
Platform regulation has become one of the most
visible manifestations of South Korea’s evolving
approach to digital governance. Historically, Korean
policymakers were focused primarily on consumer
protection and competition concerns involving
domestic technology champions such as Naver and
Kakao. More recently, their regulatory attention has
expanded to encompass the role of global technology
firms, platform dependency, data concentration,
and ecosystem-level market influence. South Korea’s
platform policies should also be viewed through the
lens of digital sovereignty: unlike many markets that

depend heavily on foreign technology firms, South
Korea has strong domestic platforms that compete
directly with global providers, including Naver and
Kakao. Of all the foreign industry complaints in
South Korea, foreign providers feel most directly
targeted through the country’s application of
competition policy.
Competition policy. Competition regulation
of digital platforms is becoming an increasingly
important feature of South Korea’s digital policy
landscape and a source of repeated friction in
the country’s trade relationship with the United
States. The Korea Fair Trade Commission (KFTC)
has devoted growing attention to concerns
regarding self-preferencing, intermediary fairness,
discriminatory treatment of business users, and
the ability of dominant platforms to leverage
market power across adjacent markets. Proposed
legislation, including the reintroduced Fairness of
Online Platform Intermediary Transactions Act,
seeks to address perceived imbalances between large
digital intermediaries and businesses that depend
on them for access to consumers. Supporters view
these measures as necessary to preserve competition
and protect smaller market participants. Critics
argue that South Korea risks moving toward a more
interventionist and sector-specific competition
framework than many peer jurisdictions.
U.S. government and private-sector stakeholders
have identified online platform regulation and
KFTC enforcement as a significant concern,
with a joint fact sheet released in November 2025
pledging to “increase mutually beneficial trade and
investment” and “address longstanding barriers
that American exporters have faced in the Korean
market.” 8 The American Chamber of Commerce
in Korea argues that proposed legislation could
have a “disproportionate impact on U.S. firms” and
contribute to additional friction in the economic
relationship. Previous research published by the
National Bureau of Asian Research (NBR) highlighted
significant procedural challenges associated with
the KFTC’s enforcement actions. 9 However, Korean
policymakers see platform regulation as a response
to real concerns about dependency, fairness, and
concentrated economic power. The debate illustrates
how South Korea’s domestic competition agenda
can intersect with trade when U.S. technology
companies are significantly affected. 10
By contrast, Japan has emphasized transparency,
procedural safeguards, and co-regulation in its
competition efforts with platforms. The Act on
Improving Transparency and Fairness of Digital
Platforms is intended to promote the “voluntary
and proactive efforts” of digital platform operators
through a system of co-regulation rather than
direct operational mandates. 11 For multinational
technology companies, Japan’s framework has
become increasingly prescriptive but remains
relatively predictable and consultation-based.
By contrast, South Korea presents a broader
and less certain landscape for competition
policy as enforcement, legislative initiatives, and
sector-specific regulation continue to develop.
Interconnection fees. While other countries have
considered different approaches to interconnection
fees, South Korea is unique among advanced digital
economies in its application of regulated network
cost-allocation concepts to internet traffic generated
by large content providers. It should be noted that
the country’s strong broadband infrastructure is a
point of national pride, and the debate over network
cost allocation has increasingly been framed as a
question of fairness. Specifically, the issue revolves
around whether large traffic-generating platforms
should contribute more directly to the costs of
maintaining and expanding national broadband
networks. The strength of South Korea’s broadband
infrastructure, together with the political influence
of domestic network operators, has shaped the
evolution of this policy. Critics argue that the
resulting framework places foreign digital service
providers at a competitive disadvantage, while
supporters view it as an issue of fairness for use
of the network. By comparison, Japan, which has
an equally impressive broadband network and
faces similar market challenges, has occasionally
examined network cost-allocation issues. But

argue that the framework imposes disproportionate
costs on large foreign content providers and may
discourage investment and competition in digital
infrastructure. However, Korean policymakers and
domestic network operators generally maintain that
this is an appropriate mechanism for allocating
infrastructure costs and supporting continued
network investment. 12
Cloud Computing: Security, Resilience,
and Sovereign Control
Cloud computing occupies a particularly
important position within South Korea’s technology
strategy because policymakers increasingly view
it as both a strategic enabler of AI and a potential
point of foreign dependency. Cloud infrastructure
underpins AI training, government digital services,
enterprise modernization, and data center growth.
Yet cloud services remain an area where domestic
champions face intense competition from large
foreign providers. The South Korean government
has identified cloud and supporting platform
technologies as vulnerabilities for its sovereignty
objectives, creating a strong policy focus on
expanding domestic capabilities and preserving
government control over sensitive public-sector
workloads. Cloud computing is probably the area
where South Korea’s digital sovereignty objectives
most directly intersect with industrial policy,
creating the strongest perception among foreign
firms that regulation in this space also functions as
a competitive advantage for domestic providers.
One of the most significant examples is the
Cloud Security Assurance Program (CSAP), which
establishes requirements for cloud providers
seeking to serve public-sector customers. Because
government procurement is central to cloud
market development, CSAP has become a major
point of tension between South Korea’s sovereignty
objectives and foreign providers’ global operating
models. Foreign industry groups have argued that
Korea-specific requirements regarding network
separation, data management, security certification,
and operations diverge from global standards and
create barriers for international cloud providers,
effectively preventing the South Korean government
from adopting leading cloud-based services. South
Korea, by contrast, treats these requirements as
part of a broader effort to strengthen national
security, protect public-sector systems, reduce
dependency, and ensure resilience of critical digital
infrastructure. 13 Foreign industry groups assert
that it has become increasingly difficult to argue
the policy is not discriminatory as U.S. providers
have been certified to the standard and still not
won contracts.14
As already noted, cloud regulation is also linked to
the digital disaster and resilience agenda discussed
above. Following high-profile service disruptions,
Korean policymakers placed greater emphasis
on disaster recovery, continuity of operations,
and infrastructure resilience. These concerns are
reflected not only in platform policy but also in cloud-
related rules because cloud services increasingly
support public-sector operations, AI workloads,
and essential digital services. As is the case now and
going forward, South Korean cloud policy will likely
be determined by the tension between attracting
global investment in advanced cloud capabilities
and strengthening domestic capacity and sovereign
control over critical digital infrastructure. This

Conclusion
Taken together, South Korea and Japan exemplify two
different approaches to digital growth and regulation.
Even though Japan is expanding its oversight of
platforms and AI, its incremental, consultative approach
remains grounded in voluntary standards, regulatory
coordination, and interoperability with international
norms. South Korea’s more interventionist model,
by contrast, reflects a deliberate strategy of using
regulation to advance domestic industrial policy,
protect economic sovereignty, and assert leverage
over powerful market participants. The cost in South
Korea is a more unpredictable, fragmented, and at
times discriminatory environment for international
technology companies. For the technology sector,
this divergence matters: it shapes where firms can
compete on equal footing and complicates efforts
to build coordinated regional approaches to AI and
platform governance. For the Asia-Pacific, it raises
broad questions of regulatory modeling, technology
ambition, and how this all translate across the region
with both regional competitors and collaborators. •
