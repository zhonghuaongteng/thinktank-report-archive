About This Report
Transformative artificial intelligence (AI)—that is, advanced systems that have the potential for broad soci -
etal impact—presents safety and societal risks, as well as prospective benefits. Although governments retain
an indispensable role in governing AI, business and civil society organizations play material roles in how AI
is designed, deployed, used, and monitored. For these reasons, in this report, we examine how business and
civil society actors can help mitigate the societal and safety risks of transformative AI in ways that comple-
ment government action.
More concretely, we develop a structured framework outlining actions and responsibilities nongovern -
mental actors can take. Drawing on an existing body of literature, stakeholder interviews, and observations
from RAND-designed games, we specify three overarching roles for nongovernmental actors: (1) managing
technical and operational risks within development and deployment, (2) shaping incentives for safety through
market and network mechanisms, and (3) supporting social stability and public trust during periods of AI-
related change. Under each role, we detail potential responsibilities that include internal risk-management
systems, incident reporting, safety-oriented procurement, workforce transition planning, and community
resilience measures. We also identify actions that can inform or reinforce public policy and complement
governmental roles.
This report is intended for business and civil society audiences seeking to understand how non
-
go
vernmental actors can contribute to mitigating the societal and safety risks of transformative AI and for
researchers investigating governance roles beyond that of the state. We draw on and synthesize a substan -
tial body of literature on AI safety, governance, and policy, and we have written it to be accessible to readers
entering these fields without assuming prior expertise.

Summary
In this report, we examine how business and civil society actors can help mitigate the societal and safety
risks of transformative artificial intelligence (AI) in ways that complement, rather than replace, govern -
ment action. We integrate a targeted literature review with semistructured interviews and observations from
RAND-designed games to identify roles that are actionable under existing legal and market conditions, the
enabling “plumbing” that is required for safety, and levers that can raise the level of baseline practice ahead
of regulation. Our analysis focuses on measures that could plausibly be implemented today and notes where
government authority remains essential.

Looking Beyond the Government’s Toolkit
The rapid advance of artificial intelligence (AI) is reshaping expectations about future economic, politi -
cal, and military power. Leading companies in the United States, China, and Europe are pursuing increas -
ingly capable systems, and some are explicitly aiming at artificial general intelligence (AGI). Properly devel -
oped and governed, these systems could spur productivity, accelerate scientific discovery, and create tools
to address enduring public-policy challenges. At the same time, these systems could introduce serious risks,
from destabilizing military innovations and pervasive disinformation to labor disruption and social disloca -
tion; in the most extreme scenarios, some analysts argue that they could generate catastrophic or even exis -
tential risks.
Many of the scientists and technologists who pioneered modern AI—including Nobel laureate Geoffrey
Hinton
1—have warned that advanced AI could pose such risks. Prominent industry leaders have also pub -
licly assigned nontrivial probabilities to extreme outcomes; for example, Anthropic chief executive officer
Dario Amodei has suggested that the chance of catastrophic failure could be on the order of one in four.
2
Whether one accepts these specific estimates, the underlying policy question is straightforward: How should
societies manage safety and societal risks from increasingly capable AI while preserving benefits?
Most research and public debate start from the premise that governments should lead in managing risk.
Yet several dynamics now test the assumption that government will necessarily lead. Frontier research and
development (R&D) and AI deployment are concentrated in private firms, technical progress is outpacing
policy and regulation cycles,
3 and the surfaces of impact lie largely outside direct state control. Given that
frontier AI is being developed and deployed in contexts in which government is neither the sole nor the
first mover, understanding the extent to which and how nongovernmental institutions, across the business
community and civil society, can lead or support the management of safety and societal risks is an urgent
research priority.
This report addresses that gap by specifying where nongovernmental institutions can lead, where they
should support, and how they can complement government in ways that are feasible, effective, and demo -
cratically legitimate. We treat business and civil society as central actors in governing AI in a setting in which
innovation is largely driven by industry. Methodologically, we synthesize the literature and incorporate orig-
inal empirical evidence (from semistructured interviews and observations from RAND-designed games) to
map roles and responsibilities and assess incentives and constraints within a practical framework.

of this writing in early 2026. Appendix B provides more information on the methodological approach. An

In the sections that follow, we summarize key insights from the literature and empirical findings that help

Summary of Key Literature
In this review, we survey leading work on nongovernmental approaches to AI risk management. We organize
the literature using four categories that move from mechanisms closest to where AI systems originate to those
that operate at progressively greater distance from the technology itself: (1) internal risk management and
safety governance, (2) independent evaluation and assurance, (3) multistakeholder coordination and norm-
setting, and (4) civil society oversight and public accountability. These categories capture a spectrum of gov -
ernance functions—from the technical and organizational to the political and societal—that the literature
identifies as necessary complements to government action.
Corporate Safety and Governance Practices
Frontier AI developers are increasingly expected to institute internal risk management practices to ensure
that their technologies are safe and trustworthy.
8 Best practices for frontier labs are increasingly framed as
a continuous risk-management life cycle: predeployment and postdeployment evaluations, red-teaming and
dangerous-capability testing, incident response and cybersecurity, independent or third-party assessment,
meaningful usage controls, and transparency artifacts that allow external scrutiny.
9
Leading frontier labs have published structured safety frameworks that formalize these practices. 10
Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy, introduced in 2023, was among the first to articulate a tiered approach
in which models are assessed against defined capability levels, and advancing to higher tiers triggers increas -
ingly stringent containment and deployment requirements. Google DeepMind’s Frontier Safety Framework
follows a similar logic, identifying critical capability levels and specifying the mitigations that are required
before deployment. OpenAI’s Preparedness Framework establishes internal risk scorecards across categories
that include cybersecurity and biological threats, and there are thresholds that determine whether a model
can proceed. These frameworks share a common structure—defined evaluation criteria, escalation pathways
when risks are detected, and postdeployment monitoring—although how rigorously these frameworks are
applied is difficult to assess from outside, and published safety reports vary considerably in specificity.
Adopting safety frameworks and design principles is one thing; sustaining them organizationally is
another. The question is how firms institutionalize risk governance so that safety commitments survive
pressure from competing priorities. We have already seen these safety frameworks at the labs change sub -

The literature is clear that corporate governance has its limits. A central concern is whether profit-driven
firms can sustain stringent safety practices that might conflict with short-term commercial interests. Tradi -
tional corporate law incentivizes maximizing shareholder value, which can discourage investments in ethics
and safety that do not directly contribute to profit. 17 Some frontier AI companies have experimented with
nontraditional corporate structures to prioritize their mission—for instance, OpenAI was founded as a non -
profit and later became a capped-profit hybrid, and Anthropic’s governing documents center a public ben -
efit mission. These structures aim to give boards more leeway to prioritize safety over profits. Yet OpenAI’s
past few years illustrate the fragility of such mechanisms: In late 2023, the company’s board (composed
of nonprofit trustees) abruptly fired the chief executive officer, Sam Altman, over transparency concerns,
only to see an internal and investor backlash force a reversal within days.
18 In 2025, OpenAI restructured
by converting its commercial arm into a Delaware Public Benefit Corporation (OpenAI Group Public Ben -
efit Corporation)—a for-profit structure—while its nonprofit parent (now the OpenAI Foundation) retained
control; the recapitalization closed on October 28, 2025.
19
The broader lesson is that voluntary corporate governance measures require supportive incentive struc -
tures. Market forces can undermine even sincere efforts at responsible AI development, unless there are
countervailing rewards or requirements for safety. Some incentives are reputational (e.g., being seen as an
“ethical AI leader” can attract business and talent) or anticipatory (preempting regulation by self-policing).
In summary, corporate self-regulation is a vital first layer of AI governance and has produced many prom-
ising practices, but it works best in tandem with external oversight and transparency. These limitations have
driven growing interest in independent assurance mechanisms that can validate and reinforce internal efforts.

Summary Findings from Semistructured Interviews
The semistructured interviews present an emerging picture of how practitioners in business and civil soci -
ety perceive transformative AI risks, where those risks materialize, and which nonstate interventions seem
feasible to interviewees in the near term. The interview protocol covered such topics as perceived AI risks
in the near versus long term, existing practices for managing AI risk within organizations, views on what
industry or civil society could do beyond the status quo, business and civil society’s dependencies on govern-
ment action, and concerns about feasibility or unintended consequences of nongovernmental interventions.
Interviewees consistently described a dual horizon of concern. On the near horizon, they emphasized
ongoing harms—fraud, scams, and nonconsensual imagery—that already strain response capacity and are
unevenly distributed. One interviewee warned, “What worries me about these [AI] systems is what is already
happening—AI-generated child sexual abuse material. We are not even close to being able to field all of it,”
noting that those “disproportionately affected are not in a position to participate.” Another observed that
“scams are highly prevalent in Indonesia and India,” underscoring geographic disparities in realized harms.
On the longer horizon, interviewees pointed to potential risks related to loss of control, concentration of
power, and expanding “cognitive delegation.” Several anticipated “societal upheaval” and “labor displace -
ment” and emphasized that “cognitive labor can be displaced,” and another noted that certain roles “might
be able to be replaced . . . in 2 years” as the autonomy of AI systems increases.
Confidence in government preparedness was generally limited across interviewees. Interviewees attrib -
uted this to the pace of change and unsettled mandates that dampen internal incentives for sustained safety
investment in corporations—a twin lack of expert consensus on transformative AI risks and clear regula -
tory mandates that makes it “difficult to create internal company incentives.” A few participants emphasized
comparatively tractable subnational levers: ongoing work on an “AI whistleblower protection bill,” “liability
reform . . . at the state level [e.g., Senate Bill 1047],” and coordinated strategies in which “state-level action—
in conjunction with California and New York” can approximate a de facto national framework. Several also
pointed to local infrastructure tools—“you can take advantage of zoning and permitting . . . data centers” to
enforce certain rules, while cautioning that “companies can threaten not to do work in an area” and that “the
state may preempt local” action.

community-level support should be treated as core elements of AI risk management rather than discretion -

adding that organizations “need a level of human thriving to have a functional business model.”
A number of interviews referred to using operations to improve system safety. Interviewees stressed
building good friction—permissioning, human-in-the-loop checks, and clear escalation routes that slow and
instrument high-risk actions. Illustrative phrases included the following: “Throw up some wrenches . . . add
some good friction . . . build [it] into operations,” “ask permission,” and “create friction between people and
those who have power over technology.” Several also emphasized the importance of documented escalation:
“Write down who can say yes, and when someone must say no,” and the need to “slow down the riskiest path-

the role of public narratives and investigative journalism, akin to “the kinds of things we had around GMOs

A clear finding from the literature, our interviews, and the games is that business and civil society can play
critical roles in transformative AI risk management and governance. By roles , we mean clusters of responsi-
bilities and activities that these actors are well suited to perform because of their positions in the AI develop-

We identify three roles for business and civil society actors. The first is managing technical and opera -

Second, not all recommended actions should be pursued at once. A practical way to sequence them is to
distinguish among three layers of effort: minimum baseline actions that organizations can begin now with
existing authorities and modest resources; coordinated sectoral actions that require shared standards, pooled
infrastructure, or third-party verification; and enabling public policy actions that are likely to matter over
time but depend on government participation. This sequencing matters because feasibility varies sharply
across actor types. Smaller deployers will often need lightweight checklists, shared tools, and sectoral support
before they can adopt more-demanding practices that might be appropriate for frontier developers, cloud
providers, or large regulated enterprises.
Third, none of these nongovernmental roles is a substitute for government action; rather, these roles bring
down risk in the near term and inform smarter regulation in the longer term. Many interviewees and game
participants expressed skepticism that governments will move quickly enough, which is why these nongov -
ernmental efforts are so critical. Over the longer term, the aim is to have a system in which voluntary and
market-driven measures work alongside formal oversight. Our analytic framework therefore also considers
the interfaces to government for each role; that is, points at which private and civic initiatives can support
and be reinforced by public policy.
Role 1. Managing Technical and Operational Risks
In this role, businesses take primary responsibility for preventing and controlling the technical risks of AI
systems, serving as the first line of defense against accidents, misuse, or system failures. Civil society plays a
complementary oversight role, offering external accountability, independent expertise, and pressure-testing
of industry measures. The aim is to embed a culture of safety and accountability within companies develop -
ing and deploying AI and to ensure that critical safeguards exist before external enforcement steps in.
Companies developing and deploying AI are closest to where risks originate—in model training, system
design, and real-world implementation. They have the knowledge and access needed to identify and mitigate
issues, such as flawed training data, model vulnerabilities, or unsafe deployment contexts, before those issues
lead to harm.
Organizations need a first-line approach to AI risk because many of the most preventable harms arise
where models are designed, trained, integrated, and deployed. Interviews and game observations highlight
a spectrum of risks: ongoing, unevenly distributed harms in the near term, such as scams, nonconsensual
imagery, and operational misuse, and concerns related to loss of control, concentration of power, and rapid
cognitive delegation over the longer term. Practitioners also reported that intent is often outpacing capacity,
especially at smaller deployers. What they described as good friction —permissioning, human-in-the-loop
review, and clear escalation pathways—can slow the riskiest pathways without stalling routine work. At the
same time, the governance expectations appropriate for a frontier model developer are not identical to those
appropriate for a downstream adopter or resource-constrained organization. The level of formality should
scale with capability, deployment context, and level of control over the system. Frontier developers, cloud
and infrastructure providers, and high-risk deployers in such sectors as health, finance, and critical infra -
structure should maintain distinct AI risk functions, formal escalation routes, and documented safety cases.
Smaller deployers and downstream adopters should instead begin with a thinner baseline: named execu -
tive ownership, an inventory of AI uses, use-case risk triage, human-override procedures for consequential
applications, and access to external expertise through sectoral associations, auditors, insurers, or shared
service providers. Observations from games also surfaced a crisis-readiness gap. One game mantra—“panic
responsibly”—captures the need to move early where resilience compounds while avoiding brittle or perfor -
mative responses. Taken together, these observations reinforce the case for auditable governance artifacts,

Barriers vary by sector. Regulated sectors already possess compliance, vendor-management, and

Conditions for success: For Role 1 initiatives to make AI safer, broad adoption and sincerity are key. One
risk is fragmentation—if only a few leading companies implement strong internal governance while others
(especially smaller start-ups) do little, then significant portions of AI activity remain vulnerable. Collabora -
tion and capacity-building are needed to spread best practices across the industry, possibly through open-
source toolkits or industry training programs for AI safety management.
Another concern is dominance by large firms. If only the biggest players shape the standards (even vol -
untarily), it could sideline smaller innovators or lead to “fox guarding the henhouse” situations. Involvement
of nonindustry actors, such as academia, standards bodies, or civil society, in designing frameworks can
mitigate this concern. Safety-washing was explicitly cautioned against by interviewees—companies might
create safety boards or publish AI principles as public relations exercises without changing actual behavior.
To counter this, internal measures should be paired with transparency and external auditability. Civil soci -
ety can play a watchdog role. For example, if a company claims to have a robust risk management process,
NGOs or journalists might later inquire about a known incident and how the company’s process handled it.
If it emerges that the incident was ignored, the gap becomes public. Finally, incentives still matter even for
voluntary safety; executives need to see that good AI governance aligns with business success (or at least, bad
governance aligns with business risk). The next role addresses how to create those incentives.
Role 2. Shaping Business and Market Incentives
This role involves using business, market, and network levers to encourage responsible AI behavior across
the private sector, especially in the absence of immediate regulation. Governments hold ultimate regulatory
power (the big stick), but businesses and civil society can craft interim carrots and sticks through contractual
requirements, industry standards, financial signals, and partnerships. The premise is that by altering the
cost-benefit equation—making it in companies’ economic and reputational interest to prioritize safety—one
can accelerate the adoption of safeguards.
Interviewees consistently pointed to tools on the demand side—procurement requirements, insurer and
investor scrutiny, and credible ratings or assurances—as practical ways to raise minimum safety standards.
Game discussions complemented this with concrete no-regret moves: independent security evaluations of
leading labs, shared playbooks and standardized definitions for loss-of-control events, and decentraliza -
tion to reduce single points of failure. These insights suggest that safety incentives are more likely to work

Role 3. Supporting Social Stability and Public Trust
This role addresses the societal and human impacts of transformative AI. Recognizing that AI’s risks are not
only technical malfunctions but also economic, institutional, and civic disruptions, this role calls on busi -
nesses and civil society to cushion shocks and to also help shape how societies could adapt. That includes
mitigating labor-market upheaval, sustaining community resilience, maintaining informed public discourse,
and ensuring that the benefits of AI are broadly shared. But it should not be understood as merely backfilling
government capacity or repairing damage after the fact. Its broader purpose is to help shape a more durable
social contract around AI: What forms of work, participation, and local institution-building remain essen -
tial? What new forms of contribution and human agency should be cultivated? And how can legitimacy be
maintained during periods of rapid technological change? If Role 1 is about safety engineering and Role 2
about economic and market levers, Role 3 is about ensuring that adaptation to AI remains pro-human, pub -
licly intelligible, and socially legitimate.

the creation of pension plans or retraining programs during industrialization, helped ease such transitions

everything—broad safety nets and education policy are governmental domains—but these actors can pilot

Business and Civil Society Interfaces to Government Action
The three roles outlined in the prior section describe what business and civil society can do on their own
authority: manage operational risks, shape market incentives, and support social stability. But these roles do
not operate in a vacuum. In practice, some of the most important nongovernmental actions serve a dual pur-
pose; they reduce risk directly and they compensate for gaps in government capacity or political will. Where
regulation lags, procurement standards and incident-reporting systems do not merely complement public
policy—they substitute for it. Where government preparedness is limited, community resilience efforts and
workforce transition plans fill needs that public programs have not yet addressed. Over time, however, the
aim is not permanent substitution but a working interface: Nongovernmental efforts generate evidence, test
approaches, and build institutional capacity that can inform and accelerate smarter regulation when political
conditions allow. The following are key ways in which these efforts connect to government action:
•
 Evi
dence generation for smarter regulation: By instituting such practices as incident reporting, evalu-
ation benchmarks, transparency reports, and audits (Roles 1 and 2), nongovernmental actors generate
a wealth of real-world data on AI performance and failures. This evidence is useful for regulators. It
allows agencies to move from speculative fears to concrete, measured understanding of AI risks. For
example, if an industry-wide incident database reveals that near-loss-of-control events are happening
with a certain frequency, regulators could home in on that class of models and mandate specific test -
ing or safety requirements for them.
79 If insurers’ data show that certain mitigations, such as having a
human in the loop on critical AI decisions, dramatically reduce losses, that insight could be codified
into standards or guidance. In environmental regulation, a precedent is how data from corporate self-
monitoring of pollution later helped shape emissions rules.
Already, we see small examples. The AI Incident Database, curated by NGOs, highlights which AI
applications (e.g., face recognition, hiring algorithms) are causing the most reported harm; this infor -
mation can help guide agencies on where to focus oversight. And the AI Risk Management Frame -
work, developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, captures important insights
from industries actors. Going forward, governments could actively partner in these learning efforts—
possibly by providing funding for some of the infrastructure (for example, the National Science Foun -
dation could fund an anonymized incident clearinghouse) and by paying attention to the findings in
their rulemaking. This partnership ensures that when governments set binding rules, those rules are
evidence-based and targeted to real risks rather than guesswork.

Conclusion
The evidence gathered in this report points to a consistent finding: Managing transformative AI requires
action from business and civil society, not only from governments. The risks are technical and social, fast-
moving and unevenly distributed, and they emerge where systems are designed, deployed, and used—thus,
well within the reach of nongovernmental actors. The framework that we propose—managing technical and
operational risks, shaping incentives through markets and networks, and supporting social stability and
public trust—is intended to give actors across business and civil society a practical, structured way to identify
what they can do now under existing legal and market conditions, without waiting for regulatory mandates
to arrive.
Progress in each role depends on building governance infrastructure that makes good practice routine
rather than exceptional. That could include board-level oversight and documented safety cases inside firms;
procurement criteria, assurance signals, and investor diligence across markets; and workforce transition
planning and community partnerships in the social domain. These are not novel ideas in isolation; elements
of these ideas are already being piloted across the ecosystem. However, they remain fragmented and ad hoc.
The contribution of such a framework as this one is to generalize those practices, specify minimum baselines
that are actionable across actor types, and identify where coordination among actors is needed to make indi-
vidual efforts stick.
We especially hope that actors who have not yet engaged directly with transformative AI risk will find in
this framework a concrete entry point. Whether or not regulation catches up, the capacity that these actors
build now will determine how well societies manage what is coming.

guide their response.
Infinite Potential Day After AGI exercises are two-hour tabletop exercises patterned after the Day After
format, with ten to 16 participants led by a facilitator whose job is to elicit responses and discussion from the

mend a U.S. government response to developments in frontier AI. In Business and Civil Society games, the
exercise simulates the convening of a fictional Business and Civil Society Council that is convened at the
pleasure of the U.S. President in the wake of a crisis. In each exercise, participants are presented with a sce -
nario that represents both (1) an acute crisis for U.S. national or economic security and (2) a signpost on a
path to a transformative AI future. With facilitation by a simulated National Security Advisor, participants
role-play as business and civil society leaders diagnosing the implications of the scenario for U.S. national
security, discussing courses of action and the roles business and civil society institutions might take to miti -
gate risks, and recommending a path forward for the President of the United States.
In the exercise, participants face a predeveloped scenario over two turns. In turn 1, participants are asked
to respond to intelligence reports and openly available information about an unfolding crisis involving AGI
and to discuss the immediate policy ramifications. Teams are briefed by a subject-matter expert on the crisis
and can ask clarifying questions about the events. The facilitator then leads the group through a discussion
about policy objectives, options for action, and ramifications of potential decisions. All discussions are cap -
tured by notetakers. In turn 2, the scenario progresses, and the crisis takes a turn, usually for the worse, but
sometimes just in an unexpected vector. Teams are again briefed by an expert on the accelerated crisis and
can ask questions and dig into the policy issues and options for action. At the end of turn 2, the facilitator
prompts participants to retrospectively evaluate their initial responses and strategies. In some iterations of
