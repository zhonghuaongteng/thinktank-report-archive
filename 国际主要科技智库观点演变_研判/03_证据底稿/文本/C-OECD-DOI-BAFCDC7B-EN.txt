Abstract
This paper presents and demonstrates a novel approach for assessing the
relevance of public R&D funding towards societal goals. The approach relies
on the development of a new machine learning classification model for R&D
activity descriptions , trained on researchers’ self-assessments about the
relevance of their R&D activity towards Sustainable Development Goals
(SDGs). Applied to R&D project descriptions in the OECD Fundstat
database, the model shows how funding , including for basic research,
contributes towards societal goals. The analysis allows to compare funding
portfolios and provides evidence of the interdependencies between societal
goals served by R&D funding and its potential to contribute to multiple goals.

Authors: Leonidas Aristodemou, Silvia Appelt, Brigitte van Beuzekom, Fernando Galindo-Rueda
Keywords: Research and Development (R&D), Government R&D funding, Machine Learning, Language
Models, Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), Survey data, Classification, Directionality

Executive summary
What do billions of government investment in research and development (R&D) across OECD countries
contribute to? Traditionally, such question was only addressed through the prism of established
classifications at a high level of aggregation . Available statistics, whilst providing a comprehensive
benchmark, leave unanswered several questions about the relevance of R&D funding towards specific and
evolving societal goals.
To respond to such needs, the OECD has developed the Fundstat analytical infrastructure to develop and
demonstrate approaches for working with administrative R&D microdata and AI tools for analysis of
quantitative and qualitative dat a in conjunction with traditional methods. This paper presents a decisive
step in the Fundstat initiative towards developing tools that can help provide evidence on the directionality
and relevance of R&D funding.
Evidence on the potential contribution of R&D to different societal goals is crucial for assessing the balance
and orientation of R&D and innovation policies. Yet, providing informative indicators remains challenging
because support for R&D can influence the attainment of such goals through multi ple and often indirect
channels. AI-based classification methods applied to administrative data, while promising, are often reliant
on superficial text features and tend to equate lexical similarity with relevance. Moreover, the widespread
absence of expli cit “goal” language in R&D activity descriptions leads to low measured rates of SDG
relevance. Such approaches may understate the relevance of R&D, particularly basic and some forms of
applied research, to outcomes that indirectly advance societal objectives.
To overcome these limitations, this study introduces a n AI-based approach that is trained on effective
domain-informed assessments of goal relevance for R&D. Close to 2 000 participants in the 2021 OECD
International Survey of Science (ISSA2021) reported which Sustainable Development Goal ( SDG), or
none, they contributed to with their research. As it touches on virtually all areas of government policy, the
UN SDG framework provides a convenient and relevant use case in the classification of unstructured R&D
activity by goals. The survey data helped label a corpus of over 1 1 000 academic publications , which
served in turn as the training dataset for fine -tuning SciBERT, a pre-trained domain-specific transformer-
based language model (trained on a large corpus of scientific literature), into predicting goal relevance
probability for a given text description. This yield ed a model capturing relationships between research
content and relevance towards the 17 SDG objectives, as well as the absence of SDG relevance.
The classifier, deployed on the Scopus custom database of scientific publications and the latest version of
the OECD Fundstat , provides a basemap for the R&D landscape comprising approximately 2.1 million
government-funded R&D project awards across 19 OECD countries and the European Union (EU)
programmes implemented by the European Commission (EC ), accounting for approximately USD 1.5
trillion in public R&D funding, between 2015-2023.
The results indicate that over half of total R&D funding between 2015 -2023 is estimated to be relevant to
two Sustainable Development Goals: SDG 9 (Industry, innovation and infrastructure: 30%) and SDG 3
(Good health and well -being: 23%). These goals dominate both in terms of funding volume and project
counts. SDG 4 (Quality education: 8%), SDG 7 (Affordable and clean energy: 5%), and SDG 13 (Climate

Over one-third (37%) of total R&D funding recorded in Fundstat over the 2015–2023 period is found to be
attributable to the “Prosperity” cluster of SDGs (SDGs 8, 9, 10, 11), followed by “Health” (SDGs 1, 2, 3, 5)

or awarded amounts provide more meaningful measures of policy priorities than simple counts of activities
or outputs, such as the number of funded projects or related scientific publications, also offering valuable
insights into the breadth and intensity of research activity. Considering all three dimensions together helps
to account for differences in cost structures across research domains, where some areas, such as health
or infrastructure, are typically more capital -intensive than others. Viewed jointly, these measures provide
a more comprehensive picture of the directionality of R&D funding and its alignment with societal goals.
Recent trends also point to a shift towards fewe r but larger R&D awards, as well as modest realignments
in priorities in response to the COVID-19 crisis.
This paper also explores the advantages of granular data and AI tools to examine the R&D topics being
funded within projects that score highly on envi ronmental and energy goals. An unsupervised topic
modelling exercise reveals research themes attracting public investment, with the topic structure is
consistent with the selected goals (projects exhibit combinations of energy and environmental topics) and
the types of project descriptions that underpin projects relevant to such goals (project descriptions exhibit
different combinations of topics that describe the nature of the R&D, such as scientific topics and
technology domains, on the one hand, and application areas that relate to goals, on the other).
The results also shed light on the synergies between different goals. Awards that primarily related to a
given SDG also make very significant contributions towards other goals. For example, R&D projects
primarily related to (economic) “Prosperity” goals ( e.g., innovation and economic growth) often also
contribute to environmental a nd health goals. Health -related R&D projects are in turn more narrowly
focused but still show links to Planet and Prosperity related goals . Projects with environmental related
goals in the SDG cluster “Planet” are particularly cross -cutting with R&D funding contributing to several
other SDGs. These findings reflect the interconnected nature of societal challenges and highlight the
potential for R&D investments to support multiple goals at once.
These results are no substitutes for studies of causal impact of R&D on outcomes; impact requires
separate ex-post outcome analysis and counterfactuals. These new findings and methods are expected to
equip policymakers, R&D funders, and other relevant stakeholders with evidence to support both strategic
and operational purposes. In addition to extending the scope of Fundstat, possible avenues for future work
include extending the application of this SDG classifier to other scientific data sources and exploring its
use in mapping policy -relevant research portfolios, su ch as in health, climate, or energy domains , and
assessing in more detail the orientations of support for emerging technologies .

Science, technology, and innovation (STI) play a pivotal role in addressing global challenges by
documenting their nature, scale and features, as well as by facilitating new solutions that overcome critical
and hard to accept trade -offs under the state of the art of knowledge and technology . Evidence on the
contribution of research and development (R&D) , i.e., activity aiming to create and develop brand new
knowledge and solutions (OECD, 2015[1]), to a wide range of societal goals is crucial for assessing the
balance and orientation of STI policies. It also plays a key part in communicating the return to public
investments in R&D. Yet providing informative indicators and analysis on the goals of R&D is challenging
because of the multiple channels through which support for R&D is intended to and ultimately impacts on
the attainment of such goals.
OECD’s long-standing work on classifying government budgets for R&D across societal goals focuses on
assessing government’s overarching aims as the result of the political process of setting the national
budget. National experts report data to the OECD according to internationally agreed standards, matching
budgetary lines to a defined range of “socioeconomic” objectives that closely match government functions
like defence and health, and which include public goods such as the general advancement of knowledge
(OECD, 2015[1]). However, several policy questions require identifying what publicly funded R&D can be
ultimately relevant for, regardless of the high -level stated aims and independently of any given
classification framework. This requires having flexible allocation and classification mechanisms as well as
data enabling granular analysis to serve different user needs . The OECD has recently developed the
Fundstat analytical infrastructure with such an objective, working with R&D project award level microdata
and using AI tools for analysing unstructured text descriptions of such awards . Examples of recent
applications of Fundstat include studies that focus on both content , relevance and directionality of R&D
funding, such as artificial intelligence (AI) development (Yamashita et al., 2021 [2]) and COVID-19
(Aristodemou et al., 2023[3]).
In the absence of formal requirements for disclosing in a structured fashion what STI activities are relevant
for, the key conceptual and practical measurement challenge is identifying goals from information available
about STI activities, such as data on inputs from R&D project funding awards, or outputs such as scientific
publications. This stands in contrast with patented inventions, where requests for protection must disclose
their potential applications, claims which are further assessed by expert independent patent examiners.
AI-based analysis of text descriptions needs to be adequately guided (i.e., trained) to reflect not only on
the content of the STI activity but also consider how knowledge outputs can be key inputs for other STI
activities that ultimately contribute to societal goals. For example, a summary description of research into
properties of new materials may not necessarily disclose that such properties may play a critical for
applications in areas such as health, defence or energy. An appropriate degree of domain expertise is thus
a necessary input into the training of reliable AI-based classification methods.
This paper uses the United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) framework as a
demonstration use case for a n ovel classification approach for R&D goals . The SDGs were adopted in
2015 to focus action towards addressing the world’s most urgent economic, social, and environmental
challenges. The 2015 OECD Daejeon Declaration on Science, Technology and Innovation (STI) Policies
for the Global and Digital Age (OECD, 2015[4]) underscored the importance of Science, technology, and
innovation in achieving sustainable development , emphasising that STI is not just an engine of economic

2.1. Perspectives for assessing the directionality and relevance of STI towards
societal goals
There are different ways to conceptualise and measure the direction and relevance of R&D funding towards
policy goals, contrasting the ex-ante or planned directionality (i.e. the intention to direct) and effective ex-
post directionality (the attribute of h aving a direction) of R&D funding. Based on related OECD work
documenting the multifaceted nature of directionality (OECD, 2023[9]; OECD, 2023 [10]; OECD, 2024 [11]),
Figure 2.1 outlines those by distinguishing between the measurement of stated policy goals, content and
features of the funded R&D work, its potential relevance to the policy goal, and the ultimate impact of
funded R&D activity on the policy goal.

focus is on top-level policy societal or economic goals of R&D funding , irrespective of the content of the
funded R&D and its potential relevance towards secondary goals, since the aim is allocation of financial

To analyse the content and characteristics of R&D support, other classifications are required,
encompassing elements such as target beneficiaries ( e.g., firms, universities), support conditions ( e.g.,
type of STI activity), and type of instrument (e.g., grants, tax incentives) (OECD, 2015[1]; OECD, 2023[10];
OECD/Eurostat, 2018[12]). While such information does not directly indicate stated goals, it can provide
insights into implied direction. For example, R&D support targeted at start-ups in the pharmaceutical sector
may suggest an underlying objective rel ated to health or SME development. When combined with
additional contextual information or assumptions, content -based classifications may support inferences
about the ex-ante policy intentions behind R&D funding interventions. This information may not be known
by the top-level budgetary authorities, who often grant a degree of decision -making autonomy in the use
of funds not only to beneficiaries, such as universities, but also to intermediary bodies such as funding
bodies, and those in turn to funding committees and panels.
Many policy questions require identifying what goals publicly funded R&D may be ultimately relevant for,
regardless of the stated high-level objectives or the classification system applied. Assessing relevance to
goals goes beyond state d intent and demands more detailed information about the nature and potential
applications of the funded R&D, which government ministries and R&D and innovation funding bodies may
not always have at their disposal. R&D activities can contribute to multiple societal goals, both directly and
indirectly, including through intermediary applications or spillovers into other domains. Capturing this
multidimensional relevance requires flexible classification approaches, access to granular data, and the
integration of domain expertise, particularly when using AI -based methods for text analysis. Such models
must be trained not only to interpret the immediate content of R&D activities but also to infer potential
downstream contributions.
Assessing the impact of the funded R&D work on policy goals represents the final and most challenging
element in measuring the direction of STI towards goals. It relies on the measurement of ex -post R&D,
innovation and broader socio-economic outcomes and the ability to establish causal links between funded
R&D work and those outcomes. The level and timing of the outcomes of this R&D work are highly uncertain
ex-ante. Moreover, due to limited data availability and/or coverage, trade-offs may need to be made in the
scale and scope of measurement and analytical efforts with a view to addressing the key policy questions
at hand. To gauge the causal impact of R&D on the policy goal, the outcomes of interest would ideally be
directly compared with their level in the counterfactual scenario in which no R&D support is provided . A
fundamental estimation challenge arises as it is not possible to observe the “counterfactual”, i.e., how much
R&D would R&D support recipients have performed had they not received this support. Being unable to
observe the counterfactual directly, the best alternative is to compare R&D performers that receive support
with those that do not but are otherwise as similar as possible , a strategy also adopted in related OECD
distributed analysis on the impact of public support for business R&D (OECD, 2023[13]; OECD, 2020[14]).
2.2. SDGs and related work on SDG classification for STI activity
2.2.1. Using SDGs to measure societal goals of R&D
The sustainable development goals (SDGs) were adopted by the United Nations (UN) in 2015 as a
comprehensive framework designed to address the world’s most urgent economic, social, and
environmental challenges, setting a broader, more inclusive vision for sustainable development. The
framework comprises 17 interrelated goals that cover areas ranging from poverty, education, and gender

equality to climate action and sustainable economic growth. Together, the SDGs reflect a universal call to

that resembles existing classifications of generic functions of government. For this reason , and given its
widespread recognisability, it provides a relevant test case in classification towards societal goals in
general and for monitoring R&D efforts in particular.
2.2.2. General SDG mapping approaches for STI efforts
The positioning of Science, Technology, and Innovation (STI) within the SDG framework is a complex one.
Since the headline indicator of gross domestic R&D expen diture is one among several used to monitor
SDG 9 “Industry, innovation and infrastructure ”, in principle all government (and private) funding of R&D
then contributes to SDG 9. However, STI efforts can contribute towards any of the thematic goals rather
beyond SDG 9, so a more interesting question is how government funding for R&D contributes to the
different SDGs. For this reason, the OECD Directorate for Science, Technology and Innovation publishes
within its GBARD statistics a set of indicators that clus ter data by socioeconomic objectives into four
clusters that are thematically related to groups of SDGs, tentatively mapped and named as Health and
Society (OECD, 2025 [22]), Industry and Knowledge (OECD, 2025[23]), Planet and Infrastructure (OECD,
2025[24]), and Security (OECD, 2025[25]) (Table 2.1).
Table 2.1 provides an overview of the tentative mapping of socio-economic objectives (SEOs) to four SDG
related clusters , including a tentative list of thematically related SDGs for each cluster. GBARD
measurement appears capable of approximating the SDG framework to some extent, in the case of
objectives related to Health (NABS07; SDG 3), Education ( NABS09; SDG 4), Energy (NABS05; SDG 7)
and Environment ( NABS02; SDGs 6, 12, 13, 14, 15) which can be directly connected to their SEO
counterparts. However, several SEO-SDG correspondences are rather ambiguous. While various SDGs
(SDG 6, 12, 13, 14, 15) can be incorporated under one SEO in the case of Environment ( NABS02), the
reverse applies in the case of SDG 9 ( Industry, innovation and infrastructure ) which appears to be
thematically related to both industry ( NABS11) and infrastructure ( NABS04) related socio -economic
objectives. Some SDGs such SDG 1 (No poverty), SDG 2 (Zero hunger), SDG 5 (Gender equality), and
SDG 10 (Reduced inequalities) also remain unmapped, being thematically more distant and not directly
linkable to the SEO framework applied in reporting R&D budgets . The complexity of correspondences
between classifications of policy objectives, combined with the fact that R&D funding may be provided with
a view to achieving multiple, possibly interdependent poli cy goals, highlights the need of a flexible, AI
assisted approach that draws on domain expertise in mapping the relevance of funded R&D work to policy
goals such as the UN SDGs.

2.2.3. Keyword and AI assisted classification approaches
Several methodologies have been developed at the micro level to map the contribution of scientific
research (Confraria, Ciarli and Noyons, 2024 [27]), technologies (Massucci and Seri, 2022 [28]; WIPO,
2024[29]; Hajikhani and Suominen, 2022 [30]) and polic y documents (Wincott, 2024 [31]) to the UN SDGs .
Approaches have centred on keyword -based and artificial intelligence (AI) methodologies, each offering
distinct advantages and limitations (Kashnitsky et al., 2024[8]). Benchmarking studies comparing various
SDG classification approaches, including those developed by Aurora and Elsevier, have shed light on the
strengths and weaknesses of each method (Armitage, Lorenz and Mikki, 2020 [32]; Kashnitsky et al.,
2024[8]). While keyword-driven models generally excel in precision, they underperform in recall, i.e., they
fail to capture the full spectrum of research contributions. In contrast, AI -based models offer better recall
but introduce interpretability challenges. The combination of keyword -based and AI-driven methods is
considered to represent the current state-of-the-art (see Table 2.2) (Kashnitsky et al., 2024[8]).
Keyword-based methods , traditionally the most common and easiest to implement , utilise Boolean
searches to identify relevant research by filtering specific sections of pu blications, such as titles and
abstracts, for SDG-aligned keywords. Elsevier, Αurora, and a few others tailored SDG-specific queries that
incorporate controlled vocabularies and expert -validated keyword combinations. Despite the utility of
keyword-driven a pproaches, they are inherently limited by the complexity of SDG -related themes.
Keywords can have multiple meanings depending on context. For instance, a publication on “clean energy”
could be relevant to SDG 7 ( Affordable and clean energy), SDG 13 ( Climate action), or both, making it
difficult to assign a single, definitive SDG label. Moreover, keyword approaches struggle to capture
research that indirectly contributes to the SDGs, as they rely heavily on key term matching, often missing
broader contributions (Purnell, 2022[33]; Bautista-Puig et al., 2021[34]; Romero Goyeneche et al., 2022[35];
Boudt, Inghels and Spithoven, 2024[36]).
In response to these limitations, AI methods, including machine learning (ML), transformer-based language
models, and a variety of others, have emerged as promising complementary approach es to SDG

on the utility or societal relevance of the work. Upstream basic research is difficult to assess based on
content alone, as its goal relevance might not be immediately obvious. Several recent classifiers rely on
text-based methods that classify research based on abstracts or textual descriptions of the work, with
expert bibliometricians’ validating the results but not necessarily the research subject-experts themselves.

3.1. Classification training data
3.1.1. OECD International Survey of Science (ISSA)
The primary training dataset to build the SDG classifier is based on responses to the 2021 OECD
International Survey of Scien ce (ISSA2021) (Bello and Galindo-Rueda, 2020[53]; OECD, 2024[54]; OECD,
forthcoming[55]). The survey collected data from over 3 000 scientific researchers, who were asked a range
of questions on working conditions, societal engagement and research relevance, career prospects, and
other topical subjects like the impact of the COVID -19 pandemic (OECD, 2024 [54]). Under the societal
engagement and resea rch relevance theme, g iven widespread awareness of the SDG framework,
respondents were asked about the most relevant SDG for their research activity. The question put forward
was the following: “You may be aware of the United Nations Sustainable Developmen t Goals (SDGs) set
in 2015 by the United Nations. In your opinion, does your work relate, directly or indirectly, to any of the
following goals? Please select the most relevant SDG”. Respondents were able to select only one of the
17 SDGs or the option of “Not relevant towards any SDG”. Close to 95% of participants (2 911 individuals)
responded to the question . While ISSA2021was not designed to be representative of a given research-
connected population, as it used non probabilistic sampling methods, its value for the present study stems
from its ability to provide an SDG labelled database for research purposes through record linking .
Accordingly, ISSA2021 is primarily used as a labelling instrument (via record-linkage to publications) rather
than a vehicle for population inference.

3.2.2. OSDG community dataset on SDG-labelled text excerpts
The OSDG community dataset is a public dataset that contains SDG-labelled text excerpts, coming from

ability to generalise across different types of text. Each text in the OSDG dataset is evaluated based on its
relevance to one SDG and validated by at least 3 volunteers (Pukelis et al., 2022[45]). To ensure that the
trained SDG classification model is evaluated using high -confidence data, the OSDG dataset is used as
an out-of-sample evaluation test, using only those texts: (i) with perfect agreement among volunteers (i.e.,
100% agreement); (ii) without negative labels (rejections); and (iii) that 5 or more volunteers have agreed
with the SDG label.
3.3. Analysis data
3.3.1. OECD Fundstat database on R&D funding awards
Fundstat is a novel data infrastructure focused on R&D funding awards developed by the OECD for

and completeness of available records. Fundstat has been used for the analysis of R&D funding for AI and

• The relative share of funding allocated via competitive projects vs. institutional block grants.
• The availability and openness of administrative R&D and innovation funding data from national and
regional agencies and organisations.
• The extent of project data/metadata completeness (e.g., funding amount, project description).

sources in Fundstat (Table A.1). Incomplete data at the project level, another factor influencing reduced
data coverage, is relatively limited: less than 1% of project awards ( 19 385 out of over 2.1 million) lack
textual information in either the title or description, while about 7% of project awards (124 441 out of over
2.1 million) have no available funding information. These entries are typically linked to internal
amendments, disclosure controls, or administrative placeholders and are excluded from average award
size calculations.
To interpret Fundstat outputs appropriately, thematic benchmarking is conducted, comparing the
distribution of funding across a selection of societal goals in Fundstat and GBARD, identifying potential
alignment or divergence in R&D funding priorities (see Annex A). Fundstat represents a growing effort to
increase transparency and analytical depth in the measurement of public R&D funding. While li mitations
in coverage persist, it complements traditional indicators by providing insight into the granular composition,
directionality, and relevance of public R&D funding.

In addition to its role in training the SDG classification model, the Scopus bibliometric database also serves

This section describes the methodology and evaluation techniques adopted in the development of a novel

4.1. Model development
Inferring information about R&D goals from scientific technical documents involves the analysis and
classification o f specialised scientific language that contains highly technical terminology, discipline -
specific jargon, and complex sentence structures that differ from general language (Dan Clark et al.,
2023[59]). Furthermore, conceptual differences between R&D content and goals potentially served by the
R&D activities also need disentangling . The SDG classification developed in this study relies on
transformer-based language models, which underpin many of today’s large language models (LLMs ) and
constitute an integral part of natural language processing (NLP), a branch of artificial intelligence (AI) that
focuses on the interaction between computers and human languages (Jurafsky and Martin, 2023[60]). NLP
enables machines to process, analyse, and generate human language in a manner that is meaningful
(Box 4.1).
Model development involved two main steps: training and validation. Training a model requires “teaching”
it to recognise patterns in labelled data so it can make predictions on unseen data. Once the model was
trained, its performance and generalisability were assessed based on an unseen (validation) data set. The
dataset for training and validation came from the 2021 OECD ISSA survey (Section 3.1.1), where the
scientific publications of the matched ISSA respondents to the Scopus author profiles had been labelled
according to the 17 SDGs and a “No SDG relevance” label. The dataset contained 11 719 scholarly articles
(OECD, forthcoming[55]). To account of possible changes in relevance of keywords and textual content in
scientific domains over time and the concentration of the ISSA respondents’ scientific publication activity
in the years 2016-20, the dataset was limited to the 2010-2021 period. The dataset was then split into the
training set and the validation set consisting of 1 000 observations (Annex B). This split is essential to
monitor overfitting and to evaluate how well the model generalises to unseen data. The text dataset was
then tokenised using SciBERT’s tokeniser, and each article’s SDG labels were one-hot encoded.
The SciBERT model for multi -label classification (Beltagy, Lo and Cohan, 2019 [61]) was fine-tuned using
transfer learning to predict the SDG for each article based on its content (Church, Chen and Ma, 2021[62];
Wang and Chen, 2022[63]; Pan and Yang, 2010 [64]). While the underlying training data structure reveals a
single most relevant SDG per respondent, rather than responses to 17 different SDG questions, multi-label
classification (i.e., SDG by SDG prediction) was applied following other related literature (Vanderfeesten,
Jaworek and Keßler, 2022 [40]; Kashnitsky et al., 2024[8]). In multi-label classification, the model assesses

Box 4.1. Language models for societal goals classification
Transformer-based language models are pre -trained on large corpora of text data, and during this
process, they learn general language patterns (Vaswani et al., 2017[65]; Lecun et al., 1998[66]; Devlin
et al., 2019[67]). They can then be fine -tuned on specific tasks or domains, which leads to improved
performance in areas like scientific text classificati on. A well -known example is the Bidirectional
Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT) and its specialised variants like SciBERT , which
have enabled machines to understand context, and handle domain -specific language. While BERT is
pre-trained on general text data such as Wikipedia, its variant SciBERT is trained on a corpus of 1.14
million scientific papers from Semantic scholar. This makes SciBERT effective for tasks that involve
academic articles, because of its specialised vocabulary focusing on scientific terminology and
structures (Beltagy, Lo and Cohan, 2019 [61]). SciBERT’s training on scientific literature enables it to
better handle the technical vocabulary found in academic research (Ambalavanan and Devarakonda,
2020[68]). For example, when analysing a scientific publication on advanced materials for renewable
energy, a general language model might misinterpret the content due to its unfamiliarity with terms like
“photovoltaic efficiency” or “perovskite solar cells”. On the other hand, SciBERT is better suited to grasp
these scientific terms and correctly classify the article under SDG 7 (Affordable and clean energy).
Using SciBERT has many advantages:
• Domain-specific training: Unlike general language models, SciBERT has been trained on
scientific texts, making it more adept at understanding specialised terminology.
• Contextual understanding: The transformer architecture allows SciBERT to understand not just
the meanings of words, but also their relationships across sentences and paragraphs.
• Efficient fine-tuning: SciBERT is already pre-trained on general scientific texts, so fine-tuning it
for SDG classification is faster and computationally efficient than training a model from scratch.

A challenge in this classification task is the imbalance of SDG labels. Some SDGs, such as SDG 3 (Good
health and well -being), are more represented than others, such as SDG 5 (Gender equality). Training a
model on an imbalanced dataset could lead to poor performance on the under -represented SDGs
(Johnson and Khoshgoftaar, 2019[69]). To counter this, two strategies were employed:
• Class weights: Class weights were computed based on the frequency of each SDG. By inversely
scaling the weights accordi ng to the label frequencies, the model focuse d more on under -
represented classes during training (Cui et al., 2019[70]; Hu et al., 2025[71]). The use of class weights
ensured the model did not become biased toward s the frequent classes and paid equal attention
to under-represented SDGs.
• Focal loss: The focal loss gave higher importance to misclassified examples, helping the model to
focus on hard -to-classify instances, by modifying the standard cross -entropy by down -weighting
well-classified examples and focusing more on difficult ones (Lin et al., 2017[72])

The model was trained through an iterative optimisation process where its internal parameters ( network
weights) are updated in response to the prediction error computed by the loss function. Each complete
pass through the training dataset is known as an “epoch” (LeCun, Bengio and Hinton, 2015[73]). Throughout
training, the model aimed to minimise the discrepancy between its predictions and the true targets. Model
performance and generalisability were assessed on a separate validation dataset (not seen during training)
using standard metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and ROC AUC (Aristodemou, 2020[74]).
At the end of training, the training loss, which quantifies the model’s prediction error on the training data,
was 0.003, while the validation loss, which quantifies the model’s prediction error on the validation data,
was 0.058, suggesting a slight overfit with the moderate difference showing that the model generalises
acceptably. The resulting precision score was 0.73, meaning that 73% of the SDG predictions classified
as relevant by the model were indeed correct. The recall score was 0.57, meaning that approximately 57%
of true relevant SDGs were ca ptured. The F1-score, which balances precision and recall, stands at 0.64
(64%), suggesting a fair trade -off between identifying relevant SDGs and avoiding false positives . While
the model was effective at classifying common SDGs (as reflected by the high precision), the recall and
the F1-score showed that there is still room for improvement to consistently capture all relevant SDG
categories, especially less frequent SDGs. The ROC-AUC score of 0.78 (78%) indicated that the model
systematically ranked correct predictions higher than incorrect ones , implying that it could differentiate
between SDG s with reasonable confidence. Accuracy stood at 60%, and while this may appear as a
modest performance, it is important to consider that t here are 18 distinct labels, many of which are often
thematically similar and intertwined.

5.2. SDG R&D topics and interdependencies
5.2.1. Topic modelling of R&D projects relevant to Energy and Planet SDGs
In addition to the visualisation and analysis opportunities provided by the funding landscape an alysis in
the earlier section, o ne additional key advantage of working with granular semantic data in combination
with funding values is the ability to explore detailed R&D topics within specific areas, highlighting what are
the issues that R&D funders are grappling with to support. In other words, what are R&D funders supporting
through projects that are relevant to specific societal goals? As a demonstration example, this subsection
explores the funding awards that score highly on the probability of being relevant towards Energy and
Planet SDGs.
A probabilistic topic-modelling methodology (Aristodemou et al., 2023[3]) was applied to the relevant subset
of “energy and environmental” award descriptions in the OECD Fundstat database for 2020-23. The model
was calibrated to balance thematic granularity and interpretability and achieve a manageable number of
topics. The analysis yielded 37 latent research topics. Each R&D project’s funding is fractionally
apportioned across these 37 topics according to the model’s normalised posterior probabilities. To ensure
contextually relevant descriptors, topic labels are generated and refined via OpenAI’s GPT -4.5 using the
topic generated top 10 keywords and a sample o f project titles and abstracts for each topic (OpenAI,
2025[77]). For visualisation purposes, Figure 5.10 shows the result of implementing a subsequent Principal
Component Analysis (PCA) on the topic probability distributions for the selected awards and plotting the
topics. The PCA reduces th e 37‑dimensional thematic space to two orthogonal components, which
combined account for 27.5% of the data variance.
The first principal component , displayed on the horizontal axis in the biplot, explains about 15.0% of the
variance in the data . Topics with h igh positive loadings on component 1 include biotechnology,
photocatalysis, (energy efficient) neuromorphic computing and precision combustion. Negative loadings
on this factor are found for topics such as Low-emission transportation, Green finance and Climate-efficient
infrastructure.
While it is not straightforward to interpret PCA factors, it might appear as if Component 1 helps distinguish
between topics that spell out scientific evidence or technologies that are or may be relevant for societal
energy or environmental goals, and topics that directly speak to the energy and environmental challenges
that R&D projects aim to address , and may touch for example on issues of technology adoption and
diffusion. It is important to note that that any specific project will potentially combine those different types
of topics to a varying degree. This confirms that the language in project desc riptions (and in the text in
scientific publication abstracts) may combine elements about the substance of the R&D work and their
relevance towards societal goals.
Turning to the second component (vertical axis), topics with p ositive loadings include Marine and
aquaculture, Ecosystems and climate and E nvironmental and ecosystem health , whilst negative scores
are found for Electric vehicles, Solar and battery technologies, Agroenergy and Propulsion . A somewhat
crude interpretation would be that this dimen sion helps differentiate across environmental (top) and
energy-(bottom) focused topics, indeed reflecting the choice made of analysing projects scoring high on
relevance towards either . Again, any given project may contain a mix of both types of topics, but the
emphasis will differ across the funding portfolios.

This report presents a novel method for attributing societal goal relevance to public investments in research
and development (R&D). Using the UN SDGs as illustrative reference framework, it documents how it was
possible to develop a conceptually relevant goal classifier for R&D activity descriptions that is informed by
a domain-specific training database. This approach brings together the relative strengths of survey-based
tools, different administrative data sources (both bibliographic and funding databases), and large language
models, facilitating a balanced assessment of the relevance of R&D towards societal goals in ways that
such methods, in isolation, cannot provide. While this paper’s approach might overstate the effective final
contribution of R&D towards the SDGs, it s analysis provides a sound and plausible set of estimates of
potential relevance which does not understate the foundational contribution of basic rese arch towards
addressing major socioeconomic and environmental challenges.
Whilst potentially not representative of the full bread th of governmental R&D funding across all countries
in the analysis , the results provide evidence on the direction of R&D funding channelled via individually
identifiable awards. The results help characterise the funding portfolios of R&D funding bodies, their implicit
priorities, and provide a guide towards strategic positioning and strategy implementation.
These results on the relevance of R&D funding are also complemented by evidence from the body of
scientific publications, calculated using identical methods. The additional results suggest that, contrary to
previous literature, most of the scientific literature can be deemed relevant towards SDGs, and not just the
fraction that includes explicit language that enables a direct connection to the SDGs as they are currently
formulated.
The approaches developed in this paper also help spell out the advantages of working with statistical and
administrative microdata coupled with flexible categorisation tools. The results show the potential to
investigate what topics are being addressed under R&D projects relevant to specifi c SDGs, a particularly
valuable form of timely STI intelligence since R&D funding precedes publications and other STI outputs.
Furthermore, the micro -based and flexible probabilistic approach developed in this paper demonstrates
synergies across different goals regardless of the need to produce meaningful additive breakdowns for
funding amounts and project counts.
This study provides experimental evidence for aligning public project-based R&D funding with societal
goals. By combining domain -informed resear cher inputs with machine -learning classification, the study
offers a n informed and interpretable mapping of R&D to wards societal goals. The application of the
classifier to Fundstat and Scopus facilitates an assessment of how current R&D portfolios contribute to the
achievement of these goals . Policymakers and funding bodies may leverage these results to identify
possible R&D funding gaps and encourage R&D investments with multiple interconnected goals. Cross-
country differences in the ex-ante and ex-post directionality of R&D funding towards societal goals imply
opportunities for policy learning and international coordination.
There are multiple possible avenues for future work by OECD and other parties. There is indeed
considerable potential to use these methods to assist in the reporting of R&D statistics and the
development of relevant quality guidelines . The methods develope d can add significant versatility in
reporting beyond fixed high level classification schemas. All this requires the continued development of

A.2. Benchmark analysis of the OECD Fundstat database
Over different cohorts since the start of the OECD Fundstat initiative, its working database has
progressively increased the number of sources and countries covered, enhancing its coverage of national
government R&D funding as well . The latest available version of the OECD Fundstat database (2024
version) displays a steady improvement in the coverage of project-level R&D funding data over the period
2010-2023. As illustrated in Figure A.1, the weighted average coverage according to each country’s
Government Budget Allocations for R&D (GBARD) relative to the total GBARD for the 19 OECD countries
in the OECD Fundstat database, excluding General University Funds (GUF), increases from approximately
27% in 2010 to nearly 51% by 2023. Similarly, when measured as a percentage of the OECD total GBARD
(excluding GUF), coverage rose from around 22% to nearly 41% over the same period. Improvements in
the availability and accessibility of administrative data from national funding agencies are likely reasons,

A.3. Comparing the thematic orientation of R&D funding in Fundstat and GBARD
Whilst Fundstat allows for significant granularity in its analysis of government R&D funding, the resulting
analysis may not necessarily be representative of total funding. Thematic comparisons between Fundstat
and GBARD must be interpreted in account of differences and limitations in data coverage and
interpretation of directionality.
Fundstat captures R&D funding in the form of project awards, whereas GBARD reflects the total budgetary
intent, including institutional funding, support delegated to funding bodies for the general advancement of
knowledge (part of which can be relevant towards goals like health or clim ate), large R&D infrastructure
investments, and support for defence-related R&D, that are not necessarily disbursed through competitive
or traceable project awards (OECD, 2015[1]).
Countries differ in the extent to wh ich all major R&D funding agencies and programmes are included in
Fundstat and the degree to which specific domains ( e.g., defence, energy, or mission-oriented initiatives)
are visible in project award databases may differ. As of 2021, Fundstat captures approximately 51% of the
R&D funding reported in GBARD (excluding General University Funds) across the 19 OECD countries
included.
To contextualise the analysis of R&D project awards by societal goals within the OECD Fundstat database
and understand potential biases relative to official statistics, a benchmark comparison is conducted against
Government Budget Allocations for R&D (GBARD) for cases where Socio-Economic Objectives (SEOs)
(OECD, 2025[57]) are thematically aligned with SDGs. This is not possible for Defence which does not have
a truly equivalent group in the SDG framework even though the Peace SDG is notionally aligned.
Figure A.2 provides comparisons between the share of R&D funding in the Fundstat database by country
and selected SDG thematic groups, relative to the related GBARD allocation for selected SDG related
clusters (Table 2.1) in 2021, the latest year for which reasonably complete GBARD data was available for
this analysis:
• Planet (NABS01, NABS02, NABS03, NABS08, NABS124, NABS134). This thematic area appears
to be slightly over-represented in Fundstat but without a clear pattern across countries.
• Energy (NABS04, NABS05). This area appears to be somewhat under -represented in Fundstat.
This may reflect that public support for Energy R&D is frequently associated with infrastructure
investments, defence or security -linked programmes, and institutional support to public
laboratories or utilities, which are typically not awarded as traceable project grants and therefore
not captured by Fundstat.
• Health (NABS07, NABS10, NABS11, NABS123, NABS133 ) appears to be systematically over-
represented on average by a factor of 10 points.
• Residual (NABS06, NABS121, NABS122, NABS125, NABS126, NABS131, NABS132, NABS135,
NABS136, NABS14, NABS09, NABS12, NABS13). The residual includes industrial production and
technology (NABS06), defence (NABS14), education (NABS09), R&D financed from general
university funds (GUF) (NABS121, NABS122, NABS125, NABS126) and R&D financed from other
sources than GUF ( NABS131, NABS132, NABS135, NABS136 ). In Fundstat, the SDG groups
remaining are Prosperity, Education, Peace and none, which appear to be under-represented by
a factor of 10 points.

Model evaluation assesses how effectively a trained model performs on new, “unseen” data. This process
involves applying various metrics to determine whether the model can generalise beyond the training data
and produce reliable predictions. In this context, the SDG classifier, trained using labelled research outputs
from the OECD ISSA3 survey, is evaluated using an external benchmark: the OSDG Community Dataset
(OSDG-CD). This provides an independent test to assess the classifier’s ability to identify Sustainable
Development Goal (SDG) relevance in textual excerpts not used during model development (see Section
3.2.2). The OSDG dataset consists of over 43 000 SDG-labelled excerpts, curated through crowdsourcing
and expert validation. For evaluation, a high-confidence subset of 8 768 excerpts was selected, comprising
only entries with full agreement (100%) among five or more volunteers and no disagreement (zero
rejections).
The OECD SDG classifier was applied to this dataset to predict the most relevant SDG for each excerpt.
Its performance is summarised in Figure B.1, which presents three heatmaps measuring:
• Precision (column-wise normalisation) , indicating the proportion of correct predictions for each
SDG among all items predicted to belong to that SDG.
• Recall (row-wise normalisation), indicating the proportion of correctly identified instances for each
SDG among all true occurrences of that SDG.
• F1 score (harmonic mean of precision and recall), providing a balanced performance measure.
Overall, the classifier achieves a micro-averaged F1 score of 0.54 and a macro-averaged F1 score of 0.47,
marginally outperforming existing approaches (Kashnitsky et al., 2024[8]), underlining the added value of
research relevance-based data, as collected through the ISSA2021 self-assessment survey.
As shown in Figure B.1 (panel b), the classifier demonstrates strong recall for several SDGs, including
SDG 3 (Good health and well -being), SDG 4 (Quality education), SDG 9 (Industry, innovation and
infrastructure), and SDG 15 (Life on land) , each exceeding or approaching 90%. These results indicate
the model’s strength in detecting these goals, likely due to distinctive language patterns and abundant,
high-quality training data. SDGs 2, 6, 7, and 16 also sho w solid recall performance. In contrast, some
SDGs (e.g., SDG 1, 5, 8, and 12) exhibit lower recall, possibly due to overlapping thematic content or less
clearly defined text. Precision patterns Figure B.1 (panel a), differ slightly, with SDGs 6, 9, 10, 13, 14, 15,
and 16 showing particularly high scores. For example, SDG 6 achieves a precision of 90%, meaning the
classifier is highly accurate when it predicts this SDG. These results suggest that when the classifier
assigns certain SDGs, it does so with hig h confidence and low false -positive rates. The F1 score, shown
in Figure B.1 (panel c) , provides a holistic view of classifier performance. The strongest alignment is
observed along the diagonal, indicating correct label matches. SDGs with high F1 scores reflect both good
recognition (recall) and precision, while lower F1 scores often reveal an imbalance , either the classifier
frequently misses the correct label or mislabels content as belonging to that SDG. SDG 0 (No SDG
relevance) also appears as a classi fier prediction within the OSDG dataset. From an evaluation
perspective, the classifier very rarely assigns this label, reflecting a low tendency to predict irrelevance.
This behaviour is consistent with the high-quality nature of the benchmark dataset, which was constructed
to include only excerpts with strong agreement among raters.
Performance differences across SDGs can be attributed to a mix of factors, including the clarity and
distinctiveness of goal-related terminology, training data volume, and the presence of interdisciplinary or
ambiguous content.
