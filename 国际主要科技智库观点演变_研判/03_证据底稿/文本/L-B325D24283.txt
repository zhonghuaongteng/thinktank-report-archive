About This Working Paper
Recent research suggests that the composition of public research and development (R&D)
investment such as for basic versus applied research or for plant R&D—not just the overall
amount spent on R&D--matters for productivity outcomes. We present new, corroborative
evidence using a local projections method to estimate dynamic responses of macroeconomic
productivity outcomes to unanticipated changes in R&D composition. We find that if R&D
spending shifted toward basic research (i.e., research to expand the knowledge base without
specific applications) are associated with the largest cumulative productivity gains over a ten-
year horizon, operating primarily through total factor productivity. By contrast, spending shifts
toward development activities (i.e., using research to develop or improve products and systems)
are associated with cumulative productivity declines of comparable magnitude. This suggests
that the current development share exceeds its productivity-maximizing level. We then use an
illustrative portfolio analysis to benchmark the historical federal R&D portfolio against
alternative allocations, finding that Pareto-optimal portfolios place roughly three times the
historical share on basic research and substantially lower shares on development. These results
have practical significance for science and technology policy, particularly in the era of increasing
budget tradeoffs against R&D spending. This report is written primarily for economists working
on innovation, productivity, and science policy. The report may also interest policy analysts
engaged with federal R&D spending, though the analysis presumes familiarity with applied
macroeconometric methods.

Summary
• Different categories of federal R&D show different associations with U.S. productivity
growth.
- A one-standard-deviation increase in the share of basic research is associated with
cumulative labor productivity gains of about 2.1 percentage points over ten years.
- A comparable increase in the development R&D share is associated with cumulative
declines of about 2.2 percentage points.
- Applied research and R&D plant investments are associated with positive but less
precisely estimated effects of 1.7 and 1.4 percentage points.
• The productivity effects of basic research operate primarily through total factor
productivity (roughly three-quarters of the cumulative effect). By contrast, for R&D
plant, capital deepening is the only positive channel that is statistically significant. The
mechanisms for applied research effects are split between the two.
• Holding fixed total research and development spending, our illustrative portfolio
optimization framework, which treats R&D categories as productivity investments,
results in Pareto-optimal compositions that allocate roughly three times the historical
share to basic research (47 percent vs. 17 percent) and reduce by a factor of three the
historical share on development research (18 percent vs 59 percent). We note that this
framework solely optimizes for productivity, thus not accounting for other potential
research objectives (e.g., military competitiveness).

The Composition of Federal R&D Spending and U.S. Productivity
Growth
The United States federal government has invested in research and development for decades,
with annual expenditures recently estimated to be roughly $200 billion (NCES, Survey of
Federal Funds for Research and Development, July 2025).1 This investment spans a diverse
portfolio of activities, from fundamental scientific research with no immediate commercial
application to the development of specific technologies for defense, health, and other missions. A
substantial literature documents that aggregate R&D investment generates positive returns to
productivity growth (Gullo, et. al. 2025). Yet relatively little attention has been paid to whether
the composition of this investment matters for productivity outcomes, a question of considerable
policy relevance.
This compositional question has practical significance for science and technology policy,
particularly in an era of constrained federal spending on R&D. If different types of federal R&D
are associated with substantially different productivity effects, then reallocating resources toward
higher-productivity federal R&D types could achieve more productivity growth from the same
total spending. Because productivity growth is one of several legitimate objectives of federal
R&D spending, we do not claim that this reallocation is strictly optimal. Our analysis focuses on
productivity outcomes and does not address how the composition might be evaluated against
other objectives such as national security, public health, human capital development, or basic
scientific capacity.
This work considers four types of R&D spending: basic research, applied research,
development, and research infrastructure (“plant”) investment. Distinctions among these types
reflect genuine differences in the nature of scientific activity, its time horizons, and its channels
of influence on economic outcomes.
• Basic research aims to expand fundamental knowledge without specific applications in
mind, potentially generating broad spillovers across industries and over extended time
periods. Examples include: decoding brain activity related to speech

Second, we find that these compositional associations substantially exceed the associations

Third, we develop a portfolio optimization framework that treats different R&D types as

historical composition of federal R&D has been substantially over-invested in development and

framework, which seek to maximize long-run productivity gains, nearly triple the investment in
basic research and cut investment in development by roughly 40 percentage points.
While such calculations require strong assumptions about the stability of estimated effects
and the feasibility of reallocation, they provide a useful benchmark for evaluating the
productivity implications of compositional choices. Our analysis characterizes associations with
labor productivity in the U.S. private business sector. Thus, implications for reallocating funding
away from development are therefore conditional on labor productivity being the relevant policy
objective.
This analysis combines detailed data on federal R&D budget authority by function with the
productivity accounts maintained by John Fernald at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco
(Fernald, 2014), constructing an annual panel spanning 1956 through 2022. This period
encompasses substantial variation in both the level and composition of federal R&D, driven by
the Cold War buildup, the Space Race, shifting defense priorities, the rise of health research
funding, and periodic changes in science policy emphasis. Figures 1 and 2 in the next section
present the evolution of R&D composition and productivity over this period.
Our empirical strategy employs Local Projections (LP) as introduced by Jordà (2005) to
estimate dynamic responses for macroeconomic productivity outcomes to unanticipated shifts in
R&D composition (referred to throughout this paper as “shocks”). The LP method
accommodates straightforward computation of cumulative effects and permits flexible treatment
of the relationship between short-run and long-run responses. We construct R&D composition
shocks as residuals from forecasting equations that control for the predictable component of each
series, aggregate R&D dynamics, and macroeconomic conditions. These innovations represent

the unpredictable component of compositional change, though we emphasize that
unpredictability does not guarantee exogeneity with respect to productivity outcomes.12
We subject our findings to extensive robustness analysis. Diagnostic tests confirm that our
constructed shocks exhibit the statistical properties expected of unpredictable shocks, passing
standard whiteness tests and show minimal correlation with lagged predictors. Estimates remain
qualitatively stable across alternative lag specifications, subsample periods, and inference
methods. Lead tests designed to detect pre-existing trends yield mixed evidence, with most
specifications showing insignificant lead coefficients but some marginal evidence of anticipation
at longer horizons for certain shocks. We interpret this pattern as consistent with either gradual
policy implementation or the presence of confounding factors that our approach cannot fully
address. We evaluate the robustness of the precision of our estimates using a placebo test
designed to shift the timing of R&D share innovations, to which our results remain strongly
robust.
Our empirical strategy constructs shocks to R&D spending and funding composition as a
forecast residual. Below, the robustness analysis narrows the set of stories under which our
estimates could be biased. The whiteness diagnostic indicates that the shocks are not predictable
from the information set used in the forecasting model, so any confounder operating through that
information set is already absorbed into the forecast. Lead tests indicate that future R&D shocks
generally do not predict current productivity outcomes, which speaks against confounders
operating through pre-existing trends or reverse causality. Placebo timing tests confirm that the
measured share-shock effects align with the actual timing of the constructed shocks. Together,
these checks raise the bar for what an unobserved confounder would need to look like to drive
our results.
The remainder of the report reviews related literature, describes the data and methods,
presents the results and robustness analysis, and discusses the portfolio application and policy
implications.

Overall, the literature establishes that who funds R&D matters for productivity, but it is
largely silent on how productivity outcomes vary with the composition of federal R&D across
activity types — basic research, applied research, development, and R&D plant. Quantifying that
composition effect is the contribution of this paper.
Detailed Discussion of Existing Literature
A large literature documents that R&D investment generates positive returns to productivity
growth, with social returns typically estimated to exceed private returns by a factor of two or
more (Griliches 1979; Jones and Williams 1998; Bloom, Schankerman, and Van Reenen, 2013;
Hall, Mairesse, and Mohnen, 2010; Fieldhouse & Mertens 2024). Within this literature, which
focuses on the US, a substantial strand examines public R&D specifically, generally finding that
government-funded research complements rather than crowds out private efforts (Moretti,
Steinwender, and Van Reenen, 2019; Guellec and Van Pottelsberghe de la Potterie, 2004;
Azoulay et. al. 2019). Examples of mechanisms for “crowding-in” effect include different
research incentives for publicly funded academia and the private sector (Aghion et. al. 2008), as
well as the increase in venture funding for entrepreneurs that win early-stage R&D grants for
small businesses (Howell 2017). In aggregate, public spending on R&D appears to lead to
significant productivity impact through positive spillovers to innovation and economic
opportunity.
Compositional studies tend to find that public and nondefense R&D spending have the
greatest productivity spillovers, relative to private and defense R&D spending. Recent empirical
work find that public R&D spending generates two to three times larger productivity spillovers,
relative to private R&D spending (Dyevre 2024). The compositional shift from public to private
space R&D may explain a recent finding of reduced space R&D spillovers over time (Carrado et.

Theoretical work has emphasized that basic research may be particularly productive because
it expands the frontier of applicable knowledge with broad spillovers that compound over time

14 At the same time, some in the field have argued that the tradition of distinguishing
between basic and other forms of research is arbitrary, with a “lack of a substantive basis”

development (Mansfield, 1980). We accept that this distinction remains meaningful both in terms

Existing insights demonstrate the link between R&D spending and productivity and establish
that not all R&D spending contributes equally. Yet these insights tend to focus on the deployer
of research funds (either defense or nondefense, public or private sector), rather than the types of
research activity being funded.
Our contribution is to estimate how the composition of federal R&D portfolio affects
disaggregated components of U.S. productivity growth: total factor productivity, capital
deepening, and labor productivity. Disaggregation of both R&D spending types and
macroeconomic productivity allows us to characterize which categories of federal R&D are
associated with gains in total factor productivity, capital deepening, or labor productivity, and
with what magnitude and timing. This information matters for policy because the federal budget
process involves explicit allocation decisions across these categories. If their productivity
associations differ substantially, the composition of the portfolio is a policy lever in its own
right. In this approach, we are contributing to a broader conversation on how policymakers can
adjust their modeling approaches for the budgetary impacts of R&D spending. Currently, policy
analysts do not integrate the existing research on R&D productivity spillovers, instead treating
R&D investments as similar to infrastructure investments (Gullo, et. al. 2025).

This section describes the sources and construction of our data, presents descriptive statistics,

Productivity Data
Our productivity measures come from the dataset maintained by John Fernald at the Federal
Reserve Bank of San Francisco, which provides quarterly estimates of total factor productivity,
labor productivity, and the components of labor productivity growth (Fernald, 2014). Fernald’s
methodology follows the growth accounting tradition pioneered by Solow (1957) and refined by
Jorgenson, Gollop, and Fraumeni (1987), decomposing output growth into contributions from
capital services, labor hours, labor quality, and a residual attributed to total factor productivity.
The growth accounting identity expresses labor productivity growth as the sum of three
components.
15 Total factor productivity captures improvements in the efficiency with which
inputs are combined to produce output, representing technological change and other factors that
raise output without requiring additional inputs. Capital deepening reflects increases in the
capital-labor ratio, weighted by capital’s share of income. Labor quality captures improvements
in the composition of the workforce, including the effects of rising educational attainment and
experience accumulation.
We use annual averages constructed from the quarterly data, covering the period 1956
through 2022. For our main specifications, we use the utilization-adjusted TFP measure, which
attempts to purge measured productivity of cyclical variation in the intensity with which capital
and labor are employed. This adjustment is important in our setting because federal R&D

Federal R&D Data
Data on federal R&D spending by function come from historical tables published by the
National Science Foundation and the Office of Management and Budget. These tables report
budget authority for research and development activities, classified into four categories.16
• Basic research encompasses “systematic study directed toward greater knowledge or
understanding of the fundamental aspects of phenomena,” without specific applications
in view (Moris and Pece, 2022).
• Applied research involves “systematic study to understand the means to meet a
recognized and specific need” (Moris and Pece, 2022).
• Development represents “systematic use of the knowledge and understanding gained
from research directed toward the production of useful materials, devices, systems, or
methods” (Moris and Pece, 2022).
• R&D plant includes construction of facilities and acquisition of equipment specifically
for R&D activities (Moris and Pece, 2022).
We construct shares of each category in total federal R&D, which sum to one by definition.
We also examine log levels of each category’s spending, which allows us to study the effects of
increasing one type of R&D while controlling for others. We express all spending data in real
terms using the GDP deflator.
Macroeconomic Controls
Our analysis includes two macroeconomic control variables that may affect both R&D
composition decisions and productivity outcomes. The output gap, computed as the percentage
deviation of real GDP from potential GDP, captures cyclical conditions that could influence both
government spending priorities and measured productivity. The change in the federal funds rate
controls for monetary policy stance, which affects capital investment and potentially the
composition of federal spending through budget pressures.

Historical Variation in R&D Composition
The composition of federal R&D has evolved substantially over the study period, driven by
shifting national priorities and the changing structure of the federal research enterprise. During
the late 1950s and 1960s, the Space Race and Cold War competition drove substantial increases
in development spending, particularly for missile systems and space vehicles (Corrado et. al.
2023). The basic research share declined during this period as development activities absorbed
an increasing fraction of the federal R&D budget. The 1970s saw a partial reversal, with the
development share declining as the Apollo program wound down and defense spending
decreased relative to GDP. The basic research share recovered somewhat during this period. The
Reagan defense buildup of the 1980s again increased the development share, though not to the
levels of the 1960s.
More recent decades have seen a gradual increase in the basic research share, driven partly
by the growth of health research funding through the National Institutes of Health (NIH). The
doubling of the NIH budget between 1998 and 2003 represented primarily basic and applied
research rather than development, shifting the overall federal composition toward earlier-stage
activities.

Identifying Assumptions and Limitations
A causal interpretation of our local projection estimates rests on a conditional exogeneity
assumption that the constructed R&D composition shocks are uncorrelated with unobserved
determinants of future productivity, conditional on the information set included in our
forecasting equations and outcome regressions.
We operationalize R&D composition shocks as residuals from forecasting regressions for
each composition series conditioned on prior investments, lagged total R&D spending, and other
macro controls. These controls ought to account for high-level macroeconomic trends, such as
the Federal Funds rate, that might influence federal spending decisions. By construction, these
shocks are thus orthogonal to the included predictors.
The identification claim is that, to the extent this conditioning is a sufficient proxy for the
information policymakers use when adjusting the R&D portfolio in response to unexpected
macroeconomic and productivity-relevant conditions, the remaining residual variation can be
interpreted as an as-if random policy surprise, or “shock” (Ramey 2016).
Several features of our design strengthen this interpretation. First, federal budgeting and
program execution operate with substantial institutional lags.
21 Compositional shifts in realized
budget authority typically reflect plans and negotiations initiated in earlier years and are
implemented over multiple years. This timing reduces the plausibility that our composition
shocks are mechanically responding to contemporaneous realized productivity shocks. Second,
our preferred productivity measure is utilization-adjusted TFP, which mitigates a prominent
source of spurious correlation of cyclical fluctuations in measured productivity that could be
jointly related to fiscal conditions. Third, for share shocks we explicitly orthogonalize
compositional shocks with respect to aggregate R&D movements by including contemporaneous

helping separate compositional shifts from broad expansions or contractions that may be more
tightly linked to macroeconomic conditions.
However, these arguments do not eliminate threats to identification. The most important
limitation is that unpredictability relative to our conditioning set does not guarantee exogeneity.
Composition may still move in response to anticipated future productivity or to policymakers’
private information about future conditions that is not captured by our observed controls. More
broadly, unobserved policy changes or geopolitical and mission-priority shocks (e.g., shifts in
defense posture, regulatory regimes, or procurement strategy) could affect both the federal R&D
mix and productivity through channels outside measured R&D composition, creating potential
confounding. An omitted shock would need to occur at the same realization date as the
constructed R&D shock, be unpredictable from the lagged variables in our forecasting model and
operate on productivity through channels other than the federal R&D portfolio it influences.
For greater clarity on how possible omitted shocks shape the interpretation of our results,
consider two potential examples: geopolitical shifts and R&D tax expenditures. First,
unanticipated geopolitical events can move federal R&D priorities and aggregate productivity in
the same year through channels that operate alongside the R&D portfolio itself. The relevant
failure mode is an event that arrives as a surprise to the forecasting model, prompts a
contemporaneous reallocation of federal R&D, and affects productivity through trade, supply
chains, regulation, capital investment, or business sentiment. A geopolitical shift telegraphed in
advance would typically be absorbed by the lagged predictors and would not necessarily bias our
estimates. Second, our R&D spending measures capture federal outlays and excludes R&D tax
expenditures. The federal R&D tax credit acts as an alternative form of public support for R&D,
and discrete changes to the credit alter the public subsidy environment in ways that our outlays
variable does not capture. Bias from this source arises only when changes to tax expenditures are
correlated with our outlay shocks at the same realization date and have an independent
productivity effect through the private R&D they stimulate. The sign of any such bias follows the
sign of that co-movement, which we cannot determine within our current specifications.
For these reasons, we interpret our findings as informative associations about the effect of public
outlays on R&D driven productivity that warrant further investigation. We probe the plausibility
of the conditional exogeneity assumptions in several ways. First, we document that the shocks
have the statistical properties expected of forecast errors, including minimal serial correlation
and limited predictability from expanded information sets. Second, we conduct lead/anticipation
tests to assess whether future composition shocks predict earlier productivity movements, as
significant lead relationships would be consistent with anticipation, reverse causality, or omitted
variables. Third, we evaluate the robustness of the results to alternative lag lengths,
specifications, and subsamples to assess whether the results hinge on narrow episodes or

===== PDF_PAGE 24 =====
17
modeling choices. These diagnostics, discussed below, cannot prove exogeneity, but they help
bound the most direct failures of the identifying assumptions.
Overall, our preferred interpretation of our results is that under a sufficient information set
assumption (i.e., that our forecasting controls approximate the information set that systematically
links R&D portfolio choices to expected productivity-relevant conditions) our shocks can be
interpreted as unexpected reallocations in the federal R&D mix that are plausibly exogenous
with respect to subsequent productivity shocks. Where this assumption fails, our estimates
should be read as reduced-form dynamic associations rather than definitive causal effects.

Basic Research Shows the Largest Productivity Gains; Development Is
Associated with Declines
This section presents our main empirical findings. While overall research spending is not
significantly associated with productivity due to large standard errors, we do find significant
compositional effects. In particular, basic research is associated with the largest gains in total
factor productivity and labor productivity, while development is associated with declines. These
relationships directionally hold for log level changes to individual categories. Simulating
bilateral composition shifts from one category to another, we find that expected productivity
increases when funding is reallocated from development to any other research type, with the
greatest point estimates when reallocated toward basic research. In this section, we describe our
results in more detail.

Compositional variation, by contrast, may provide cleaner identification to the extent that the

conditional on prior allocations and broad macroeconomic trends. We turn now to share shocks,

R&D budget across categories, however, may reflect a more specific set of decisions involving
agency-level program choices, congressional earmarks and appropriations subcommittee
preferences, the maturation of particular research agendas, and shifts in mission emphasis within
agencies. Based on this understanding, these allocation decisions would be less obviously
correlated with aggregate macroeconomic conditions that independently drive productivity.

Stability Check Across Structural Break: Bayh-Dole Act of 1980
To examine whether our estimated associations are stable over time, we re-estimate the
models allowing for the shock coefficient to vary before and after 1980. We focus on 1980 as the
break year due to the Bayh-Dole Act of 1980, which fundamentally restructured incentives for
U.S. R&D. Prior to the Act, government agencies retained patent rights to federally funded
research. The Bayh-Dole Act allowed universities and publicly funded researchers to patent and
commercialize their inventions, granting private industry access to licensing agreements for
exclusive use. This shift catalyzed academic-industrial collaboration and accelerated technology
transfer (Loise and Stevens 2010; CSIS 2022).
Robustness results are reported in Appendix A. Overall, the estimates appear stable across
this break, with 17 of 20 estimates not differing significantly between periods at the five percent
level. The three exceptions occur at the zero horizon, where standard errors are relatively narrow.
It is possible that this shift reflects a change in the relationship, though estimates appear to
converge over time. Basic research prior to 1980 does appear to contribute more to labor
productivity and innovation by year ten, but the difference is not statistically significant. One
possibility is that this difference reflects the mission-oriented funding of the 1960s and 1970s
and complementarities with the substantial baseline funding level and structural support for
public R&D. Our robustness check is consistent with findings of a general decline in R&D

total factor productivity and labor productivity in the longer-term (Corrado et. al. 2023). At the
same time, large standard errors and time horizons make it difficult to distinguish between
genuine structural change and estimation uncertainty.
A Portfolio Framework Suggests Public Investment in Development at the
Cost of Productivity
Building on the estimated productivity associations documented in the preceding sections, we
now develop a portfolio optimization framework to evaluate alternative allocations of federal
R&D. We emphasize that this exercise requires strong assumptions about the stability and causal
interpretation of our estimates, and we present it primarily as an illustrative tool for thinking
about compositional tradeoffs rather than a definitive policy recommendation. The exercise also
requires a specific focus on federal R&D as a productivity-enhancing investment, which ignores
potential other policy-relevant objectives of R&D spending.

We draw 10,000 random portfolios uniformly from the four-dimensional simplex and

portfolio, we conduct 1,000 Monte Carlo iterations. In every iteration, we redraw the asset-level

outcome weights from a Dirichlet distribution centered on the baseline proportions. The resulting

Caveats
There are several important limitations to this application of portfolio analysis. First, the
optimal portfolios depend on the point estimate coefficients on productivity effects, though we
consider uncertainty in the estimates using bootstrapped standard errors. To the extent that the
true productivity effects differ from our estimates, optimal allocations would shift. Second, the
analysis focuses on marginal effects of shifting the marginal dollar from one research type into
another, assuming that effects are stable across different allocation regimes. General equilibrium
considerations suggest that large reallocations could change the returns to each category, or that
the true effects could be nonlinear. Third, federal R&D composition reflects objectives beyond
productivity maximization, including national security, public health, and scientific capability.
Development spending in particular is heavily concentrated in defense and other mission-
oriented programs whose social value extends beyond measured labor productivity. Reallocating
funding away from development involves tradeoffs that our productivity-focused analysis does
not characterize. Compositional policy must account for both the productivity consequences we
estimate and the consequences for other policy objectives.

We present the portfolio analysis primarily as a tool for illustrating the implications of
heterogeneous productivity effects on the opportunity cost of high R&D funding allocation to
development. The estimated optimal portfolio is not intended as a policy prescription, but as a
directional indicator. The analysis clarifies that if our estimates are approximately correct, and
assuming stable linear marginal effects, the productivity consequences of compositional choices
are substantial enough to warrant serious attention in science and technology policy
deliberations.
Our Results Support a Rebalancing of U.S. R&D Budget Priorities
Our findings support recent calls for a change to budget scoring of R&D spending, in order
to better capture the different expected returns across R&D types. At the policy level, a
productivity-focused Federal R&D portfolio would rely less on development spending and more
on basic research; this finding ought to be considered as a tradeoff to the current development-
heavy R&D budget. In this section, we elaborate on this interpretation and mechanisms.
Federal R&D Investment in Basic Research is Low, Relative to Expected Productivity
Gains
Our finding of a positive association between basic research and productivity is consistent
with both prior literature and theoretical expectations, including the dominant mechanism of total
factor productivity. Basic research generates fundamental knowledge that can be applied across
many contexts, creating broad spillovers that raise productivity throughout the economy (Nelson
1959; Dyevre 2024). Further, funding for basic research has been found to contribute to and
maintain high-caliber human capital in the United States (Tham et. al. 2024). Long lags typically
associated with basic research effects are consistent with our finding that basic research works
primarily through TFP rather than capital accumulation, as knowledge diffusion takes time. Yet
our analysis supports the view that gains at least begin to materialize within the budget window
of ten years, rather than taking 20 or more years to materialize as assumed in current budget
scoring approaches (Gullo et. al. 2025). The cumulative ten-year effect of approximately 2.1
percentage points per standard-deviation shock is on the higher end of prior estimates and
implies substantial returns to basic research investment. This estimate, combined with our
portfolio analysis, suggests significant underinvestment in basic research relative to a
productivity-maximizing benchmark.
The implication of this finding for policymakers is twofold. One, standard budget scoring
approaches may underweight the productivity value of basic research investment, contributing to
a systematic underinvestment in this category of R&D spending relative to other categories.
Updating budget scoring to reflect longer-term futures, as well as greater returns within the
budget window, could help policymakers more accurately weigh the estimated benefits against
the costs of basic research. Two, federal spending on basic research is a tested and effective

===== PDF_PAGE 36 =====
29
channel to increase economic productivity through total factor productivity. As the United States
faces high and increasing budget deficits, taking an investment approach to the Federal budget
would suggest a particularly high return to basic research spending, as one potential path to
reducing the future debt-to-GDP ratio.
Development Drains U.S. Economic Productivity, Relative to Other Research Types
We also find a negative association between development spending and productivity.
Development represents the largest category of federal R&D historically; this finding suggests
that shifting marginal dollars away from development would strictly improve projected future
productivity outcomes. This finding suggests a misallocation of R&D spending against the
objective of economic productivity.
Several mechanisms could generate this pattern. First, federal development spending is
heavily concentrated in defense and space missions, and the productivity returns to this type of
work may flow only weakly to the economic productivity measures captured in this analysis. Our
productivity data covers the U.S. private business sector and uses cost-based deflators for federal
output. Productivity gains realized inside government operations or in defense contractor
production of military goods are therefore likely understated relative to the gains realized in
private commercial production. Second, the technologies developed for specific military
applications or space vehicles may have few uses outside those narrow domains and heightened
information security that limits dual-use applications.
Third, to the extent that federal development competes for scientific talent and resources with
more productivity-enhancing activities, it could reduce aggregate productivity. And fourth,
development spending may substitute for adoption of existing technologies, which would raise
measured productivity in the short run but would not be captured in our R&D measures. If
development crowds out technology adoption, measured productivity could decline even as the
underlying technological capability improves. Each of these mechanisms is consistent with our
findings, though they imply different normative interpretations.
The primary implication for policymakers, thus, is to explicitly consider the objectives of
R&D policy and whether existing development investments are aligned with public priorities.
The comparative advantage of Federal R&D dollars, when it comes to productivity, appears to be
in basic research. At the same time, there may be investments – such as military technologies—
that the private sector otherwise would not produce without Federal investments. More analysis
of effective public production systems and conditions for contractor productivity may help close
the gap between noneconomic objectives and macroeconomic outcomes; otherwise, tradeoffs
could be considered more explicitly in budgeting priorities.
Limitations and Future Directions: Statistical Power, Causal Inference, and Spillovers
Several limitations of our analysis suggest directions for future research. Due to aggregation,
our analysis relies on a small number of data points, limiting statistical power and estimation

===== PDF_PAGE 37 =====
30
precision. Further, our use of aggregate federal R&D data masks substantial heterogeneity across
agencies, programs, and scientific fields. Other work (e.g., Fieldhouse & Mertens 2024, Moretti
et. al. 2025) has suggested that productivity effects differ between defense R&D and nondefense
R&D, and our aggregate estimates are not designed to capture this heterogeneity. Future work
with more disaggregated data could examine whether the compositional patterns we document
hold within specific domains or reflect differences across domains, by, for example,
decomposing basic research into different subcomponents or categories.
Our time-series identification relies on variation that may be confounded by unobserved
factors correlated with both R&D composition and productivity. Alternative identification
strategies, such as instrumental variables based on political or institutional factors that affect
R&D composition independently of productivity considerations, could provide complementary
evidence on causal effects.
The international dimension is also absent from our analysis, though there is some evidence
of cross-border spillovers from public R&D investment (e.g., as documented by Moretti et. al.
2025 in the defense sector). Other countries have made different compositional choices in their
public R&D investments, and cross-country comparison could provide additional identifying
variation. However, institutional differences across countries complicate direct comparison and
would require careful attention to context.
Conclusion: Opportunity for Compositional Shifts in U.S. R&D Spending
This paper has examined the relationship between the composition of federal R&D spending
and U.S. productivity growth using data spanning 67 years. Our analysis finds substantial
heterogeneity in the estimated productivity associations across R&D types. Shocks that increase
the share of basic research in the federal portfolio are associated with cumulative productivity
gains of approximately 2.1 percentage points over ten years. Applied research and R&D plant
investments also show positive associations of 1.7 and 1.4 percentage points, respectively. In
contrast, positive shocks to the development share are associated with productivity declines of
approximately 2.2 percentage points.
These compositional effects substantially exceed the aggregate R&D effects we can detect,
suggesting that how federal R&D is allocated may matter more for productivity than the overall
level of investment. Portfolio optimization analysis indicates that historical allocations have been
tilted toward development relative to compositions that would maximize expected productivity,
implying potential gains from reallocation toward basic research and research infrastructure.
Our identification strategy cannot definitively establish causality. The constructed shocks
represent unpredictable changes in R&D composition, but unpredictability does not guarantee
exogeneity with respect to productivity outcomes. Diagnostic tests support the interpretation of
our constructed shocks as valid, but there remains the possibility of unobserved confounding

===== PDF_PAGE 38 =====
31
factors. We interpret our results as informative associations that motivate further investigation
rather than definitive causal estimates.
Subject to these limitations, our findings argue that science policy deliberations should attend
to the composition of federal R&D investments alongside the overall level of R&D spending.
The productivity consequences of compositional choices are large enough to warrant weight
alongside national security, human capital development, public health, and other objectives of
federal R&D. We caution that our estimates reflect responses to shocks of standard-deviation
magnitude; large reallocations could generate different returns through general equilibrium
effects that our linear framework does not capture. Further research using alternative
identification strategies and more disaggregated data will strengthen the evidentiary foundation
for compositional policy recommendations.

Lead Tests
A standard concern in time-series designs that exploit policy changes is that the measured
shock may still proxy for slow-moving and forward-looking policy adjustments or other latent
forces that also predict future productivity. To evaluate this potential concern, we implement a
lead test that estimates whether future values of the constructed R&D composition shocks have
incremental predictive power for labor productivity, conditional on the current shock and
standard controls. For each composition shock, we estimate regressions that include three-year

Placebo Tests
Are the estimated productivity responses in Table 3 tied to the historical timing of R&D
share reallocations, or would similarly large effects appear even if the same shock sequence
occurred in different years? To evaluate this, we implement a circular-shift placebo test. For each
R&D share shock, we preserve the entire realized shock sequence (including serial correlation
and low frequency structure) but rotate it by a random offset in calendar time and re-estimate the
same LP specification. This test generates a demanding placebo null that the shock retains
realistic time series properties, but its timing is no longer aligned with the historical sequency of
productivity movements and policy episodes. Small placebo p-values indicate that our baseline
cumulative effects are unusually large relative to estimates obtained under alternative calendar
