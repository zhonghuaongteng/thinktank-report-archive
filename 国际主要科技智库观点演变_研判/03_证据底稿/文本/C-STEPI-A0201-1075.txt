2022 K-Innovation Partnership Program with Indonesia

Since 2014, the Science and Technology Policy Institute (STEPI) has maintained long-term
cooperation with Indonesia by providing policy consultation related to science and
technology and capacity -building programs for the science and technology institutes
(Agency for the Assessment and Application of Technology (BPPT) and the Indonesian
Institute of Sciences (LIPI)) in Indonesia.
Recently, STI governance issues have become more critical in Indonesia with the
establishment of its National Research and Innovation Agency (BIRN) in 2021. BRIN
requested from STPEI a policy consultation on effective R&D innovation governance, and
STEPI duly gave an STI policy consultation based on a three-year framework (2020-2022).
The arrangement of STI Governance (Coordination Mechanism, Institutional Framework,
Financial and Investment System) in Indonesia for the period 2020-2022 comprised various
contents. In 2020, STEPI analyzed Indonesia’s STI governance with regard to its coordination
mechanisms, investment systems, and R&D program evaluations. In 2021, STEPI conducted
various analyses of Indonesia’s coordination mechanisms, policy think tanks, and the law's
regulatory framework (No. 2019-11). The research topics for 2021 included (i) the
adjustment mechanism (or coordination mechanism) for the establishment of mission -
based research institutes, (ii) the national evaluation system for R&D programs, and (iii) the
contents of law no. 209-11 in comparison with Korea’s S&T framework Act. In 2022, STEPI
conducted research focused on (i) BRIN's role in green growth policy, (ii) development of STI
human capital in Indonesia, and (iii) improv ement of BRIN’s management regime for
mission-oriented research and innovation.
In 2022, the research was conducted through joint research and performance dissemination
workshops with Indonesian partners. Researchers from both Korea and Indonesia (BRIN)

The 2022 project achieved the following outcomes:
First, by benchmarking Korea's green growth success cases, the research team presents
directions and strategies for BRIN's role, mission, and operational systems in implementing
Indonesia's green growth policy.
Second, the research team provides experience and implications for Korea's S&T human

Indonesia's STI human capital. It also proposes a promotion strategy for developing
Indonesia's human capital and sets directions for improving the relevant laws and systems.
Third, the research team suggests ways to operate BRIN more effectively, and proposes a
new operation and governance system for BRIN to facilitate cooperation between the three
deputies (Research & Innovation Facilitation, Research & Innovation Infrastructure, and
Research & Innovation Uilization) and cooperation with external industries and innovation
actors.

1. Project Background
Since 2014, the Science and Technology Policy Institute (STEPI) has been providing policy
consultation and a capacity-building program for the experts of Indonesia’s Institute of
Science (LIPI) and its Agency for the Assessment and Application of Technology (BPPT) as
part of a long-term partnership. In particular, it has engaged in practical collaboration with
LIPI under the K-Innovation Partnership Program since 2018. Accordingly, the 2020-2022 K-
Innovation Partnership Program with Indonesia is also a cooperative projected initiated at
LIPI's request. Over the past two years, the STEPI and LIPI project teams have conducted
joint research and policy consultation on reforms of Indonesia's STI governance, its plan for
the establishment of a think tank, and its national R&D program management and
evaluation system.
Recently, there have been significant changes in science and technology governance in
Indonesia. For instance, o n September 1, 2021 various national R&D institutions were
integrated into the National Research and Innovation Agency (BRIN), a presidential agency
of Indonesia, while LIPI was merged into BRIN.
In response to these changes and the altered demands of the partner, in 2022 the overall
theme of the project was switched from 'Financial System, Evaluation, and Investment
Strategy for STI Governance in Indonesia' to 'Effective Implementation of the National STI
Policy System with BRIN' under an agreement between the two institutions.

3. Project Framework
In essence, the 2022 project consists of two axes: i) international joint research and ii)
capacity-building activities. The purpose of this project is not to provide one -way
consulting from Korean experts , but rather to enable local policy researchers to
diagnose themselves and devise improvement strategies together by directly
participating in the research. In addition, the training workshops are designed to
promote capacity building through knowledge sharing and case studies from Korea ’s
experiences. Strengthening the research capabilities of individual researchers will
eventually act as an engine for exploring the direction of national development.
First, the int ernational joint research team was formed with researchers from BRIN in
Indonesia and Korea. As regards the detailed subjects presented for policy consultation
in 2022, the Indonesian research team studied Indonesia's current circumstances and
situation, and identified limitations and challenges based on an analysis of their findings.
At the same time, the Korean research team diagnose d the situation in Indonesian and
researched Korean cases. (Ultimately, policy recommendations were proposed based on
the results of the analysis of the current situation and Korean case stud ies. As for the
training workshop, it was designed to provide more practical methodologies on policy
research by providing diverse cases. Indonesian researchers strengthen ed their policy
research capabilities, which influenced the derivation of the final strategies in the
international joint research.
Finally, an outcome dissemination forum was held to share the results of the
international joint research and policy advice. In particular, the policy suggestions
presented on the basis of comparisons with and analyses of the related systems and
policies in Korea were promoted to local partner organizations and various stakeholders
in the STI field. In the process, we expected not only to spread the results of this project
but also requests for additional cooperation.
Therefore, the inputs and outputs of diverse activities and results of the project were
systematically and convergently connected.

 International Joint Research
International joint research was conducted to derive final policy advice. Studies on the
current status of STI in Indonesia and Korean case studies were conducted with respect to
each of the three sub-topics, and three reports were produced. Based on these results, the
following policy advice was implemented:
• After r eflecting the current status of Indonesia’s system a nd South Korea’s
experience, the strategy to promote Indonesia's green growth policy effectively was
proposed.
• In Indonesia's STI human capital development, Korea's science and technology
human resource development cases were introduced, and specific human resource
development plans were presented based on the current situation.
• The policy implications and agenda s were suggested for improving the operating
system of BRIN according to the case analysis of public research institutes in Korea,
especially the National Research Council for Science and Technology and
government-funded research institutes.

1. Introduction

 Background
The so-called “Green Economy” (GE) is a global and regional response to finding solutions
to the economic, ecological, and social problems that is not separate from the Paris
Agreement. The term “green economy” originated from developed countries as a policy
trend to create a more efficient economic system and develop more environmentally-
friendly technologies, and to save resources while reducing carbon emissions. Based on this,
the GE concept has recently received worldwide attention as a solution to various global
changes. These terms and approaches emerged in the field of economics relatively recently,
i.e. about four decades ago (Ivlev & Ivleva, 2018).
GE leads to sustainable development, which is supported by technology advancement as an
end goal in many countries (Ryszawska, 2017; Sulich, 2021). GE is a form of economic
development aimed at achieving two goals: to reduce dangers and ecological scarcity and
to enhance global well-being and social fairness (UNEP, 2014). All economy activities,
ranging from the production and distribution of goods to their consumption, are pushed to
human prosperity with minimal or no detrimental effect on the environment (Ivlev & Ivleva,
2018).
GE has been widely received in many countries around the world as a response to a global
agreement, and translates to national green policies in many fields. In essence, GE implies a
departure from the conventional 'business as usual' economic paradigm to that of 'business
as usual' economics combined with strong regulatory measures and financial incentives
aimed at promoting innovation, investment (e.g. in green technology), sustainable

===== PDF_PAGE 34 =====
2022 K-Innovation Partnership Program with Indonesia

22
consumption patterns and behaviors, and information sharing (UNEP, 2011). While green
growth (GG) is a policy that aims to provide contingent practical prescriptions (Hamdouch
& Depret, 2010) , GE is a form of governmental intervention that attempts to achieve
sustainable goals through GE development, and also aims to realize human well -being
through green development (Samson & Capozza, 2019). Practically speaking, GE and GG are
similar concepts that are used to define environmentally-friendly and sustainable economy
policies. For the purposes of this study, we often use the two terms (GE and GG )
interchangeably or sometimes combine them; hence, we refer to them as “green economy
growth” (GEG).
GEG is aligned with science and technology innovation (STI) to promote sustainable socio-
economic development (Ryszawska, 2017, Sulich, 2021). It focuses on the quality of
economic growth and realizes economic prosperity by creating better social impacts and
reducing pressure on the environment and natural capital in a given region. In practice, GEG
is close to the concept of STI and sustainable development goals (SDGs). As such,
governmental intervention (STI policy) is required to promote GEG to achieve SDGs (See
Bakkera & Konings, 2017; Stucki et al., 2018; Aguirre-Bastosa, & Weber, 2018). Therefore,
GEG, STI Policy, and SDGs are inter-dependent governmental policies that should be carried
out in both developed and developing countries.
The most challenging issue for many countries, especially for developing countries in the
current decade, is not the need to foster economic development but rather the need to
maintain sustainable environmental development. Though industrial -based econom ic
growth and natural resource-based innovation are the main issues facing such countries
(Aguirre-Bastosa, & Weber, 2018), they must face GEG issues in in order to respond to global
policies to save the earth (OECD, 2012). As a developing country, Indonesia faces major
challenges to promoting and accelerating green-oriented economy growth. In addition,
Indonesia needs to adopt GEG development at the same time. This means that Indonesia
needs to adopt a pilot policy design from an advanced country that has succe eded in
developing its industrial economy and green technology policy (GTP) concurrently.
As a part of the newly industrializing economies (NIEs), the government of South Korea has
developed a comprehensive green growth strategy. The country has become one of the

 Scope of Research
The scope of this research includes the STI policy think tank’s contribution to supporting
GEG development at the national level. In this regard, the role of the STI policy think tank,
both in the GTC and BRIN, forms the main part of the discussion of this study. Ryszawska

developing green industries. Thus, the GTC’s practices in South Korea were examined and

In general terms, the STI think tank is an element of the overall public policy think tank. Its
plays the role of a channel of knowledge from academicians to policy makers and society,
incubating ideas concretely before delivering them to stakeholders (Mac Kenzie et al., 2015;

facilitate the establishment of hard and soft infrastructures in order to improve the
innovation system, and to monitor the system, and ii) to organize and maintain multiple
networks widely (funders, experts/academician, society, other partners) (Seo et al., 2021).

this analysis.

2. Green Economy Growth and Policy Think Tanks

 Green Economy Growth (GEG)
The Green Economy (GE) create s more economical systems and greener technologies,
thereby reducing GHG emissions through better resource management. It has attracted
worldwide attention due to changes in the global environment. During the late 1960s and
early 1970s, certain environmental problems became prevalent in developed countries
(Ivlev & Ivleva, 2018). The original idea of GE was proposed by David Pearce and his
colleagues, who argued that the pricing system was the main cause of environmental
variability in the allocation of natural resources. Social and environmental factors are rarely
considered in pricing goods and services. In addition, interest in GE is driven by global and
regional efforts to address economic, environmental, and social issues (Ryszawska, 2017;
Sulich, 2021).
As such, the green economy should aim to minimize environmental damage and ecological
scarcity while improving human well-being and social justice (UNEP, 2014). Therefore, the
main goals of GE are to generate low-carbon, efficient and socially inclusive resources and
to promote the transition to sustainability. Advocates of the GE believe its current business
model is inefficient. GE is committed to improving the material well-being of a small portion
of the world's population at the expense of the environm ent, as well as to mitigating
widespread poverty, the effects of climate change, loss of biodiversity, and other serious
problems. It helps the process of production, distribution, and consumption in the long run
to promote human well-being without destroying the environment (Ivlev & Ivleva, 2018).
GE is not a singular term for green industry development. Green Growth (GG) is a general
term used to promote economic growth and development. GG is defined as strong growth
that takes into consideration the role of natural capital in natural disasters, environmental
management, and the prevention of natural disasters, and which also minimizes pollution
and other negative environmental impacts (World Bank, 2021).

 The Role of the Think Tank in the Policy Process
Although the origin of the term "think tank" is unknown, most reports (Rich, 2004) indicate
that it was coined during World War II to refer to military research and development
organizations. Think tanks are defined as "... organizations engaged in regular research and
advocacy on any matter related to public policy." However, the use of think tanks, including
in A sian countries, may reduce the importance of independent research. In modern
democracies, they serve as a link between knowledge and power" (UNDP, 2003). Similarly,
the Cambridge Dictionary (2022) defines a think tank as a group of experts brought together,
usually by a government, to develop ideas and make recommendations for action on a
specific subject.
The Anglo-American tradition views think tanks as relatively autonomous organizations with
their own legal identity that conduct policy analysis independently of the government,
political parties, and pressure groups. Think tanks are widely assumed to provide unbiased,
well-researched advice and advocacy on complex social issues (Diane Stone, 2005). In
general, think tanks generate accumulated ideas or thoughts that are inevitably
disseminated to the targeted spheres, and more particularly to policy-relevant fields (Shaw,
Russell, Parsons & Greenhalgh, 2015, McGann, 2018).
Think tanks are groups of professionals who research and analyze public policy challenges,

===== PDF_PAGE 41 =====
Chapter 2. Designing the STI Policy Think Tank for Green Growth Policy Formulation:
Lessons Learned from South Korea’s Practices and Experience
29
develop and evaluate potential solutions, including policy alternatives, and inform decision-
makers, their staffs, and the public of the findings of their research, analyses, and
assessments of potential solutions (Cheney, D.W., Heaton Jr., G.R., Hill, C.T. & Windhamet,
P.H., 2016). In other words, think tanks are groups that conduct policy-oriented research,
analysis, and interaction on domestic and global issues, all of which helps politicians and the
general public to make well-informed decisions about public policy.
This study defines ‘think tank ’ as a professional group within an independent and
autonomous organization. Such groups are composed of academicians, experts, and
practitioners whose function is to generate and incubate ideas before delivering them to
policy makers. Those ideas are derived from academic and empirical discussion referring to
research results, scientific discourse, experiences, and history. The goal is to reduce bias
opinion among policy makers and strengthen evidence-based policy.
In the policy process, the role of think tanks is to serve as policy knowledge intermediaries,
research centers, and idea incubators. Think tanks are popularly called “knowledge broker.”.
As knowledge brokers, think tanks connect academics, policy makers, and civil society. They
also transform R&D results (theories, discourse) into policy recommendations , and
demonstrate the power of entrepreneurial solutions in addressing public issues. Most
importantly, think tanks should provide credible, simple, and relevant information to policy
makers (CIPE, 2022).
This study focuses on the role of the think tank in the science-technology-innovation (STI)
policy making process. However, the role of the think tank in STI policy -making is very
limited. Seo et al (2021) discuss the role of the think tank in an open innovation system (OIS).
According to Seo’s study, a think tank can play a role in academic research, contractual
research, concept promotion, and networking. Organizationally, a think tank is likely to
affiliate with businesses, universities, government, and non-government organizations
(NGOs). Lastly, it should be noted that NGOs are independent think tanks (no affiliation).
STI policy think tanks play a role in two areas: 1) facilitating the establishment of hard and
soft infrastructures in order to improve the innovation system (IS), including monitoring
overall performance of the system, and 2) organizing and maintaining various global-wide

networks, such as funders, experts/academicians, society, and other partners (Seo et al,
2021). Regarding the first role, think tanks are not only producers of ideas or policy advocacy
groups, but also groups involved in formulating, implementing, and evaluating STI policy.
Think tanks are prompted to facilitate innovation needs, guarantee the establishment of an
OIS, and monitor the progress and impact of an OIS. As for the second one, think tanks are
a hub both for policy makers who seek information and knowledge and for academicians
who produce and provide information and knowledge. Think tanks are very important in
that they can identify and provide the right information and knowledge, from among all the
information and knowledge available in plenty of sources, to policy makers.
In the OIS, the role of think tanks could be expanded. Their role should not be limited to
facilitating the establishment of innovation infrastructure and keeping with pluralistic
networks; rather, a think tank could also assist in creating IS. As Seo et al. (2021:325) point
out, “a new think tank serves as an intermediate in the ecosystem , assisting innovative
players in establishing interactive linkages with partners in order to develop, communicate,
and turn knowledge into economic values, all of which contribute to more mature
innovation systems.” However, a think tank’s role in the STI policy making process is very
broad, comprising completed phases ranging from agenda setting, formulation and
implementation to monitoring and evaluation. A think tank moves from policy advocates to
policy entrepreneurs.

 Policies for Green Economy Growth (GEG) in Indonesia
As discussed previously, the terms GE, GG, and GEG are used inter-changeably. The
Government of Indonesia’s policies related to GE, GG, or GEG are classified as follows: 1)
Indonesia’s national long-term development plan (RPJPN) 2005-2025; 2) Indonesia’s mid-

development working group; 5) circular economy programs; and 6) the Green Economy

3.1.1 Indonesia’s national long-term development plan (RPJPN) 2005-2025
Indonesia’s national long-term development plan (RPJPN) 2005-2025 is a national policy
that covers all aspects of national development including CEG over a period of twenty years.
Under this policy, the Government of Indonesia has set up programmes for food resilience
with sustainable agriculture, sustainable forestry management, energy efficiency,
renewable energy usage, clean technology support, waste management, low carbon
transportation management, and green infrastructure development.

3.1.2 Indonesia’s national mid-term development plan (RPJMN) 2020-2024
Indonesia’s national mid-term development plan (RPJMN) 2019-2024 is a part of the RPJPN
2005-2025. Covering a period of five years, it is the end policy in the sequence of policies
within the RPJPN 2005-2025. In RPJMN 2020-2024, GE is one of the policies and pillars of
implementation deployed in Indonesia to attain the SDGs. In the Indonesian RPJMN 2020–
2024, for instance, GE is a national priority agenda and a strategic priority project. There are
arrangements for GE targets in the Indonesian RPJMN 2020-2024 which make GE a National
Need Motivation and Vital Need Venture. In expansion, there is the Green Development
Program started by the Worldwide Green Development Founded by the Global Green

3.1.3 Green Growth Program

5. Green Growth Policy and System in South Korea

 Korean Green Growth Strategy
In August 2008, the sixtieth anniversary of the establishment of the Republic of South Korea,
the President of South Korea announced "Low Carbon Green Growth" as a new vision to
guide the nation's growth for the next sixty years. The vision sets out South Korea’s aim of
becoming a global leader among low-carbon societies based on its green growth policy.
Furthermore, it declares Korea’s aim of rising to the rank of fifth “Green World Power” by
2050 and seventh by 2020. The government announced the Framework Act on Low Carbon
Green Growth in 2009, and renewed it in 2010 and 2021 in an effort to meet its lofty
objectives. The government also established the presidential committee for sustainable
development in 2007, and provincial committees in 2021.
The milestones of the National Green Growth Strategy, which primarily consists of three
objectives and ten policy directions, are defined in the legal instrument. Given that 96
percent of South Korea's principal energy sources are imported , improved energy
independence and climate change mitigation constitute the first goal . Meanwhile, the
development of green technology and the promotion of the industry's green structure are
elements of the second goal, which is to produce new catalysts for economic growth. Lastly,
the third goal is to promote a sustainable approach to living, green transportation
infrastructure, and the use of land and water.
The South Korean government originally unveiled the Five-Year Action Plan 2010-2015 to
promote technological development and assist the implementation of the Green Growth
Strategy.
Each year South Korea invests roughly 2% of its GDP in R&D, in addition to the range of
green development initiatives outlined in the plan. The government created a plan for the
implementation of the K-SDGs (Korean Sustainable Development Goals) in 2018, which

 Incorporating Key Elements of the GTC into the DPKRTI-BRIN
The section above titled “Scope of research” examines the practices of South Korea’s GTC
and identifies three key elements, namely, resources, strategy, and institutional governance,
which can be adopted by the DPKRTI-BRIN in Indonesia. As with developing countries,
Ryszawska (2017) & Sulich (2021) revealed that a lack of financial resources and institutional
capacity are the main elements for determining governmental strategies when developing
GEG policies for those countries. Practically speaking, Indonesia has similar issues related to
the development of national GEG policies.
Responding to the problem of developing GEG policies in developing countries, Ansell and
Gash (2007), Wahab (2008), Anderson (2011), Shafritz et al. (2013) all stated that
government strategies, existing resources, and institutional governance are indispensable
elements for public organizations engaged in the policy-making process. As such, these
elements should be present when applying the GTC’s practices to the DPKRTI-BRIN.
However, incorporating key elements from the GTC into the DPKRTI-BRIN is not simple in
that they cannot be transmitted fully and directly from the GTC to the DPKRTI-BRIN. Indeed,
such a process requires adjustments, or perhaps incubation, before being carried out. The

6.2.1 Government Strategy
Government strategy is part of the detailed policy of a government (Wahab, 2008). The use
of a government strategy is similar to that of a public policy. The emergence of the GTC was
very much influenced by the long -term policy of the South Korea n government in
responding to the Paris Agreement in the global arena. In 2009, the president of South Korea
announced “Act of Low Carbon Green Growth” as a long-term policy goal, through which
Korea aimed to become the fifth “global green powerhouse” by 2050. This is the general
green growth (GG) policy that prevails in the provinces of South Korea. The National Green
Growth, which consists of the three objectives and ten policy directions defined in the legal
instrument, aim to promote sustainable industrial growth in diverse sectors with a positive
impact on human well-being.
The long-term GG policy is translated into the medium-term GG policy, which is called the
Five-Year Action Plan, to accelerate the implementation of the GG policy. Each action plan
consists of instruments, achievements, and indicators. The government of Korea assumes
that the GG policy is not separated from policies for supporting green industry. The GTC was
established by the government to respond to two issues simultaneously: i.e. 1) contributing
to the global need to reduce GHG emissions by using green products and technologies, and
2) reinforcing the national green technology capability to satisfy national and global

In the contact of the DPKRTI-BRIN, the government’s strategy for achieving the SDGs is

 Enhancing the Think Tank Functions of the GTC and
Transmitting them to the DPKRTI-BRIN
The functions of a policy think tank in an open innovation system (OIS) are twofold: 1) to
facilitate the establishment of hard and soft infrastructures related to improv ing the
innovation system (IS) and monitor the system, and 2) to organize and maintain multiple
networks widely (funders, experts/academician, society, other partners) (Seo et al., 2021).
As a policy think tank, the GTC focuses on the core areas of agenda setting (i.e. identifying
technology demand), policy formulation (project design, feasibility study, and master plan),
and financial support.
The GTC facilitates the establishment of hard and soft infrastructures in order to improve
the use of green technology across countries based on three aspects, namely, 1) discussion
of the GG policy agenda with stakeholders, 2) negotiations about the implementation of
technology policy with the UNFCCC, and 3) consultation o n the development and
application of green technologies with CTCN members and developing countries. The GTC
does not carry out technical assistance projects to build such infrastructures; however, it
can be a partner for many counterparts in diverse countries in discussing GEG policies and
their instruments based on its accumulated information and knowledge.
The GTC organizes and maintains multiple networks in the national and global arenas, and
has the following divisions: 1) Technical Supervision, 2) Policy Research, 3) Climate Change
Cooperation, 4) National Climate Technology Cooperation Center, 5) Administration, 6)
Strategic Planning, and 7 ) Audit. Th ese divisions involve diverse partners such as
academicians, researchers/experts, government/stakeholders, business/
shareholders/practitioners, donor/funding agenc ies, communities/NGOs, and related
partners from South Korea and overseas. The GTC has the main duty of keeping all the
networks going in order to support its functions.
The GTC requires support from the UNFCCC, CTCN, and developing countries. Also, it is
closely connected to the Technology Executive Committee (TEC) and the Ministry of Science
and Information Communication Te chnology (ICT) in South Korea. Korea’s National
Designated Entities (NDE) are a trigger for the GTC to develop the national green technology

capability and then to transmit the results to developing countries. The GTC is dedicated to
strengthening national R&D capacity in order to develop green technology and to
responding to global green technology demands. In turn, the GTC, through the UNFCCC, is
allowed to collaborate with developing countries in developing a green technology
partnership initiative (GTPI) with Indonesia.
The GTC’s function is limited to setting the agenda and formulating policy on green
technology, while implementing and managing a green technology project lies outside its
core area. As an ideal policy think tank in the OIS, a think tank plays a role in the entire policy-
making process encompassing agenda setting, formulating, implementing, monitoring, and

national and global networks as possible in the climate change and green technology fields.
Therefore, it could be very helpful as a best practice for the DPKRTI-BRIN.

knowledge produced by academicians to STI policy produced by stakeholders .” Agenda
setting and policy formulation are core areas of a think tank’s activities. Referring to Seo et
al. (2021), it is a conventional think tank because STI policy think tanks should play a role in
the implementation and evaluation areas. The emergence of the NGTPP is an opportunity
to deliver and enhance the STI think tank at BRIN. The NGTPP is expected to become the
leading think tank in Indonesia because of its new working area. It will play a role in four
areas at once, namely, the role of an expert in the agenda setting phase, an advocacy role
in the policy formulation phase, the role of a consultant in the policy implementation phase,
and the role of a supervisor/monitor in the policy evaluation phase.

6.4.2 Creating Shared Values of Actors Involved in a New Program
Efforts to create shared values of the actors involved in a new program show an
organization’s readiness to change. Simpson (2009) states that organizational readiness for
change (ORC) is determined by three layers of factors: 1) the individual level is the lowest
layer in which each individual works for the sake of his/herself; 2) the group level is the
middle layer in which individuals work and interact with other individuals in the same groups;
and 3) the organization level is the highest layer in which all groups work and interact
together in accomplishing an organization’s goals. Thus, according to the three layers of ORC,
creating shared values works on three levels: individual, group, and organization.
Creating shared values at the individual level is related to how each individual is able to gain
benefits in responding to GEG and SDGs issues. Each individual tends to maximize personal
benefits for him/herself, during which different values in individual interests are likely to
emerge. In this case, the DPKRTI -BRIN should facilitate each individual to make shared
values among those ind ividuals related to GEG and SDG goals. Common agreements,
individual career development, rewards, broad opportunities to join in national and global
seminars, publication support, and closeness to an exclusive group circle are practical
examples of attracting individuals to join the NGTPP.
Creating shared values at the group level is related to how the individuals within groups are
able to work autonomously when addressing GEG and SDG issues. Each group tends to
struggle collectively to show their group’s contribution, to be silo and not coordinated each
other, and to compete fiercely to attract the attention of the top management. To address
this problem, the DPKRTI-BRIN makes a clear division of the tasks for each group, and there
are interdependent tasks where multiple groups need to work together. As each group is
unique in managing tasks and individuals, the DPKRTI-BRIN can provide valuation, feedback,
acknowledgements, and rewards to each group accordingly
Creating shared values at the organization level relates to how all groups are able to
collaborate in achieving the organization’s goals concerning GEG and SDG issues. Each group
has a different aim or intention in contributing to the advancement of an organizational goal.
At this level, the involvement of individuals, groups, and top-middle level management is

6.4.4 Enhancing and Strengthening National and Global Networks
National and global networks are the main capital and resources required to operate an

key points for enhancing and strengthening the national and global networks of the NGTPP.

1. Introduction

According to Presidential Regulation No. 38/2018 on RIRN 2017 -2045, the Indonesian
government has set various goals for research development for the period 2015-2045. Each
target will be raised simultaneously every five years from 2015 to 2045. In contrast, the
Indonesian research goal for 2045 is the same as the Korean scientific achievement for 2015.
Consequently, in 2015 Indonesia had a 30-year gap to catch up with Korea. In 2015,
Indonesia had 1,071 researchers per million people, whereas Korea now has 8,600
researchers per million. Regarding the government budgets for R&D, Indonesia ha d a
BOARD/GDP ratio of 0.15 percent, while Korea had a BOARD/GDP ratio of 1.26 percent in
2015. Furthermore, Indonesia had just 1,071 researchers per 1 million people, while Korea
had 8,600 researchers per 1 million. Regarding the government budget for research and
development, Indonesia's BOARD/GDP ratio was 0.15%, compared to Korea's 1.26% in 2015.
GERD was another ratio used to calculate government spending on research and
development. In 2015, Indonesia had a GERD/GDP rat e of 0.20%, whereas Korea’s was
5.04%. When comparing graduate and undergraduate students, Indonesia had a 5.6% of
graduate students per undergraduate students in 2015, while Korea had a 100% ratio.
Furthermore, for research productivity in 2015, Indonesia received a score of 0.02, while
Korea received a score of 0.22. Finally, in 2015, Indonesia had a multifactor productivity
score of 16.7%, while Korea scored 70%.

engineers, lecturers, and others, according to Law No. 11/2019. According to the PDDikti,

Meanwhile, Becker et al. (1990) propose a human capital fertility model. Fitz-Enz, J. (2000)

consistent with the ideas of David, P. and Lopez, J. (2001), who define human capital as

attempting to produce quality research programs that result in international publications,
researchers are limited by time constraints. The average time required to conduct research

Indonesia’s state university bureaucracy. State employment regulations and policies for civil
servants are based on the State Employment Agency's promotion system, which recognizes
advancement based on the accumulation of credit point s rather than academic
performance. These points can be earned by teaching, giving seminars, and other activities,
with research and international publication receiving less weight.

level in order to be able to admit more students and thereby fund their operating costs,
resulting in a decline in academic research at state universities. The issue of “inbreeding”
has resulted in isolationism because, despite efforts to encourage more joint research, these
problems indicate that universities and their faculty are more concerned with their
individual research interests. Under these circumstances, Indonesian academics are more
likely to pursue advanced degrees at their home institutions, which may have contributed
to more “inbreeding” among state universities.

Due to a lack of institutional support, including core funding for academic research,
university research centers focused on consultancy and service provider activities rather

NGOs. Hence, at the micro-individual level, academics gained status and financial rewards
by becoming prominent media commentators, policy advisors, and service providers rather
than by doing research (McCarthy, 2010).

4.2.1 Background to and Purpose of Enactment of the Special Act
In Korea, in the early stages of national economic growth, the role of scientists and
engineers who returned to Korea after completing doctoral programs was significant. They
studied in advanced countries such as the United States and worked in the industries of
advanced countries to build their careers. These advanced human resources in the fields of
science and engineering made a highly significant contribution to nurturing excellent human
resources for master’s and doctoral degrees in universities in Korea.
In the late 1990s, the following series of problems were raised (Kang et al., 2003: 30-36) in
relation to the imbalance in the supply and demand of S&T human resources and the use of
human resources in the science and engineering fields: 1) the lack of high -quality
professionals in the science and engineering fields arising from a failure to respond to
industrial demand; 2) the inadequate establishment of a career-path development system
for scientific and technological personnel; 3) talented youth’s avoidance of science and
engineering subjects due to poor socioeconomic treatment compared to professi onal
occupations such as law and medicine; 4) the problem of science and engineering
researchers and graduate students switching majors to non-science and engineering fields;
5) the lack of relevant and comprehensive science and engineering statistical data and
human resource databases; 6) the relatively high rates of unemployment among high-level
human resources with master's and doctoral degrees after the IMF economic crisis; and 7)
the lack of science and engineering personnel in public offices.
Among these problems, the avoidance of science and engineering majors by outstanding
talents was given particular emphasis. As such, it is necessary to propose a legal basis for
inducing excellent students to choose university majors in science and engineering and to
support human resources in science and engineering. Accordingly, in April 2004, the Special
Act on Support for S cientists and Engineers for Strengthening National Science and
Technology Competitiveness (hereinafter referred to as the “Special Act”) was enacted.

4.2.2 Overview of the Special Act
The Special Act covers all aspects of human resources in the science and engineering fields,
from nurturing to promoting the use and improving the status of science and engineering
personnel and building the necessary infrastructure. At the time of its enactment in 2004,
the Special Act consisted of four chapters and twenty-five articles ([See Table 3.8]). Looking
at the main contents, Chapter 1, as a general rule, stipulates the reason and purpose for
enacting the law and the state's responsibility . Chapter 2 define s the contents of the
establishment and implementation of the master plans for nurturing and supporting
scientists and engineers. Chapter 3 presents ways to foster human resources in the science
and engineering fields, and stipulates scholarship support for outstanding science and
engineering students (Article 9), support for strengthening linka ges between industrial,
academic and research circles (Article 10), fostering and supporting research -oriented
universities (Article 11), and re -education and retraining for science and engineering
personnel (Article 12). Chapter 4 aims to promote the use of scientists and engineers and
improve their status. This chapter also stipulated expansion of the appointment of scientists
and engineers as public officials (Article 13), financial support for companies seeking to
recruit scientists and engineers (Article 16), support for universities that operate programs
to strengthen research and education in the science and engineering fields (Article 21), and
the establishment of R&D job placement centers to assist scientists and engineers with
employment or reemployment (Article 22).

4.3.1 Government established Top Research University: KAIST
Established in 1971 as Korea's first research -oriented university, t he Korea Advanced
Institute of Science and Technology(KAIST) has produced a total of 69,388 graduates (as of
2021), including 19,457 bachelor's, 35,513 master's, and 14,418 doctoral degree students.5
It has established itself as an excellent university, ranking 41st in the QS World University
Rankings in 2022 and 16th in Engineering in the 2021 QS World University Rankings (KAIST,
2021a). KAIST primarily focuses on history, education, and research (KAIST, 2021b; KAIST,
2021c).

◼ History
The idea for a new graduate school of science and engineering surfaced in the late 1960s,
along with the rising demand for skilled engineers to enhance the nation's industrialization.
KAIST was established in February 1971 as the country’s very first research-intensive
university under the name of the Korea Advanced Institute of Science (KAIS). KAIS was
established with the technical and financial assistance of the U.S. Agency for International
Development (USAID) with the full endorsement of the Korean gov ernment, and was
mandated by a special law. Under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Science and Technology,
KAIS concentrated on fostering science and engineering human resources with considerable
autonomy. Furthermore, with full backing from the government, KAIS was able to provide
support, such as teachers’ salaries at the level of advanced countries and tuition waivers for
full-time students, as well as substituting mandatory military service with research.
In January 1981, KAIS was merged with Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST,
Korea's first comprehensive government research institute created in 1966), resulting in a
change of name to KAIST. The government revised the Special Law on KAIST in order to
establish an undergraduate college and established the Korea Institute of Technology (KIT)
in 1985. In July 1989, KAIST was separated from KIST and merged with KIT, which made
KAIST a full -fledged university able to provide every level of degree course including
bachelor's, master’s and doctoral programs.
In 1996, the Korea Institute for Advanced Study (KIAS), Korea's first research institute
devoted purely to theoretical studies on the basic sciences, was established as an affiliated
institution of KAIST. In 2009, the Information and Communications University (ICU),
established in 1997 under the Ministry of Information and Communication, was absorbed
into KAIST. As a result, KAIST reached a critical mass for securing global competitiveness in
the IT field. Also in that year, Korea's first school of science for gifted students, the Korea
Science Academy(KSA), became a part of KAIST. As a result, KAIST has established a
comprehensive education system for nurturing gifted science and engineering students
from high, undergraduate, and graduate schools.

◼ Education
Since KAIST was established and operated under the KAIST Law, it has enjoyed a relatively
high degree of autonomy in student selection compared to other public and private
universities regulated by the Ministry of Education.
The characteristics of the student selection process are as follows. In addition to the written
test, selection is made on the basis of creativity tests and in-depth interviews. In particular,
to induce high school students at the top of the science and engineering fields to enter the
university, high school students are allowed to apply for the entrance examination before
graduating from high school graduation, provided that they meet certain conditions. All new
students are admitted into a single department, and may only choose their majors and
departments freely at the end of their first year. In the case of the graduate school,
admission without a written test was introduced in 1994 for the doctoral program and in
1998 for the master's program. The written test may be taken according to the policy of the
department.
KAIST runs its departments by reflecting government policies in its graduate programs. It
has established the Graduate School of AI and the Graduate School of Future Strategy, and
also runs a specialized master's program for nurturing industrial human resources. In 2016,
it introduced a master's program in entrepreneurship and innovation comprising a one-year
training period in order to nurture talents with an entrepreneurial spirit.
KAIST also has a well-equipped scholarship system for nurturing talented people in S&T. In
the case of its undergraduate programs, tuition fees and a small portion of students’ living
expenses are subsidized. In the case of its graduate programs, tuition fees are subsidized
differentially according to each individual student’s admission conditions. However, KAIST
provides support of at least 700,000 won for master's students and one million won for
doctoral students. If the allowance that graduate students receive through such activities as
employment as research and teaching assistants is less than the amount specified above,
KAIST provides them with additional grants to guarantee them a minimum standard of living.

◼ R&D system
KAIST has been carrying out R&D projects ever since its foundation. Established by a special
law, KAIST has dual status as a university and a research institute, so it has different
characteristics from other public and private universities under the Ministry of Education in
terms of the funding it receives for research projects. KAIST receives a large amount of
support from the government through national R&D projects. Therefore, government
subsidies account for a higher amount of its total budget than is the case for other
universities.
KAIST provides basic research funds to all its faculty members to create an environment in
which they can immerse themselves in their research. KAIST supports the research projects
not only of its faculty members but also those if its graduate and undergraduate students.
For example, the Undergraduate Research Participation Program (since 2006) and the
Master's and Doctoral Adventure Research Program since (2012) are operated to provide
students with opportunities to present creative ideas and conduct research by themselves.
The annual budget is around 800 million won for undergraduate research programs, and
400 million won for master’s and doctoral research programs.
KAIST has also established and oversees about 150 research centers, including the Research
Center of KAIST, the Institute of Natural Sciences, the Institute of Mechanical Technology,
the Institute of Industrial Management, the Institute of Applied Science, the Institute of
Information Electronics, the Institute of Techno Management, and the Institute of
Humanities and Social Sciences.

4.3.2 Government-Funded Research Institute Joint Graduate School: UST
◼ History
The University of Science and Technology (UST) was jointly founded by twenty-two
government-funded research institutes in 2003. The UST has fostered talented individuals
in S&T by utilizing its outstanding experts, research knowledge and experience, and research
facilities. As of 2022, thirty-two government-funded research institutes are participating.
The legal basis for UST ’s foundation is set forth in Article 33(1) of the Act on the

Establishment, Operation and Fostering of Government-funded Science and Technology
Research Institutions, etc., which is under the jurisdiction of the Ministry of Science and
Technology. The main goal of the UST ’s educational programs is to cultivate practically-
minded, creative human resources to lead development and innovation in the field of core
and comprehensive technologies and industrial technologies based on field-centered
education and research on new interdisciplinary and converging technologies (UST, 2020;
UST,2022).

◼ Characteristics of the UST
First, there are thirty-two government-funded research institutes dedicated to fostering
master’s and Ph.D. students, with around 1,000 of the ir 13,000 researchers serving as
professors. In particular, students are guided by competent doctoral-level researchers who
serve as academic advisors, together with a group of experts in the field. Students can use
the latest research equipment during their field-centered education. Student mentoring is
very strong, with a professor-to-student ratio close to 1:1.
Second, it has the flexibility required to create and change majors . The curriculum is
reorganized every 2-3 years according to changes in the R&D environment. Majors are
opened according t o the professional fields of government-funded research institutes
related to high-tech industries and public services. The UST's majors are broadly divided into
majors for national strategies and those for resolving current issues. The majors for national
strategies are long-term majors aimed at developing and cultivating experts in S&T related
to national strategy, whereas the majors for resolving current issues are supported in the
mid- to long-term perspective with the aim of fostering S&T experts who can respond to
current social demands and governmental issues. The latter m ajors are related to the
following issues: infectious diseases; ICT fields such as artificial intelligence and machine
learning; environmental fields such as particulate matter; and s afety fields such as
earthquake, radioactivity, materials, parts, and equipment. In addition, the UST operates an
Industry Co-Operation Research Education (I-CORE) course based on an agreement it signed
with industries and national research institutes to create and operate a new major’s course
for the recruitment of industrial field personnel and the career development of experienced
workers. The course is divided into two main tracks: the recruitment type, in which industry

BK 21 was the largest government-initiated project in the education sector.

 Creation of More Flexible Regulations for Government
Researchers
Judging by the results of the data analysis, there are various conditions that are specific to
Indonesian researchers. First, researchers and lecturers at state universities in Indonesia
have the same personal status as civil servants, which means that they also follow the same
rules as civil servants, despite the obvious differences in their work. The rules regarding civil
servants are made by KemenPANRB and BKN, and they are also affected by government
regulations and other regulations. However, as researchers are professionals who require
special competencies in the scientific field, the recruitment process should differ from that
of administrative employees.
Second, certain regulations on HR development sometimes conflict with civil servant
regulations, such as start-up ownership, mobility, and issues that require flexibility for HRST.
Concrete examples in government R&D institutions and universities and the budget
management system tend to be rigid, as they are tied to the system's rigidity in the
bureaucratic organization, and start-up ownership is limited to researchers. In the past,
government R&D institutions such as BPPT, LIPI, BATAN, and LAPAN had incuba tion
institutions which were work units attached to the hierarchical organizational structure.
Hence, funding these incubation institutions was still a burden on the state budget.
Meanwhile, the income pattern is based on Non-Tax State Revenue (PNBP), in which the
system must deposit with the Ministry of Finance and cannot be directly managed by the
incubation institution. The current condition is not much different from that experienced by
Pusyantek – BRIN or the university's incubation institute. For this reason, a middle way is
needed to bridge the regulatory rigidities, as this will increase the technology transfer
pattern.

Technology transfer refers to the transformation of research activities into a ctual
applications (vertical) or the movement of what occurs in one field to another (horizontally)
(Grosse, 1996). Based on Law No. 18 of 2002, technology transfer is the ability to use and
master science and technology between institutions, agencies and people within the
country or overseas. Technology transfer can be realized through a joint venture mechanism
or a cross-licensing agreement (Grosse, 1996).
Another form of technology transfer is possible through the Technology Transfer Office
(TTO), an intermediary institution that facilitates technology transfer processes by bridging
the gap between research and innovation (Ristekdikti, 2017). Through the TTO, government
or university R&D can facilitate the formation of start -ups by utilizing products and
technology produced by R&D institutions or universities ( spill over). They can generate
royalties for research inventors, and also generate funding sources, as well as facilitate
Intellectual Property (IP) for downstream R&D products.
The TTO can be a Public Service Agency (BLU), i.e. it can be more flexible and not bound by
the rigidity of the ASN rules ; for instance, in managing the budget autonomously.
Regulations regarding the Public Service Agency (BLU) are regulated in Government
Regulation No. 72 of 2012. Professional positions in the TTO can be filled by the State Civil
Apparatus (ASN) and Non-ASN, including young professionals or entrepreneurs. The portion
of non-ASN human resources could be bigger, considering that rigid bureaucratic rules limit
the work pattern of ASN.
The TTO could also serve as an autonomous institution that connects R&D institutions with
industry (i.e. building corporate relationships with local businesses) because the most
important thing is how the R&D results ( spill over) can be used by business partners
(industry). With the right business partners, R&D institutions and universities could produce
the technology products that users need. For this reason, it is vital to have the right basic
program and a good program evaluation.

Korea and other countries). Nevertheless, regulations that constrain lecturers and
researchers need to be made more flexible. In Korea, researchers and civil servants are

6. Conclusion

This study points out that Indonesia urgently needs to take bold steps to promote the
development of HRST. More particularly, it needs to increase the number of doctoral
researchers as this is essential for increasing the nation’s R&D capacity. One approach would
consist in coordinating a variety of scholarship programs conducted by different ministries
and tailoring them to fit the needs of researchers and graduate school students.
Furthermore, it is necessary to upgrade the 'Degree by Research' program offered by BRIN
so as to make it a better program that integrates other funding sources, such as LPDP, which
is dedicated to HRST.
In addition, collaborations involving public research institutes, universities, and industry are
critical to enhancing Indonesia’s HRST competencies and capacities. Facilitating mobility by
exchanging knowledge and technology currently a major problem for Indonesia. However,
mobility can be promoted by targeting funding schemes and by building collaboration
platforms. Lastly, a more flexible application of government civil servant regulations to
government researchers would facilitate mobility and benefit the development of HRST.

2. Research Framework and Method
This research covers the research of public research organizations (PRO) in South Korea,
such as government-funded research institutes (GRIs), non-profit research institutes (NPRIs),
and National or Public Research Institutes (N/PRIs), with a focus on research and effective
intermediation (funding, governance, and commercialization of technology), which will
serve as worthwhile lessons for BRIN to develop and improve its current management and
governance.
This study has been based on the framework of the management regime. The term
“management regime” is commonly used to describe the system, method or way of doing
business in an organization. In the scientific realm, this term is often used to describe
practices in an environmental ecosystem, such as in agriculture (e.g. fertilization, farming
practices, crop circulation), in forestry (e.g. conservation/pr otection, specie/habitat
frequency), or in fisheries (e.g. fish stock s, fishing practices). Focusing on research and
innovation, the management regime of such organizations encompasses those aspects that
sustain its way of doing business and producing innovations. The five elements of the
management regime that will be used in this study are as follows: (1) Objectives, (2)
Strategies, (3) Governance, (4) Funding, and (5) Organization Management.
Generally speaking, an “objective” constitutes the main reason or purpose of forming an
organization. The objective, in other words, the goal, is something that the organization
collectively wants to achieve or realize in the future (Christensen, et al., 2007). Thus, in order
to fulfil the organizational objectives (1), strategies (2) are set as a short-term goal, and these
are meant to define the organizational objectives in a more operational manner. The
objectives and strategies of the organization are then supported by its governance (3),
funding mechanism (4), and organizational management (5). The concept of governance
refers to the system in which organizations are directed or controlled (Cadbury Report,
1992). The funding mechanism and organization management are more closely related to
the technical aspects of management regimes, which explains how an organization
manages its funding resources and organizational functions.

3. Results and Analysis

 Missions and Objectives of BRIN and the NST/GRIs
The genuine development of Korean S&T began when the military government of Park
Chung Hee, which took power on May 16, 1961, announced the First Five-Year Economic
Development Plan for 1962-1966 (EPB, 1962). Accordingly, the major objectives and
strategies of South K orea’s S&T policies in the 1960s were to build a technological
infrastructure to support the government’s strategy, including the creation of a government
department responsible for S&T matters, the enactment of laws to support S&T
development, the creation of new R&D performance capabilities, and the development of
human resources. The government decided to establish the Korea Institute of Science and
Technology (KIST), a new research institute with an excellent environment and high salaries,
to conduct a wide range of industrial R&D, including technological feasibility studies,
technological services for small and medium companies, and engineering studies on a pilot
plant scale.
The main focus was on finding solutions for simple and practical problems arising from the
application of technologies transferred from abroad. KIST led Korea’s early stage of
technological innovation and made a major contribution to solving many problems during
the process of adapt ing and applying imported technologies, under a contractual R&D
system with business (Kim, L.S., 1997: 139). In the 1970s, as labour-intensive industries were
gradually replaced by heavy industry and chemicals, it was vital to es tablish an S&T
infrastructure capable of modifying and internalizing foreign technology in order to meet
the demand for the more sophisticated production technologies required by the machinery,
steel, chemical engineering, shipbuilding, and electronics industries (OECD, 1996). To meet
the ever-increasing demand for qualified scientists and engineers, Korea’s S&T policy
focused on expanding technical and engineering education in the related fields.
Furthermore, a number of GRIs were established in the 1970s in such fields as machinery,
shipbuilding, chemicals, marine science, electronics, and electricity to satisfy diverse
industrial needs and to improve the institutional mechanism for adapting and internalizing

The main objectives of the STI policy in the 1990s were to i) build the industry-led technology
innovation system and strengthen industrial competitiveness; ii) promote the strategic drive of
national R&D programs both in individual departments and across all relevant departments; iii)
nurture all R&D performers and promote collaboration between them; iv) strengthen the base for
science and technology innovation; v) increase government expenditure on R&D ; vi) prioritize
efficient and effective government investment; vii) pay greater attention to basic research; and viii)
promote a scientific culture focused on globalisation and localisation.
During this period, the main objectives of Korea’s GRIs were switched to the pursuit of efficiency and
effectiveness, the creation of a competitive environment, and increased emphasis on performance
and R&D quality, accountability and autonomous responsibility, the utilisation of R&D across all
departments, and collaboration with other R&D performers such as universities and industry. Then,
in January 1999, the Research Council system was introduced to support these changes in the
objectives of the GRIs.
With the evolution of the Research Councils, the main objectives of the GRIs under the National
Research Council of Science and Technology (NST) since 2014 have been to develop new
technologies, support companies and industries , contribute to regional innovation,
participate in global cooperation, provide give solutions to societal problems, and support
the national agenda and government policy in the field of science and technology.

has been leading and driving the GRIs towards securing research capabilities, fostering GRIs

the aim of fostering GRIs that are trusted by the general public, securing collaborative
research capabilities, and establishing a research-friendly supportive system. The main
mission and objectives of the NST are to support national research projects and policies and
to lead future -oriented national STI, create a sustainable convergence innovation
ecosystem, and strengthen social responsibility.

 Governance
The Korean government created the Economic Planning Board (EPB) in July 1961, and then
established the Technology Development Bureau, a government unit responsible for S&T
policy within the EPB, in 1962. The first and second five-year plans for the promotion of S&T
were formulated in line with two Five-Year Economic Development Plans for the periods
1962-1966 and 1966-1970. In addition, the S&T Promotion Law was enacted in 1967, and
the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) was established as a lower level government
department in 1967, in order to put science and technology higher on the agenda. Th e
establishment of the MOST and its functions within the policy mechanism of the central
government meant that S&T would become one of the national tools from the outset of
national development, unlike in other less developed countries (MOST, 1997: 43).
After the creation of KIST in 1966, the enactment of the Law for the Promotion of Specific
Research Institutes in December 1973 was designed to drive the proliferation of GRIs. After
the dramatic development and expansion of the GRIs and the national R&D program in the
1980s and 1990s, the National Science and Technology Council (NSTC), chaired by the
president, was launched in January 1999 to prioritize and coordinate national R&D
programs and policies. The Minister for Science and Technology became the Secretariat of
the NSTC, and the KISTEP was created under the MOST to evaluate the national R&D

 Funding
At the end of the 1970s, South Korea’s total R&D investment represented about 0.8% of
GNP, with government expenditure accounting for almost 70 % of the nation’s total R&D
investment. N/PRIs and GRIs accounted for 86.4% of the total domestic R&D investment in
1969 and 56.4% in 1979, while industry’s share was 10.2% in 1969 and 34.1% in 1979. Of
the 86.4% for which N/PRIs and GRIs were collectively responsible, GRIs carried out 51.6%.
In 1973, KIST conducted about 170 projects, i.e. three times as many as in the early stage,
and the portion of R&D funds originating from industry was 61%. Consequently, KIST
received rave reviews as a Korean success story (MOST, 1997: 31).
The GRIs managed organizations by using grant-in-aid funding from the government and
conducted research under project contracts with private companies until the mid-1990s.
This kind of dual funding system for the GRIs began to change with the introduction of the
Project Based System (PBS) in January 1996. At first, this system aimed to abolish the grant-
in-aid funding and to introduce a new system of funding based on the full-cost pricing of all
R&D projects, including the salaries of staff participating in R&D projects and the operating
cost of the GRIs. The government decided to keep less than 30% of the institutional funding
for the least stable operations of the GRIs. Since the establishment of the Research Councils
in 1999, the funding s ystem based on PBS has continue to evolve. In one sense the
introduction of the PBS resulted in a highly unstable funding system and too much open
competitive contracting. To remedy the problem of unstable GRIs that lacked institutional
funding, the Finance Ministry started allocating core institutional R&D projects to the GRIs
under a project based system that was free from any competition.
Nowadays, South Korea’s GRIs benefit from two types of funding: institutional funding and
project-based funding. In 2021, the GRIs’ funding consisted of institutional funding (43%)
from the central government and project-based funding (57%) from government
departments and industries, etc. The GRIs must submit their annual budget plans for the

 Organization Management
South Korea’s N/PRIs were a part of the departments immersed within the bureaucratic
culture and hence lacked autonomy. GRIs, including the KIST, were designed to guarantee
not only the principle of autonomy, but also to ensure financial support for stable R&D from
the government. They were also self -financing in relation to the overhead s of internal
research divisions and labs, which were their basic management units. Individual labs were
compelled to earn sufficient funds from research contracts to cover their overheads for
human resources and facilities. Despite the principle of stability and autonomy, the GRIs
faced two major problems at the start of the 1980s: i) less autonomy under the government
departments and ii) less stability due to a decline in the number of research contracts from
industries which they themselves had grown.

evaluation and a three-year term evaluation, the results of which were used to determine
the performance-related allocation of budgets by the Finance Minister. Th is evaluation
scheme has continued to evolve since the establishment of the Research Councils in 1999,
based on the A ct on the Establishment, Management and Promotion of Government -
funded Research Institutes.

The purpose of commercialization through industry is to link BRIN ’s research results to

4. Policy Recommendations

 Strengthening Research Convergence
More collaborative programs are needed to give BRIN more opportunities to develop
convergent technologies and create new demand industries based on those technologies.
Organizational and logistical challenges are essential for activating convergence research,
supporting various degrees of integration between different disciplines, and adopting new
convergence strategies through grants, workshops, and cooperative agreements.
One of the main convergent research goals is to solve issues and challenges facing society,
such as climate change, disasters, energy and transportation issues, etc. Convergent
research has two characteristic features: 1) challenging research driven by problems arising
from scientific questions or acute societal demand; and (2) the integration of research
across disciplines, i.e. interweaving different disciplines in order to find solutions to research
challenges based on diverse knowledge, theories, methods, and data. In short, convergent
research is similar to transdisciplinary research. Transdisciplinary research accompanies
interdisciplinary and multidisciplinary research (International Center for Transdisciplinary
Research in Darian-Smith and McCartney, 2016). The elements of transdisciplinary work are
problem-oriented and based on the practical application of knowledge.

In South Korea, the main background to the foundation of the NST is the need to activate

The convergent research strategy could be implemented at BRIN via research funding
programs, both from the national budget (APBN) and from external funding. This policy
recommendation will specifically explore the arrangement of research funding programs
from the national budget (APBN).
Currently, in the early stage of BRIN’s formation, the tools and mechanisms used to support
mission-oriented research seem to be scattered, and lack guiding actors; or, if they exist,
their agency in integrating and connecting one program with the others is rather weak. Basic
research funding programs for research organizations (OR) and research centers (PR) can
be obtained through a scheme known as ‘Program House.’ The ‘Program House’ research
theme is determined by each OR and the head of BRIN to support the solution of global
and/or national issues. The research proposals submitted are expected to be able to solve

In a mission-oriented research term, Mazzucato (2018) implies the importance of sustained

4.1.1 Encouraging Convergence through Research Funding Policies
a. Reinforcement of the existing funding scheme for research convergence in BRIN
through the finding program of the Research Collaboration Center (Pusat Kolaborasi

BRIN with the research organizations of industry or universities with specific research
topics. The topic /mission of each collaboration needs to be determined according
specific top -down guideline s, contrary to the current mechanism in which the
topic/mission is determined by the researchers in a bottom -up manner. This
mechanism also needs to be strengthened with a clear research plan that supports

collaboration, and the prioritization of research proposal s in other research funding

Placing an industry as a research partner needs to be a prerequisite of the BRIN research

4.2.1 Creating a co-investment program with the private sector for research
development
This could be implemented by creating a new research grant scheme that invites industry
or private sector contributions (in cash or in other forms) and rewards the contributors with
co-investment by the government (usually a multiple of the industry funding). Furthermore,

The incentives for industry to conduct research and development are now a standard policy
solution to boost national investment in research. Most countries, whether they are mature
or developing knowledge economies, have now adopted tax incentives (as deductions or
credits) for industry expenditure on research and development (Appelt et al, 2019; OECD,
2017).
Some economies (generally those with highly developed industrial sectors and/or very high
levels of foreign investment) have been successful in securing significant research and
development spending by industry.
That, however, does not guarantee that all research actors are collaborating and producing
research of the highest quality possible. Strategies for attracting more industry expenditure
on research and development by co-investing government funds, as mentioned in Making
Indonesia’s Research and Development Better (2020), are becoming increasingly important.
Making such co-investment conditional will help to forge partnerships between industry,
university, and/or government or non-governmental research actors. In fact, such ‘linkage’,
‘consortium’ or ‘collaboration’ schemes are quite often linked to national research missions.

The downstream (hilirisasi) process of utilizing research results is still a significant issue in
Indonesia. When it comes to commercialization, there are certain requirements which must

supported by several documents and data testing.
BRIN could act as an intermediary between research actors and industry by creating
guidelines on the standards and processes to follow, and by assisting with the conclusion of
mutually acceptable research and downstream agreements. This would ensure results of
better quality and minimize the possibility of the results being rejected by industry and the
private sector. BRIN could also use its convening power through the human resource
support in licensing technology and incubation services to help identify the needs of a given
industry or company and help it to connect and communicate with the research actors.

Formulation: Lessons Learned from South Korean Practices
The study has explored the practices of the prominent GTC in South Korea, as outlined in

strategy, and governance of the institution on the GTC which could be adopted by the PKRTI-

of the political and government system of South Korea, a developed country, into Indonesia,
a developing country; in other words, the situation in these two countries is vastly different.
Korean cases can influence how a public think tank works and sustains itself. Besides, history,
value, and culture are unveiled factors that influence the work style of think tanks in both
countries.
Both the GTC in South Korea and the PKRTI-BRIN in Indonesia are public R&D institutions
that provide STI policies based on the results of research and development activities .
Although the GTC can provide practical lessons for the DPKRTI-BRIN, it is no secret that
establishing a world-leading think tank requires ample resources, specific and long-term
policies or strategies, and appropriate institutional governance. We will elaborate on those
elements as follows.

2.2.3 Improvement of the Research Personnel Regulations to Disseminate
Research Results
In Indonesia, lecturers and researchers at national universities and BRIN researchers are
subject to the same regulations as civil servants. The regulations for public officials have
limitations in dealing with such issues as start-up ownership and human resource flexibility.
In particular, when a researcher from a public institute starts a business, there are
restrictions on ownership and budget execution. The Ministry of Finance manages the
research budget of the start-up, and the start-up is owned by the institution. Therefore, the
current system, which restricts autonomy due to such civil servant regulations, urgently
needs to be improved.
This study presents two main alternatives. The first is to establish an intermediary agency
for start-up and technology transfer. The members of the Technology Transfer Office (TTO)
can alleviate potential rigidity in the operation of government agencies by staffing them
mainly with private personnel. The e stablishment of the Technology Transfer Office is
feasible according to government regulation no. 72 of 2012.
The second alternative is to reorganize the classification system of public research
institutions such as BRIN, a civil servant organization. Government -funded research
institutes in Korea are classified and operated as quasi-governmental institutions, rather
than as government ones. Because they are not an official government organization, it has
autonomy and independence in executing its budget and using its human resources.
However, in the case of Indonesia, the concept of a third sector public sector (i.e. Korean
quasi-governmental institutions) is unfamiliar, so it may be challenging to convert the status
of a researcher into a civilian by introducing Korea's R&D system as it is. Alternatively, it may
be possible to consider a plan to operate such institutes by partially easing the current
regulations, including incentive payments for research achievements and recognition of
research achievements. However, deregulation must also be accompanied by researchers'
responsibility. In this case, it is necessary to establish a system for evaluating whether
researchers carry out their duties faithfully within the institution. However, it is possible to
minimize the potential side effects of deregulation through continuous monitoring.

 Monitoring Policy Recommendations and Policy Success
“Policy transfer” refers to the process by which policies implemented by other governments
or international organizations are transferred to and adopted by a specific government. To
ensure the success of a policy transfer, it is necessary to modify and improve policies that
are more appropriate for one's own country rather than simply transferring other countries'
policies as they are. In other words, if the time and circumstances are different, the situation
or context of the policy problem will also be different. As a result, the nature of the policy
problem changes, so we should consider this in the design of policy alternatives.
The K-innovation project has been working to improve Indonesia's SIT governance for three
years, from 2020 to 2022. South Korea's experience based on the Framework Act on Science
