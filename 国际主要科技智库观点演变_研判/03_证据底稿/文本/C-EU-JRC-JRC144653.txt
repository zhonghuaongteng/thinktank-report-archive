Abstract
Nuclear power plays a crucial role in Europe's energy landscape, providing nearly a quarter of the
electricity produced in the region. With approximately 100 active nuclear power reactors Europe
operates almost a quarter of the global power reactors. Meanwhile, China is advancing rapidly with
around 30 ongoing projects, positioning itself to soon overtake France and, eventually, the US to
hold the largest nuclear reactor fleet worldwide.
Like other areas of innovation, the market for nuclear reactors in Europe faces challenges due to
persisting specific national regulator prerogatives and constraints, preventing the development of a
fully integrated single market. Stakeholders are addressing these challenges, aiming to unlock the
full potential of the European nuclear industry.
Europe boasts competitive technology and industrial capacities, even on a global scale. European
enterprises handle most components of the nuclear supply chain, although the global export market
for large-scale nuclear power plants remains dominated by Russia.
Looking to the future, the development of small modular and advanced modular reactors presents a
promising opportunity for the European industry. However, domestic start-ups face intense
competition from predominantly US enterprises, which appear to have secured earlier and more
support from venture capitalists. In Europe, on the other hand, venture capital is scarcer and nuclear
has traditionally been more dependent on public as opposed to private funding.
The nuclear sector is a significant source of employment in Europe, supporting approximately
500 000 direct and indirect jobs, even in Member States without their own nuclear power plants.
However, the age structure of the workforce presents a challenge. Over the next two decades, the
sector will require up to 250 000 new recruits across a wide range of STEM
1 professions,
necessitating proactive strategies to attract and train the next generation.

Foreword on the Clean Energy Technology Observatory
The European Commission set up the Clean Energy Technology Observatory (CETO) in 2022 to help
address the complexity and multi-faceted character of the transition to a climate-neutral society in
Europe. The EU’s ambitious energy and climate policies create a necessity to tackle the related
challenges in a comprehensive manner, recognizing the important role for advanced technologies
and innovation in the process.
CETO is a joint initiative of the European Commission Joint Research Centre (JRC), who run the
observatory, and Directorate-Generals Research and Innovation (R&I) and Energy (ENER) on the
policy side. Its overall objectives are to:
• monitor the EU research and innovation activities on clean energy technologies needed for
the delivery of the European climate change mitigation actions
• assess the competitiveness of the EU clean energy sector and its positioning in the global
energy market
• build on existing Commission studies, relevant information & knowledge in Commission
services and agencies, and the Low Carbon Energy Observatory (2015-2020)
• publish update reports on the Strategic Energy Technology Plan (SET-Plan
) Information
System (SETIS) online platform
CETO provides a repository of techno- and socio-economic data on the most relevant technologies
and their integration in the energy system. It targets in particular the status and outlook for
innovative solutions as well as the sustainable market uptake of both mature and innovative
technologies. The project serves as primary source of data for the Commission’s annual progress
reports on competitiveness of clean energy technologies. It also supports the implementation and
development of EU research and innovation policies.

Executive summary

EU objectives and challenges / policy context
Aligned with the European Union’s climate neutrality objective and the REPowerEU strategy, the
European Commission has underscored the importance of low-carbon energy sources in its February
2024 Communication entitled 'Securing Our Future: Europe’s 2040 Climate Target and Path to
Climate Neutrality by 2050 – Building a Sustainable, Just and Prosperous Society' [1].
In January 2025, the European Commission adopted the Communication entitled 'A Competitiveness
Compass for the EU’ [2], presenting a strategic roadmap to revitalise Europe's economic dynamism
and foster sustainable growth. Central to this initiative is the objective of enhancing the European
Union’s global competitiveness by closing the innovation gap, accelerating the decarbonisation of
the economy, and reducing strategic dependencies. It outlines a strategic shift towards clean and
affordable energy, notably through the introduction of the Clean Industrial Deal [3]. This initiative
aims to support the reduction of carbon emissions—particularly within energy-intensive sectors—by
facilitating the adoption of low-carbon technologies. Complementing this effort, an Affordable
Energy Action Plan [4] is developed to help lower energy prices and reduce overall energy costs.
Furthermore, the EU strengthens its innovation ecosystem through a dedicated EU Start-up and
Scale-up Strategy [5]. This strategy will focus on promoting innovation-friendly regulation,
enhancing access to finance, accelerating market deployment and expansion, attracting and
retaining top talent, and improving access to essential infrastructure, networks, and services. In May
2025, the European Commission presented the REPowerEU Roadmap [6] to gradually phase out
Russian oil and gas and restrict imports of uranium, enriched uranium, and other nuclear materials
derived from Russia, while simultaneously accelerating the transition to clean energy.
In early July, the European Commission proposed an amendment to the EU Climate Law [7]
establishing a 2040 target to reduce net greenhouse gas emissions by 90% relative to 1990 levels.
This ambitious objective aims to provide long-term certainty for investors and innovators, reinforce
the global industrial leadership of EU enterprises, and enhance Europe’s energy security. The EU
climate target for 2040 [8] emphasises the role of nuclear energy alongside other clean
technologies.
The Net-Zero Industry Act (NZIA) [9], adopted last year, will be instrumental in facilitating and
accelerating the manufacturing of nuclear components within the EU and could be a powerful
legislative tool to strengthen and revitalise the nuclear supply chain development in the coming
years. The Nuclear Illustrative Programme (PINC) [10] adopted by the Commission in June 2025
provides an up-to-date, comprehensive, fact-based overview of nuclear energy investment needs
across the EU, underlining the role of nuclear to ’supply clean power, suitable for low-carbon
baseload electricity, also enhancing system integration and providing flexibility, facilitating further
roll-out of other clean technologies’.
As announced in the Affordable Energy Action Plan, a Communication on the SMR strategy is
expected to be adopted in 2026.
More than 20% of the electricity production in Europe, amounting to over 600 TWh annually, is
generated by 100 nuclear reactors. To achieve climate neutrality by 2050, the demand for
electricity from low-carbon technologies is expected to rise, along with the need to supply process
heat for industries generated by low-carbon energy sources. Current nuclear reactors primarily
produce electricity, and their applications in providing process heat are limited. However, with the

8

development of Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) and Advanced Modular Reactors (AMRs), there is
hope that the need for heat for industrial processes can also be addressed.
AMRs are still in the technical developmental phase, and clear financial and regulatory frameworks
have yet to be established for SMRs. Additionally, the lack of new nuclear builds over the past
decades has negatively impacted the nuclear supply chain across Europe.
Technology status
The technology status in the nuclear domain varies significantly.
Large Generation III+ nuclear power plants are already in operation, but the situation for SMRs
differs.
SMR designs using light water as a coolant share high readiness levels due to their similarity to
large plants. The lower Technology Readiness Levels (TRL) of SMRs are a results of design concepts
and choices that have not undergone the licensing process. For more advanced modular reactor
concepts, TRLs vary widely, requiring extensive validation of novel designs, fuels, and safety
systems before commercial readiness.
Numerous studies on nuclear energy production costs show a wide range of estimates for new plant
builds, but generally, these costs are competitive with those of many renewable energy sources.
There is much more consensus on the cost of the long-term operation of existing reactors, marking
it as one of the most cost-effective sources of low-carbon electricity.
Investment and funding
Research and innovation in nuclear safety are primarily funded by public sources, with funding
exceeding EUR 1 billion annually and steadily increasing over the past five years. In terms of
venture capital, Europe lags the US both in total funding amounts and timing. While early-stage
funding is growing in Europe, the US is shifting its focus to later-stage financing.
Value chain
The nuclear industry plays a significant role in the economy by providing approximately 500 000
jobs [11], both in energy production and throughout the nuclear supply chain. Its total economic
impact is estimated at around EUR 250 billion annually. Of this amount, approximately
EUR 190 billion is directly associated with energy production and the associated supply chain.
Sustainability
Nuclear energy performs well compared to other low-carbon energy sources across several
indicators, including greenhouse gas emissions, land use, water consumption, and pollution.
However, public discussions often focus on the impacts of radiation impacts, potential
consequences of accidents and waste management.
The average annual public radiation exposure from nuclear energy-based electricity is ten thousand
times lower than the dose from natural background radiation. The ‘Technical assessment of nuclear
energy with respect to the ‘do no significant harm’ criteria of Regulation (EU) 2020/852’ [12] finds
fatality rates from severe accidents are notably lower than those from fossil-fuel plants. It finds the
overall human health impact from radiological and non-radiological factors across the whole
nuclear chain is of the same order as that of other low-carbon energy sources.

EU positioning and global competitiveness
The nuclear market has been strongly nationally focused for a long time, and although supply
chains are now global, some traces of the past still linger. The European nuclear industry appears
competitive in the global marketplace. By omitting the import of raw materials, particularly natural
uranium as the fuel base, the nuclear industry has created a trade surplus of approximately
EUR 1 billion annually in recent years.
Europe's reliance on imported natural uranium is managed by the Euratom Supply Agency, which
aims to diversify supply sources and establish secure supply chains to ensure consistent and
reliable access while reducing dependence on a single supplier.

1. Introduction
1.1 Scope and context
This report on nuclear power is part of the annual series of reports from the Clean Energy
Technology Observatory (CETO). This report is the first CETO report on this topic. It provides an
overview of the current state of nuclear power including large scale nuclear power plants, small
modular and advanced modular reactors, and some of the aspects required for the deployment of
these technologies. The back end of the fuel cycle will not be addressed in detail in this edition,
neither will be Generation IV reactor designs.
Nuclear power can play a crucial role for achieving the climate neutrality objective and the
REPowerEU strategy. The EU has set ambitious targets for carbon neutrality, and nuclear power is
expected to contribute significantly to these efforts.
The report is organised into five main chapters. Chapter 2 examines the state of the art and future
developments of nuclear fission technology, focusing on advancements in technology readiness,
energy capacity, costs, and research funding. Chapter 3 focuses on the value chain analysis,
covering economic contributions, sustainability, and the role of EU companies in the market. Chapter
4 provides an overview of the EU's global position and competitiveness in the nuclear industry,
analysing market status, trade dynamics, and resource efficiency. Chapter 5 concludes the report by
synthesizing key findings and highlighting strategic opportunities and challenges.

2. Technology status and development trends
Approximately one quarter of the final energy consumed in the European Union comes from
electricity, and approximately two-thirds of the EU's electricity is generated from low-carbon
sources [13]. In 2024 the EU and Brazil were the only major electricity markets generating more
than 50% of their electricity from low-carbon sources, with the EU’s contribution exceeding 70%.
[14]
In terms of electricity production, nuclear power plants accounted for around 23.4% total electricity
generated in 2024 [15] . France had the highest share of nuclear power in its electricity mix, at
64.1%, followed by Slovakia at 57.8% and Hungary at 43.3% [16]. In 12 of EU Member States are
101 reactors operating [17]. These reactors have a combined capacity of approximately 98
gigawatts electric (GWe). Notably, over half of the EU's nuclear electricity is produced in France.
Most the EU's nuclear reactors are light water reactors, with 92 Pressurised Water Reactors (PWRs)2
and 7 Boiling Water Reactors (BWRs) in operation. Additionally, two heavy water moderated reactors
of the CANDU type, a design of a pressurised heavy water reactors (PWHR), are operating in
Romania.

These developments align with the forecasts of the Euratom Treaty's nuclear illustrative

existing units are expected to cease operations before 2050. According to the latest PINC report of
2025 [23], if lifespan extensions are assumed, the capacity of current reactors is projected to
decrease from approximately 98 GWe today to around 50 GWe by 2050. Adding new reactors,
which could contribute roughly 60 GWe, would potentially bring the total installed capacity to
approximately 109 GWe by 2050—an 11% increase compared to current levels. This capacity may
temporarily exceed 109 GWe if new reactors come online earlier than planned. By 2050, these
reactors are anticipated to supply about 12% of the European Union’s electricity generation. In
addition to traditional large-scale reactors, the scenario may be supplemented with SMRs. The
European SMR Industrial Alliance is working on setting out a strategic plan to achieve the first SMRs
in commercial operation in the early years of the next decade. In 2023, in the preparatory phase of
the European SMRs Industrial Alliance, a preliminary evaluation by the sector organisations resulted
in projections of SMR capacity ranging from 17 GWe to 53 GWe by 2050 [24] Such projections are
consistent with other more recent reports [25] [26].
Nuclear capacities anticipated in this 'base case' scenario will require investments of around
EUR 241 billion in present value terms. New-build large-scale reactors account for EUR 205 billion
of these investments, while lifetime extensions account for EUR 36 billion [23]. Thus, while the
actual achievement of lifespan extensions can significantly impact the installed capacity by 2050,
they represent only a minor share of the total investment needed. Additional investments are
needed in Small Modular Reactors (SMRs), Advanced Modular Reactors (AMRs), and microreactors
for the longer term.
Currently, six European Union member states - Bulgaria, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland,
Romania, and Sweden - are actively planning the construction or finalisation of new nuclear power
plants. Additionally, the Czech Republic, Poland, Slovakia, Slovenia, Sweden, Netherlands, Romania
and France have proposed new large nuclear power plant projects, the maturity of the plans vary.
Furthermore, several member states are exploring the deployment of SMRs, not only to generate
electricity but also to provide energy for non-electric applications such as process heat or district
heating. (Presently, several “conventional” nuclear power plants serve both as a source of electricity
and as a provider of direct heat). Some of the observed changes in the stance of governments
vis-à-vis nuclear energy have been triggered by energy security considerations after the start of the
Russian invasion in Ukraine.
Some of these geopolitical considerations were also considered in the report of the competitiveness
of Europe, i.e. the Draghi report [27]. Following the Draghi report to maximise the potential of
nuclear energy, the EU should focus on three key areas. Firstly, extending the lifespan of existing
reactors, provided that a robust safety case can be demonstrated, can help to maintain a low-
carbon energy supply and ensure that the EU's climate goals are met. Secondly, building new
reactors using established technologies can help to increase the EU's nuclear capacity, but it is
essential that costs and schedules are kept under control to make nuclear power a competitive
option.

Thirdly, the EU should prioritise the development and deployment of next-generation nuclear
reactors, such as SMRs. These innovative technologies have the potential to play a significant role in
integrated energy systems with high renewable penetration, providing flexible generation and
enhancing overall energy system efficiency. While the deployment of these new reactors is
expected to have a medium-term impact, with most plans in Europe scheduled to begin in the next
decade, they can help build a competitive technological supply chain in the EU and ensure the EU
remains a leader in the nuclear industry. By pursuing these three areas of action, the EU can unlock
the full potential of nuclear energy and ensure a secure, reliable, and low-carbon energy supply.
2.1. Long term operation
The long-term operation (LTO) of nuclear reactors is crucial for decarbonisation strategies, as 444
reactors worldwide providing a significant source of low-carbon electricity. As the nuclear industry
evolves, addressing technological challenges is essential to ensure the safe and efficient operation
of reactors beyond their initial design life.
Recent analysis has revealed that refurbishing existing nuclear facilities to extend their operational
lifespan can significantly lower costs compared to constructing new ones [28]. This approach, which
leverages existing infrastructure, yields cost savings even when the facilities are not operating at
full capacity, a scenario that may become more common as renewable energy sources become
increasingly prevalent. In fact, the costs associated with these refurbishments are often lower than
those of investing in other low-carbon energy technologies, even when those technologies have long
lifespans, such as hydroelectric power plants.
LTO is supported by a strong scientific foundation and extensive operating experience, enabling
ageing management, maintenance, refurbishment, and replacement of structures, systems, and
components (SSCs) where needed. Most components of a nuclear power plant can be replaced
during regular maintenance outages or LTO refurbishments, which also present opportunities for
safety upgrades and modernisations, such as replacing analogue equipment with digital systems to
enhance monitoring and control, or implementing power uprates to boost electricity generation
capacity.
However, challenges related to the ageing and obsolescence of SSCs need attention. As reactors
age, the risk of component failure increases, and the availability of spare parts and qualified
suppliers can become a concern. Integrating new technologies, such as digital systems and
advanced materials, also presents challenges in terms of compatibility and validation.
To overcome these challenges, the nuclear industry is developing advanced ageing management
programmes that involve continuous monitoring and assessment of reactor components. These
programmes also use advanced materials and technologies to mitigate the effects of ageing. For
instance, advanced non-destructive testing techniques help detect defects in components, while
coatings and surface treatments enhance corrosion resistance and reduce maintenance needs.
Already in 2008, a study published by the electric power research institute (EPRI) found that about
20% of identifiable plant equipment was obsolete [29], meaning it was no longer commercially
available for purchase. A report published by FORATOM in 2020 [30] suggests this figure is now
considerably higher. European nuclear fleet operators are particularly concerned about the
obsolescence of SSCs and their ability to replace them. Licensees operating large fleets of
standardised reactors and participating in international cooperative efforts face fewer concerns. The
JRC supply chain initiative [31] has highlighted the obsolescence and supply chain issues faced by
several licensees from various countries.

To support technological development, ongoing research in advanced materials, digitalisation, and
artificial intelligence is essential. International cooperation and knowledge sharing among nuclear
operators, regulators, and research institutions help develop best practices and standards for LTO.
Efforts are also being made to foster international collaboration for qualifying industrial equipment
from sectors with similar safety needs, such as oil, chemical, and aeronautics, for use in the nuclear
industry.
2.2. Power Plants – Generation III+
Most of European Union Member States planning to build large power plants are expected to deploy
Light Water Reactors (LWRs). The European Utility Requirements (EUR) organisation, composed of
European utility companies, aims to create common European positions on design requirements for
future Nuclear Power Plants (NPPs). This organisation was founded to promote harmonisation of
requirements toward standard bodies across Europe. Its main goal is to ensure NPP designs comply
with safety and technical requirements set by national nuclear authorities and to harmonise and
make design requirements more transparent, thus facilitating the entry of NPP vendors and designs
into the European market. A report submitted to the Directorate-General for Energy states that the
EUR organisation's criteria align with the latest recommendations from the Western European
Nuclear Regulators’ Association (WENRA) and the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) [32].
Currently focused on LWRs, the organisation plans to extend its reach to SMRs in the future.
For a reactor design to be considered for assessment, it must have the backing of both utility

A more detailed analysis of the cost structures of Asian and Western builds reveals that project
overruns are a significant factor in the increased financial cost of nuclear power plant construction
in the West. The escalation of indirect costs can be attributed to various factors, including changes
to designs during the construction phase, whether driven by evolving regulatory requirements or the
challenges inherent in first-of-a-kind designs [37]. Furthermore, weaknesses in the supply chain and
project management have also been identified as significant contributors to project overruns and
increased building costs [38].
The experience of recent European and US nuclear power projects offers valuable lessons, and a
closer examination of the European Pressurised Reactor (EPR) projects in Europe can provides some
insights. The EPR was conceived in the 1990s by a French-German partnership (Framatome/Areva
and Siemens) to combine the safety and cost-efficiency features of France's N4 and Germany's
Konvoi reactors. Although a design report was approved in 1997, progress stalled due to declining
public support for nuclear energy in both countries. In 2003, Areva and Siemens secured a
EUR 3 billion contract to build the first EPR at Finland's Olkiluoto, with construction commencing in
2005 despite incomplete design work and an unestablished supply chain. Similarly, EDF began
constructing a second EPR at Flamanville, France, in 2007, with less than 40% of the detailed
design finalised. These projects subsequently faced severe delays and cost overruns due to
immature designs, regulatory differences between Finland and France, and over 4,500 design
modifications.
Another aspect, highlighted, in an analysis of the Flamanville [39] construction, is the long absence
of new builds in France., and in the rest of Europe. In fact, no new reactor had been built for
approximately 2 decades, resulting in a loss of people, knowledge and capabilities across the supply
chain and even with regulators. This has affected not only project management and technical
expertise but also the skills of industrial component manufacturers, welders, and other players,
leading to a shortage of qualified personnel and a lack of technical monitoring. The supply chain
has been particularly impacted, with many companies experiencing a significant reduction in their
nuclear-related workload, leading to a decline in their expertise and capabilities.

2.3. Small modular reactors

2.3.1. History and Context
Small Modular Reactors are compact nuclear reactors with a capacity of less than 300 Megawatts
electric (MWe) and are designed in modular units. SMRs have a long history, especially in naval
applications. Approximately 100 nuclear reactors power 86 submarines and aircraft carriers today,
providing electricity, heat, and propulsion [44]. The U.S. Navy, for instance, has been refining SMR
technology for over 75 years, beginning with the USS Nautilus, the first nuclear-powered submarine,
launched in 1955. Many other countries, including the UK, France, Russia, and China, have developed
their nuclear fleets, showcasing extensive experience with modular reactors.
The main reasons they gained a lot of attention in recent years are their key advantages over
conventional large-scale nuclear power plants.
• Lower upfront investment costs:
SMRs require a lower upfront capital investment due to their smaller size. It is expected
that they will achieve cost efficiency through design simplification, factory fabrication,
and potential regulatory harmonisation, making their electricity costs competitive with
traditional reactors.
• Quicker deployment:
Traditional nuclear plants can take many years to become operational due to site-
specific designs and significant on-site labour. The aim is for SMRs to be primarily
manufactured in factories, minimising on-site labour, which could reduce deployment
timelines.
• Siting flexibility and land efficiency
Their compact size and modular design provide greater siting flexibility compared to
traditional reactors. SMRs might be integrated into conventional industrial sites to
supply process heat or produce industrial gases, such as hydrogen, effectively utilising
land.
Several different SMR technologies are envisaged, ranging from technologies already employed in
large nuclear reactors, to more innovative designs, e.g. Generation IV nuclear reactors. Therefore,
there is a significant variation in technology readiness levels, with more mature light water
concepts, gas-cooled, and sodium-cooled concepts which have been built before, and thus at the
higher end of the range, while some of the more advanced concepts, such as liquid lead or molten
salt reactors, are at a lower level.

• Boiling water reactor (BWR)
Smaller, modular versions of traditional reactors using water as a coolant and neutron
moderator. The technology is well established, with licensing, fabrication, and supply
chain close to demand of existing power plants.

21

• Pressurised water reactor (PWR)
Smaller, modular versions of traditional reactors using water as a coolant and neutron
moderator. The technology is well established, with licensing, fabrication, and supply
chain close to the demand of existing power plants.
• Integral pressurised water reactor (iPWR)
This is a self-contained design integrating all necessary components into a single
vessel, offering enhanced safety and simplified operation. The technology is similar to
classical PWRs, but due to integrated designs, some developments of modelling tools
for the licensing process, as well as new fabrication, and supply chains will have to be
established.
• High-temperature gas reactor (HTGR)
These use helium gas as a coolant and graphite as a moderator, suitable for industrial
high-temperature applications. Demonstrators for this technology are being operated in
China (HTR-10 and HTR-PM, 2 HTR-PM600 under construction) and in Japan (HTTR);
other designs in the west are still in the design phase (e.g. Xe-100 or KRONOS in the
US). In Europe, although the technology’s original home, the supply chain for fuel,
materials and components needs to be revived.
• Liquid metal-cooled reactor (LMR)
Liquid metals such as sodium and lead are utilised as coolants, offering potential for
both electricity generation and nuclear propulsion. While these are often grouped
together, they represent two distinct concepts currently under consideration.
For sodium-cooled fast reactors, there is significant experience in Europe, particularly
with the operation of reactors like Phénix and Superphénix. Although the technology is
well-established, further research and advancements in materials may be necessary.
In the case of liquid lead-cooled reactors, the technological situation in Western Europe
is similar, with ongoing material research required. However, unlike sodium-cooled
reactors, there is little operational experience outside of Russia.
For both concepts, the nuclear fuel typically used is Mixed Oxide (MOX) fuel, which
contains a higher concentration of plutonium than in Light Water Reactors (LWRs).
Currently, production capacities are not sufficient for large-scale deployment, and the
available plutonium stock is very limited.
• Molten salt reactor (MSR)
With the use of liquid fuel, generally a mixture of fluoride or chloride salts serving as
both fuel and coolant, these reactors are ideally suited for operations at relatively high
temperatures. Yet, several crucial challenges must be addressed before MSRs can be
deployed on a wide scale. These challenges mainly concern safety, fuel management
(including online processing if necessary), and material compatibility, which involve the
establishment of standards for design safety, managing the transportation of fuel salt,
building dependable supply chains for specialised components, and conducting in-depth
analyses of unique accident scenarios. Additionally, important research areas include
understanding and managing the long-term behaviour of fuel salt under irradiation and
ensuring effective radionuclide retention.

2.3.3.1. CityHeat Project
Led by Calogena
3 and Steady Energy4, this project focuses on using small modular reactors (SMRs)
for urban heating solutions, aiming to deliver sustainable heating options and reduce carbon
footprints in city environments [65]. Both companies are European startups. Calogena is a French
company founded by former nuclear industry executives, while Steady Energy is a spin-out of VTT,
Technical Research Centre of Finland.
Both companies are developing reactor designs primarily for district heating applications, targeting
water temperatures around 120°C. Steady Energy aims for a thermal power output of 50 MW, while
Calogena targets 30 MW. The design philosophies for both reactors are quite similar: they utilise a
PWR core, enclosed in a double shell with natural convection for core cooling, located inside a pool.
The primary circuit is fully enclosed within the pressure vessel and uses 'clean' water, which means
boron is excluded for reactivity control. Due to lower operating temperatures than those of
conventional PWRs used for electricity production, the operating pressures are well below 1 MPa,
resulting in reduced pressure vessel wall thickness and less stringent engineering constraints.
The reactors are intended to use conventional low enriched uranium fuels, similar to those used in
traditional PWRs, with a refuelling cycle of approximately 2 years. Due to the low energy density
and robust design, significant fission gas release from the nuclear fuels is not expected even under
accidental conditions. Additionally, the decay heat after shutdown is significantly below 1 MWth,
allowing for passive cooling through natural convection in the pool during this phase. The inclusion

2.3.3.7. EAGLES
EAGLES is a 300 MWe lead-cooled fast reactor. The consortium, including Ansaldo Nucleare,

2.3.3.9. Thorizon One Project
Led by Thorizon, a Dutch-French nuclear startup, this project explores the use of thorium-based fuel
cycles in SMRs, which could offer a more abundant and less waste-intensive nuclear energy option.
The reactor's first stage is designed to burn plutonium and minor actinides, with further fuel
developments including the potential to breed uranium from thorium.
This project is built around the patented concept of nuclear fuel cartridges [77]. Unlike traditional
molten salt reactors, where the fuel/coolant mixture cycles in and out of the reactor vessel, this
design uses individually sealed cartridges. The core salt volume is divided into subcritical
assemblies, specifically using closed and contained cartridges, which prevent salt from entering or
leaving and maintain low primary pressures under all circumstances. The reactor relies on pump
action to remain critical, and if the pump fails, the core will automatically drain into a subcritical
configuration. The design features double cartridge containment and primary containment integrity
monitoring, along with passive decay heat removal from the cartridges' outer surfaces during
station blackout scenarios.
Fuel development is conducted within a consortium that includes ORANO and Stellaria, a French
MSR concept. Presently, the company is working with partners and regulators to complete the
design phase and evaluate possible sites, aiming to start construction by 2030.

2.6. Technology costs
The cost of electricity from different power sources can be divided into three main categories for a
fair comparison: (1) the upfront cost of building the plant (capital costs), (2) the ongoing fixed costs
of running it, such as maintenance and salaries (fixed O&M), and (3) the variable costs that change
with the amount of electricity produced such as fuel costs, which for nuclear energy typically
account for all costs in the front-end and the back-end of the fuel cycle.
Nuclear energy is characterised by high initial construction expenses. For a new nuclear plant, the
upfront construction cost can even exceed 80% of the total energy cost [79]. The remaining
expenses are allocated between operations and maintenance and fuel costs, with fuel cost typically
accounting for 5-10% of the total costs [79]. Typically, more than 2/3 of the fuel costs cover the
front-end, i.e. mining, milling, conversion, enrichment and fuel production, and less than 1/3 the
back end, i.e. from the moment the fuel is unloaded from the core until disposal, including
construction of the final repository. The given percentages vary depending on factors such as
interest rates, construction delays, and the specifics of engineering contracts. It has been reported
[80], that the choice of back-end strategy, direct disposal, partial recycling in a LWR, or multiple
plutonium recycle in LWR and fast reactors, has only a minor effect on the magnitude of the fuel
costs. Finally decommissioning costs, while depending on assumed interest rates, are in general well
below 1% [81].
In comparison to gas turbines, nuclear power has significantly lower fuel costs, but its capital costs
are up to six times higher, strongly depending on interest rate and capacity factor assumptions.

33

This, in addition to their flexibility to accommodate quickly large load changes, gas fired power
plants are broadly used to adapt to fluctuating electricity demand, while nuclear power plants,
which can accommodate load changes in a more limited way, are generally preferred for
maintaining a consistent output.
Typically, the Levelised Cost of Electricity (LCOE) is used to compare different electricity production
methods. However, as various technologies offer different services and roles within the energy
system, their outputs can impose varying overall costs beyond the plant-level LCOE. This means
that while LCOE provides a useful baseline for comparing costs, it does not fully capture the
broader economic impacts and system-level costs associated with integrating different types of
energy technologies (see e.g. [81]). Additional components that should be considered include:
• The extra costs of managing a grid with variable power sources, such as wind and solar.
• The timing of when electricity is produced, which affects its value.
• Harmful effects like carbon emissions and pollution (externalities).
• Strategic considerations, such as minimizing reliance on a single player for imports
Consideration of these factors provides a more comprehensive understanding of the true costs and
impacts of different energy technologies.
System integration challenges have been addressed in the nuclear illustrative programme [23].
Evidence suggests that, although nuclear energy involves significant initial capital costs, its
deployment can reduce overall system expenses by minimising the need for investments in
transmission, distribution, and storage infrastructure. This is supported by studies across the EU [26]
and France [82]. A Polish case study shows [83] that scenarios incorporating nuclear energy
generally result in lower total system costs. However, it is important to note that the effectiveness
of any solution is highly context-specific, requiring careful consideration of regional energy markets,
infrastructure needs, and policy objectives [84].

2.6.1. Levelised cost of electricity
The Levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) for nuclear power is primarily driven by construction-related
capital costs. These costs are highly sensitive to factors such as assumed discount rates and
specific building expenses for nuclear facilities. The limited number of new construction projects,
coupled with cost overruns due to various factors previously discussed, makes it challenging to
reliably estimate future costs. As a result, the LCOE for nuclear power is highly influenced by the
assumptions and parameters used, leading to significant variation in reported LCOE figures across
studies.
For new nuclear constructions, estimates of the LCOE vary widely. One source indicates a range of
40-100 USD/MWh [81], using data from eight builds worldwide. Another report [85] suggests an
LCOE ranging from 80-100 £/MWh, based on data from [86] and projected construction costs.
Meanwhile, another estimate suggests a range of 140-220 USD/MWh [79],
 using data from
Vogtle 3 and assuming a 30% cost reduction for Vogtle 4 due to anticipated learning and
efficiencies. The main factors for the LCOE of nuclear new builds are capacity factor and capital
costs, i.e. the combination of overnight costs, capacity factor and discount rate. While for an
individual study applied discount rates and capacity factors determine the ranges given, differences
between studies often are caused by assumptions on overnight costs. Typically, variation in fuels

34
costs – including costs for waste disposal - induce changes hardly exceeding a few percent, i.e. far
within the given ranges.
SMRs are expected to have LCOE figures within a similar range [87]. However, the economic
parameters for SMRs could be positively affected by their capacity for cogenerating heat. Whether
these benefits can be realised may depend on several factors including the regulatory framework,
factory manufacturing processes, and modularity, among others. Furthermore, reliable estimates for
building costs and the extent of cost reductions achievable between demonstrator models and full-
scale industrial production remain areas of significant uncertainty and debate.
In contrast, the long-term operation (LTO) of existing nuclear facilities incurs much lower costs. This
is because the investment required for upgrades is significantly lower, and the original construction
costs have already been amortised. Typically, the LCOE for LTO is estimated to be around 30
USD/MWh, with much greater consistency across different studies.

2.6.2. System costs
System costs can be divided into three main categories: utilisation costs, balancing costs, and grid
costs. Grid costs reflect the impact on transmission and distribution networks due to location
constraints. Connecting geographically constrained production sites to areas where electricity is
needed may require additional infrastructure, such as building new lines or reinforcing existing ones.
This is especially necessary when transporting electricity over long distances, which increases
losses.
Utilisation costs are primarily associated with variable energy sources like solar and wind, whose
output depends on weather conditions and time of day. This variability leads to a concentration of
output that can reduce the market value of each megawatt-hour (MWh) produced. At high levels of
variable renewable energy (VRE) penetration, multiple plants may generate power simultaneously,
further decreasing the electricity's market value
Balancing costs arise from the need to adjust the system more frequently to balance supply and
demand due to the uncertainty and variability in power production from VRE sources. The system
requires greater reliance on spinning reserves, and conventional power plants must cycle and ramp
their operations more frequently, leading to inefficiencies and higher overall costs.

2.6.3. External costs
Following the methodology described in report [89], there are five major components contributing to
the external costs of electricity production. For nuclear energy, the overall external costs are
relatively low due to minimal greenhouse gas emissions, limited land use, and low chemical
involvement throughout the process. The highest external costs for nuclear energy stem from
uranium use and related mining activities. These costs could be reduced by utilising fast reactors
and transitioning from a once-through to a recycling scheme. However, in the latter case, it would
be essential to rigorously estimate the external costs associated with chemical usage and potential
radioactive risks in reprocessing plants.

The SET Plan, established in 2007, aims to advance the EU's energy and climate objectives by
positioning Europe as a global leader in low-carbon energy and energy efficiency technologies. It
aligns with the fifth dimension of the Energy Union: 'Research, Innovation, and Competitiveness.'

To advance nuclear safety within the SET Plan, several essential actions and outcomes have been

3. Value chain analysis
This chapter provides an overview of the nuclear industry within the European Union, structured
around key components of the front end of the nuclear value chain—from uranium mining and fuel
processing to reactor technology, a detailed analysis of the back end of the fuel cycle is not in
scope of this edition. It also describes factors enabling the deployment of nuclear energy.
3.1. Nuclear Fuel
Under the Euratom Treaty, the supply of nuclear materials to Community users, for all civilian uses,
is ensured by a common supply policy, based on the principle of equal access to sources of supply.
The Euratom Supply Agency, established as a separate entity and endowed with specific
prerogatives by the Treaty, takes responsibility, under the supervision of the Commission, for the
common policy and aims to ensure that, as per the Treaty, all Community users receive a regular
and equitable supply of nuclear materials and fuel.
By using its powers (notably, its exclusive right to conclude contracts for the supply of nuclear
materials both from inside and outside the Community) in accordance with its statutory mission, by
deploying its market-monitoring role and by providing information, expertise and advice on any
subject related to the nuclear market, the Agency fosters the security of supply of nuclear materials
and services for European users, including in the medium and long term and strongly supports the
overall EU policies to that end – such as the European Energy Security Strategy and, most recently,
the REPowerEU Communication and Roadmap.
Despite the limited availability of natural resources for uranium extraction in the territories of its
Member States, the Community, taking advantage also of the possibilities offered by the Euratom
Treaty, has developed a significant nuclear industry. This includes a robust infrastructure, high-level
expertise and capacities for the provision of services (including uranium enrichment, the fabrication
of fuel assemblies and reprocessing) throughout the whole nuclear fuel cycle. By focusing on these
capabilities, Europe can maintain a degree of energy independence, even with limited natural
uranium resources.
In the following chapters, we will delve into more detailed discussions of processing facilities
available in Europe, as well as the strategies in place to ensure steady, reliable nuclear fuel
supplies. The next sub-chapters will rely extensively on data from the European Supply Agency’s
annual report and the staff working documents of the nuclear indicative programme. All data
sourced from external references will be clearly attributed to their original sources.
3.1.1. Uranium Mining and Supply
Between 2020 and 2023, uranium exploration and mine development activities in the European
Union accounted for only 0.1% or less of global expenditure in this sector [97]. The primary focus
has been on the remediation of closed uranium mines. Several potential mining projects have been
blocked by local jurisdictions—such as those in Denmark (Greenland) and Hungary—or due to safety
concerns, as seen in Spain, and uranium resources within EU territory remain largely unexploited. As
a result, uranium production within the EU currently remains minimal, with only small quantities
extracted as a by-product of remediation activities (e.g., in the Czech Republic), though these
amounts are declining. However, some European companies are actively engaged in international
uranium mining activities, demonstrating some access to the necessary resource base.

Beginning of November 2025, Sweden has opted to lift the ban on uranium mining, effective
January 2026. Meanwhile, Finland is developing a project for uranium recovery by extracting it as

Radiation and Safety Authority granted permission to initiate extraction. Once full operation is
achieved in 2026, it is anticipated that the production capacity will reach 200 tonnes of

3.1.3. Enrichment
Uranium enrichment is a crucial step in the nuclear fuel cycle, as it increases the concentration of
the fissile isotope U-235 to a level suitable for use in most nuclear reactors. Natural uranium
contains only about 0.7% U-235, requiring enrichment to around 5% to meet the needs of the
LWRs currently prevailing in the EU.
Urenco, with enrichment plants in the EU, plans to expand capacity to approximately 31 500 tonnes
of separative work units (tSWU) per year by 2040. The largest global enricher, Rosatom/TVEL from
Russia, currently has a capacity of 27 100 tSWU per year. Brazil's INB and Japan's JNFL have a
combined capacity of under 200 tSWU annually, with Japan's Rokkasho-mura plant currently
inactive. Both countries plan modest capacity increases before 2030.
Increased geopolitical tensions and resulting shifts in global energy markets have highlighted the
need for strategic self-sufficiency in enrichment services, catalysing market changes. Although
enrichment service deliveries to EU utilities increased by 12% in 2023 compared to 2022, there has

3.1.5. Nuclear Technology
The construction and operation of a nuclear power plant involve several key cost components that
contribute to the overall capital expenditure. Construction costs typically account for 40-45% of the
total capital investment, ranging from approximately USD 1 billion to USD 3.8 billion. These
expenses encompass site development, design, architecture, and engineering. In regions with limited
local expertise, foreign Engineering, Procurement, and Construction (EPC) companies often manage
75-80% of the construction work, while local firms contribute 20-25%, primarily through on-site
labour and project management. Costs can vary based on factors such as labour availability,
construction standards, and site-specific conditions, particularly in areas with high living costs or
significant environmental requirements.
Equipment costs constitute 50-55% of the total capital expenses, ranging from USD 1.3 billion to
USD 4.6 billion. These costs are distributed among the plant's primary circuits. The Primary Circuit,
or Nuclear Island, which includes critical components such as the reactor pressure vessel and steam
generators, accounts for about 25% of the equipment costs and must adhere to strict safety
standards. The Secondary Circuit, or Turbine Island which converts thermal energy into electricity,
also represents roughly 25% of these costs. The Balance of Plant (BoP), which includes essential
systems such as cooling towers and HVAC, accounts for 25-30% of the equipment costs. Finally, the
Switchyard and Grid Connection, which are crucial for integrating electricity into the grid, comprise
10-25% of equipment costs. Additionally, other costs such as transportation, commissioning, and
fuel load contribute 5-10% of the total capital cost, ensuring the plant's operational readiness once
construction is complete.
The nuclear supply chain is structured in tiers. At the top (Tier 1), contractors like EPC firms or
technology vendors deliver the Nuclear Steam Supply System (NSSS) or the entire Nuclear Power
Plant (NPP) in turnkey projects. Tier 2 consists of system integrators, which may include reactor
vendors. Tier 3 features original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) providing major components like
steam generators and reactor pressure vessels (RPVs), relying on sub-components from Tier 4
suppliers. Tier 5 involves processors and fabricators of specialised materials, while Tier 6 supplies
raw materials. Vertically integrated companies may streamline this process by handling more
production in-house, reducing subcontracting needs. This tiered structure reflects the
interdependence and complexity of the nuclear supply chain, which demands specialised
manufacturing and technical expertise. Barriers to market entry rise with specialisation, leading to
fewer participants. This can lead to a vertically segmented supply chain, with a low number of
suppliers and each layer, increasing risk of double marginalisation, i.e. a stacking of cost mark-ups
at various stages of the supply chain leading to a final price exceeding what a vertically integrated
monopoly would charge, therefore a vertically integrated supply chain could in theory reduce this
risk. New nuclear projects focus on how Tier 1 vendors manage their supply chains, while operating
plants deal directly with Tier 3 and below for spare parts. Decisions made by technology vendors
impact the entire supply chain throughout a plant’s lifecycle.

52

In Europe, France is the only country with a fully integrated nuclear value chain, capable of
supplying almost all necessary components for a nuclear construction project. Following France are
countries like Germany, which possess extensive experience and capability in nuclear energy. The
next tier in Europe includes countries such as Italy and Spain, which have some advanced elements
of the nuclear value chain elements. Italy primarily exports, as no nuclear power plants are currently
in operation in the country.
Historically focussed on domestic markets, the nuclear industry now benefits from increased global
competition. However, domestic supplier preferences, supported by regulatory and technical
barriers, particularly for the NSSS, persist in several countries.
3.1.5.1. Nuclear Island
The Primary Circuit, also known as the Nuclear Island, is a crucial component of a nuclear power
plant, responsible for housing and controlling the nuclear reaction. For a PWR, the most common
reactor type presently operating in Europe, it functions as a closed-loop system, enclosing a coolant
that can become radioactive during operations to efficiently transfer heat from the reactor core for
steam generation. Key components of the Primary Circuit include the reactor pressure vessel, which
holds the reactor core; steam generators that use the heat from the reactor to produce steam; and
coolant pumps that circulate the coolant to regulate temperature effectively.
Among the most critical components in modern nuclear power plants are large-scale or heavy
forgings, which are essential for constructing key structures such as containment vessels, reactor
vessels, turbine rotors, and steam generators. A single large reactor can require hundreds of these
forgings, totalling over 4 000 tons of steel, with individual pieces weighing between 500 and 600
tons. The production of such massive components relies on a limited number of ultra-large forging
presses capable of processing these enormous hot steel ingots. While the largest number of heavy
forges are in Russia and Asia, within the European Union, Germany, France, and Italy have large
forges operating. In Europe only one can produce most of the large size requirements for large-
scale nuclear power plants. Further investments are ongoing to ensure that all parts of a large EPR
and EPR2 can be produced domestically [102].
In the past, a significant number of suppliers of forged reactor pressure vessels existed in the EU
(such as Klöckner-Werke, Kraftwerke Union, the Rotterdam Dry Dock Company, Škoda, and
Uddcomb, which also fabricated large RPVs). Additionally, ENSA of Spain has been involved in the
recent past in the production of large RPV, using forgings from outside sources. Forging smaller
RPVs as required for most SMRs is comparatively more straightforward, and more suppliers are
available. This leads to more competition, thus in principle shortening delivery times, accelerating
deployment, and reducing costs.
Worldwide, the number of active steam generator providers for PWRs is larger than for pressure
vessels. Similarly, a larger number of providers is in Russia and Asia. However, within Europe,
significant suppliers and experience exist in a small number of countries—France, Italy, Germany,
Spain, and the Czech Republic.
Original equipment materials are a major concern not only for new builds but also for the long-term
operation of existing power plants. Over the last few decades, the business environment for the
nuclear industry in Europe has slowed down for most due to a lack of new builds. This has led some
nuclear suppliers diversifying and seeking new industrial markets, with some opting to leave the
nuclear market altogether. This trend is apparent among European nuclear operators who are

53

witnessing a declining number of appropriately qualified nuclear suppliers as several companies exit
the market.
For example, the number of equipment supplier companies holding ASME N-Type certificates has
declined in both Europe and North America, with an observed decrease of around 5% per year in the
period 2013-2020 [103]. Although not all nuclear suppliers are ASME certified, this trend suggests
that the nuclear-grade supply chain is shrinking.
A 2017 study commissioned by Finnish utility TVO [104] found that the quality assurance (QA) and
quality management (QM) requirements for safety-related systems, structures, and components
(SSCs) in Finland's nuclear facilities are nearly equivalent to those in the oil and gas industry.
Consequently, high-quality and reliable SSCs can be achieved using well-established non-nuclear
industry standards, such as those from the American Petroleum Institute (API), International
Electrotechnical Commission (IEC), and American National Standards Institute (ANSI)/International
Society of Automation (ISA). Companies that adhere to these standards may be encouraged to enter
the nuclear market if a sufficiently large number of reactors are constructed.
The European SMR pre-Partnership Workstream 4 – Supply chain adaption [105] sought to identify
potential suppliers and assess their status in Europe. While the overall findings were positive, the
study highlighted potential bottlenecks in the supply chain. It found that certain sectors, such as
nuclear-grade pumps and valves, may face a constrained supply chain in Europe due to stringent
quality requirements, especially for body castings and forgings. The generator/turbo generator
sector could also face challenges due to a restricted market. However, this may be mitigated in the
context of SMRs, as their power range allows more suppliers to comply without significant
additional effort. Additionally, critical activities like the production of control rods, reactor cores,
grids, fuel elements, and criticality monitors/systems might have limited suppliers.
Similar to the findings of the 2017 Finnish study, it was concluded that products and skills from
other technologically advanced sectors could be utilised in the nuclear energy supply chain,
including items like pumps and valves under the concept of "Commercial Grade Dedication." The
smaller overall size of SMRs will enable a broader range of suppliers to compete for the necessary
scope of work, as smaller forgings will allow more forges and steelworks to participate in the SMR
supply chain. This could help mitigate some supply chain concerns and create opportunities for new
suppliers to enter the market. Supporting this view, a study of the Italian nuclear supply chain [106]
estimated that it could provide up to nine reactor pressure vessels for SMRs per year.
3.1.5.2. Turbine Island and Balance of Plant
The Secondary Circuit, or Turbine Island, plays a crucial role in a nuclear power plant by using heat
from the Primary Circuit to produce steam, which drives turbines connected to generators for
electricity production. In PWRs, this circuit is designed to strongly limit the possibility of radioactive
contamination as the steam should be generated through heat transfer without direct contact with
the reactor coolant. The key components of this circuit include turbines, which convert thermal
energy into mechanical energy; generators, which then convert mechanical energy into electrical
energy; and condensers, which condense the steam back into water for reuse in the cycle.
The BoP comprises the supporting and auxiliary systems necessary for the plant's operation,
ensuring the smooth functioning of processes beyond the reactor core. This includes systems for
emergency power supplies, emergency cooling, safety measures, and a tertiary cooling circuit that
removes heat from the Secondary Circuit, often dissipating it through cooling towers.

54

The Switchyard and Grid Connection represent the final stage of the power generation process,
where electricity is transferred from the plant to the grid for public and industrial use. This stage
involves managing the transmission of power from the generator and ensuring it is converted to the
appropriate voltage levels for the electrical grid. Key components include transformers, which adjust
voltage levels; switchgear, which controls the flow of electricity and protects circuits from overloads;
and transmission lines, which deliver electricity from the switchyard to the broader electrical grid.
In Europe, certain countries demonstrate significant expertise in areas related to nuclear activities,
particularly mechanical engineering that is applicable to the nuclear sector, though not exclusively
nuclear-specific. France, Germany, and Italy are leaders in this field, showcasing substantial
experience and projects. Additionally, the Czech Republic and Sweden are actively engaged in similar
endeavours, focusing on mechanical engineering that supports the nuclear industry.
3.1.6. Nuclear Services
The nuclear industry involves a comprehensive range of services essential at various stages of a
nuclear facility's lifecycle. Beginning with the supply chain, services include supplier assessments,
quality audits, material and equipment testing, and non-destructive testing to ensure compliance
with the highest standards. During construction and commissioning, services extend to civil work
supervision, mechanical and electrical system testing, welding process qualifications, and rigorous
safety coordination, all aimed at minimising risks and ensuring projects progress smoothly and
within budget.
In the operational phase, facilities regularly undergo planned outages for refuelling, maintenance
and upgrades. Specialised services during these times focus on minimising downtime and
maintaining regulatory compliance through in-service inspections, statutory inspections, health and
safety audits, and energy management. Additionally, careful planning and execution of maintenance
tasks ensure the longevity and reliability of nuclear operations.
As facilities move towards decommissioning, specialised services ensure safe and efficient
dismantling, reducing residual radioactivity to levels that allow for the release of property. This
involves practical radiation protection measures, comprehensive nuclide analysis, waste
management, and strategic handling of nuclear fuel storage. In parallel, comprehensive training
programmes equip personnel with the necessary technical skills and qualifications, ensuring a
skilled workforce.
The nuclear industry relies on specialised transport companies to safely move radioactive materials,
and that's especially true for spent nuclear fuel, in compliance with relevant EU directives [107].
Currently, there are over 20 million shipments of radioactive materials annually, this number covers
also materials for e.g. nuclear medical applications. Transport remains a critical enabler of nuclear
energy, both in countries with nuclear energy and those embarking on new nuclear energy
programmes.
3.1.7. Licensing Capabilities
In Europe, protection of the health of the population (including the workers) against the dangers
arising from ionising radiations is, pursuant to the Euratom Treaty, a competence of the Community.
The Community’s duty is to set and regularly review the relevant basic safety standards [108],
aligning them to the highest level globally (in due account of the IAEA recommendations), while its
Member States must transpose them in their internal legal and administrative orders.

55

As the Court of Justice of the EU found, however, there is a significant overlap between radiation
protection and the safety of nuclear installations, and therefore nuclear safety is also a Community
competence. The Nuclear Safety Directive 2009/71/EURATOM [109], and its amendment [110],
creates a framework in which independent national regulatory authorities are tasked with
overseeing licensing, enforcing safety requirements, and conducting inspections for nuclear
installations. Member States must conduct periodic self-assessments of their national framework
and competent regulatory authorities and invite an international peer review of relevant aspects of
their national framework and/or authorities. The results of any peer review must be reported to the
Member States and the Commission.
Smooth and rapid development of SMRs within the Community would call for more harmonisation
of the applicable technical standards, as well as in the practices of the licensing authorities. This
could be the way forward, provided that the approach fully respects the Community competence in
the field of nuclear safety, as well as the applicable Community legal framework.
An OECD-NEA report suggests, indeed that greater global harmonisation could resolve some issues.
By aligning technical standards across borders, while abiding by the Euratom acquis, regulators
could reduce duplication, expedite assessments, and enhance investor confidence. Within the
European Union, national regulatory authorities have been working towards these goals. Interaction
between the nuclear industry and regulatory bodies on new developments can provide input to
setting new Euratom rules, and if necessary, address design constraints early in the process. To
strengthen such process the European Nuclear Safety Regulators Group (ENSREG), an expert
advisory body to the European Commission, consisting of officials from national nuclear safety
authorities across EU Member States has established a Task Force on Small Modular Reactors. This
Task Force aims to gather lessons and best practices, assess specific SMR designs, interact with the
European Industrial Alliance on SMRs, and identify regulatory challenges to be addressed.
Another consideration is the challenge posed by transporting prefabricated SMRs, including their
fuel, which may challenge the regulatory framework. Within the Alliance, Member State authorities
are collaborating to propose responses to challenges posed by new reactor designs and
unconventional fuels to current regulations.
Some of the new designs and unconventional fuels can present challenges in determining the most
appropriate safeguards approach and methodology. For this reason, early communication and
collaboration between designers and the safeguards research community are both essential and
already taking place. This is particularly important for concepts that incorporate 'safeguards by
design,' where safeguards practices and requirements are best integrated from the earliest stages
of the design process as proactively embedding safeguards ensures alignment with regulatory
expectations and enhances the feasibility of future verification and monitoring efforts.
3.1.8. Waste management
Under the Radioactive Waste and Spent Fuel Management Directive (2011/70/EURATOM) [111],
each member state is required to establish a national strategy for managing spent nuclear fuel and
radioactive waste. These obligations are rooted in the Euratom Treaty. Member states must develop
and carry out national programmes to ensure the safe and responsible oversight of these materials.
Inventory data on radioactive waste and spent fuel within the EU is published periodically, in line
with the Euratom framework for waste management.
Radioactive waste production is a universal concern across all EU countries, with 17 member states
also managing spent fuel within their borders. Given the radiological properties and potential risks

56

of these materials, their safe management is essential throughout all phases of the nuclear
lifecycle. This material must be regulated as radioactive waste under the appropriate legislative and
regulatory frameworks by relevant authorities.
Annually, the EU generates approximately 40 000 cubic meters of radioactive waste and around
1 000 tonnes of heavy metal in spent nuclear fuel, corresponding to an electricity supply of 620
TWh, based on 2023 data. Of this, over 90% is categorised as very-low-level or low-level waste,
while high-level waste comprises 0.2% [23]. The rest is classified as intermediate-level waste.
Progress has been made in safely disposing of very-low-level and low-level waste in the EU and
regarding final repositories, for spent nuclear fuel, Finland and Sweden have advanced farthest.
Currently, the Finnish regulator has a deadline of 31 December 2025 to complete its assessment of
the license application [112]. In Sweden, an agreement for the construction of an underground final
repository for spent nuclear fuel was signed in early 2025, with disposal planned to start in the
2030s [113].

3.3. Environmental and socio-economic sustainability
To summarize some findings from the analysis of the environmental impacts of nuclear power
compared to other electricity generation technologies [12]:
• Nuclear energy's average lifecycle greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions for electricity
production are on par with those of hydropower and wind.
• Emissions of nitrous oxides (NOx), sulphur dioxide (SO2), particulate matter (PM), and non-
methane volatile organic compounds (NMVOC) from nuclear energy are very low,
comparable to or even better than emissions from solar photovoltaic (PV) and wind energy.
• Concerning acidification and eutrophication potentials, nuclear energy performs similarly to
or better than solar PV and wind.
• In terms of freshwater and marine eco-toxicity, as well as ozone depletion and
photochemical oxidant creation potential, nuclear energy compares favourably to solar PV
and wind.
• The land required for nuclear energy generation is roughly the same as that needed for a
gas-fired power plant with equivalent capacity. It is significantly less than that required for
wind or solar PV installations.

In examining the areas requiring special attention when utilizing nuclear energy, several key issues
have been identified:
Thermal Pollution of Freshwater Bodies: Large inland nuclear power plants that use once-through
cooling systems withdraw a substantial amount of water from rivers or lakes for normal operations.
The return of heated cooling water can cause significant thermal pollution, requiring careful
management. Strict controls must be in place to monitor the maximum discharge temperature of
the condenser cooling water and the resultant temperature of the freshwater body after mixing.
Thorough analysis of water withdrawal options and thermal pollution risks should be integral to the
site selection process.
Water Consumption: Power plants that use a thermal cycle to convert heat to mechanical energy
require continuous cooling. While once-through cooling involves low water consumption,
technologies such as recirculation cooling, evaporative cooling towers, or pond cooling consume
considerable amounts of water due to evaporation. Water usage for these cooling methods is
comparable to that of concentrating solar power and coal. Careful assessment of water resource
availability and the potential environmental impacts of high-water consumption is essential during
site selection to ensure the implementation of optimal solutions.
The discussions on the impact of nuclear energy on human health and the environment, along with
the potential consequences of severe accidents, yield several important findings:
The average annual radiation exposure to the public from nuclear energy-based electricity
generation is approximately 0.2 microsieverts. This level is ten thousand times lower than the
average annual dose from natural background radiation.
In terms of public exposure in the event of accidents, the fatality rates from severe accidents in
current Western Gen II nuclear power plants are around 5x10
-7 fatalities per GWh. This rate is
significantly lower than that of any fossil fuel-based electricity production and is comparable to

58

hydropower in OECD countries and to wind power, with only solar power having a markedly lower
fatality rate.
According to Life Cycle Impact Analysis studies, the overall human health impact from both
radiological and non-radiological emissions in the nuclear energy chain is on par with the impacts
from offshore wind energy.
Overall, while nuclear energy presents some potential risks to human health and the environment,
implementing specific safety measures—such as careful site selection, appropriate facility design
and construction, and strict operational and waste management practices—ensures that these risks
remain within established regulatory limits. Impacts during key lifecycle phases, including mining
and milling, nuclear power plant operation, and others, are addressed through rigorous regulatory
and legislative compliance.
3.4. Role of EU companies
The European nuclear industry is an intricate and multifaceted sector, encompassing a variety of
specialised fields, such as reactor design and construction, fuel production and enrichment,
reprocessing and waste management. Its robust value chain is supported by interdependent areas
such as reactor technology, fuel cycle services, component manufacturing, digital solutions,
decommissioning, and nuclear research. A diverse network of companies, research institutions, and
technological innovators underpins the industry's capabilities, particularly in tackling the pressing
challenges of decarbonisation and energy security.
While large corporations often take centre stage in discussions about the nuclear sector, the
contributions of smaller, niche players are equally vital. These enterprises bring essential expertise
and innovation to the table, ensuring the industry remains cohesive and dynamic. This report
highlights some of the more prominent companies active in the sector, yet it remains mindful of the
indispensable role played by the lesser-known entities which, together, help maintain the innovative
spirit of Europe's nuclear industry.
1. Électricité de France (EDF) is a major nuclear operator, central to designing, building,
operating, and decommissioning reactors. EDF's nuclear operations are a cornerstone of the
French energy mix, contributing to national energy security and supporting the country's
transition to low-carbon energy. EDF is also involved in the development of small modular
reactors (SMRs) and next-generation European Pressurised Reactors (EPRs) to meet future
energy needs. Framatome, a subsidiary of EDF, is a global leader in reactor design,
construction, and maintenance. Specialising in pressurised water reactor (PWR) technology—
employed in over 60% of the world's nuclear reactors—Framatome supports EDF's nuclear
fleet in France and key projects like the UK's Hinkley Point C power station, covering the full
lifecycle of nuclear plants from supplying components like steam generators and control
rods to offering digital solutions for plant optimisation.
2. Orano, formerly Areva, plays a pivotal role in the nuclear fuel cycle both in Europe and
globally. The company handles uranium enrichment, MOX fuel fabrication, spent fuel
reprocessing, and radioactive waste management. The Georges Besse II enrichment plant in
France is a significant part of the European and even global supply chain, while the La
Hague reprocessing facility possess a notable portion of the world's spent nuclear fuel
reprocessing capabilities. Ongoing investments in the Georges Besse II site aim to increase
production and enable HALEU production. In 2024, Orano was among the top three global
uranium producers, with a market share of approximately 10%. The company operates

59

uranium mining sites in Canada, Kazakhstan, and Niger13, with additional projects under
investigation to assess their viability [114]. These initiatives support the long-term
sustainability of nuclear energy by ensuring a stable supply of raw materials and advancing
closed fuel cycle technologies.
3. Urenco, a joint venture between the UK, Germany, and the Netherlands, is a leading global
provider of uranium enrichment services. Employing centrifuge technology, the company
supplies low-enriched uranium to more than 50 reactors worldwide, holding a substantial
share of the global enrichment market. Its facilities in the UK, Germany, and the
Netherlands ensure a stable and secure fuel supply for Europe, reducing dependence on
external sources. Urenco has recently announced plans to expand its capacity in the UK and
US, including the development of HALEU (High-Assay Low-Enriched Uranium) enrichment
facilities, reflecting increasing demand for nuclear fuel driven by the need for low-carbon
energy and the evolution of advanced reactor technologies
4. Siemens Energy plays an important role in integrating nuclear power in modern energy
systems. Its nuclear division focuses on reactor systems, safety instrumentation, and digital
solutions that improve plant efficiency and safety. The company supplies steam turbines,
transformers, and control systems for nuclear plants, while developing digital platforms for
real-time monitoring and predictive maintenance. Siemens Energy collaborates with
industry partners for the long-term operations of reactors and supports the deployment of
next-generation technologies, contributing to the transition to a low-carbon energy mix by
ensuring nuclear power remains a flexible and reliable part of the grid. Its expertise in grid
integration and digitalisation assists in addressing the challenges of balancing nuclear
energy with renewable sources.
The situation for small modular reactors is particularly complex, with numerous startups emerging
in Europe and globally. A previous chapter provides an overview of existing activities, projects, and

3.5. Employment
According to established frameworks [11] [115] direct employment in the nuclear industry involves
employees working directly at the plants, including permanent subcontractors such as security
personnel and full-time equivalent outage personnel. Indirect employment encompasses roles
within the nuclear supply chain in industries that supply products and services to it. Induced
employment involves jobs created in the broader economy due to direct and indirect spending. It is
estimated that each gigawatt of nuclear power generates 50 000 labour years of direct
employment [115].

4.1.1. Current EU positioning in the global market
In recent years, the nuclear industry has undergone a significant shift, with the construction of
large-scale reactors increasingly dominated by emerging markets and developing economies,
particularly China and Russia. By the end of 2024, 63 reactors were under construction globally,

efforts, with 29 reactors under construction offering a combined 33 GW, accounting for nearly half
of global new builds. Most of these reactors are of Chinese design. Meanwhile, Russia has
established itself as the leading exporter of nuclear technology, with 23 GW of reactors being built
in six countries, highlighting its influence in the nuclear market. Other significant projects are
underway in India, Turkey, Egypt, Bangladesh, and Ukraine, largely utilising Russian designs.

4.1.2. Market prospects
The European Union’s nuclear power capacity is projected to grow from around 98 GW today to 109
GW by 2050, [23] an 11% increase, based on the intentions stated in Member States' National
Energy and Climate Plans (NECPs). This projection assumes that existing reactors will either operate

The European Union acknowledges the considerable potential for SMRs, even though none are

dedicated to heat and hydrogen production. All scenarios highlight a rapid deployment acceleration
after 2040, aligning with the broader rollout of AMRs. However, this growth depends on several
critical factors, including a strong nuclear supply chain, a skilled workforce, large-scale public and
private investments, and robust research capabilities.

4.3. Resource efficiency and dependence in relation to EU competitiveness
The nuclear supply chain in Europe is generally well-developed; however, there are potential
bottlenecks, particularly in areas like large-scale forging. If the demand for SMRs increases rapidly,
there could also be challenges in obtaining mechanical equipment such as nuclear-grade valves.
With those exceptions, Europe's nuclear industry does not critically depend on external countries for
the production of essential nuclear materials.
Nuclear energy projects utilise a wide range of materials, from common ones like concrete, steel,
and copper to specialised materials essential for specific functions. These specialised materials
include certain rare earth elements, boron, zirconium, and specific alloys. In fact, concrete and steel
account for the majority of the materials needed for constructing a nuclear plant [23]. Nuclear-
grade concrete requires a rare, specific type of sand. At the same time, Europe is losing its steel
manufacturing and processing capabilities against Chinese competition. The price of copper and
other key materials is subject to high volatility. This illustrates that even common materials require
attention to ensure their availability in a secure European value chain.
While nuclear energy is known for its lower mineral demands compared to other low-carbon
technologies, similar to hydropower [127], it still requires specific minerals such as chromium,
copper, nickel, hafnium, and yttrium [128]. Rare earth elements are primarily used in reactor control
applications. For example, samarium-149 is an effective neutron absorber for control rods;
europium is valued for its low melting point and neutron absorption capabilities; and gadolinium is
used for its effectiveness in neutron radiography and for enhancing the durability of reactor
shielding. However, the reliance on rare earth elements in nuclear reactors is relatively minor
compared to other clean energy technologies [129].
The use of high-alloy steels in nuclear plants significantly influences the demand for chromium and
nickel. Still, precise data on the quantities of different steel alloys used is rarely available. Given the
maturity of nuclear technology, a significant reduction in mineral demand is unlikely in the near
future. . Notably, only one of these critical elements, hafnium, is classified as critical under the
2017 Critical Raw Materials Act, with France being the primary global producer.Some additional
demands might be created by new concepts and new designs
There are no clear indicators that key materials used within the nuclear supply chain will be
inaccessible in the near term. Nonetheless, challenges may arise in accessing some materials in a
cost-competitive and timely manner at required volumes, due to geopolitical and other factors.
Despite the scarcity of its domestically available natural uranium, the EU has developed a
significant nuclear industry, thanks also to the resources of the Euratom Treaty. The common supply
policy can be a precious tool to address the challenges lying ahead on the way to a clean-energy
future.

5. Conclusions

Nuclear energy remains a vital component of the energy mix in Europe today, providing a significant
share of the continent's electricity. As of recent data, approximately 98 GW of nuclear power
capacity is installed in the European Union. This capacity plays a crucial role in delivering low-carbon
electricity, contributing significantly to Europe's climate goals. The nuclear industry is a well-
established sector in the EU, contributing approximately EUR 250 billion annually to the economy
and supporting around 500 000 jobs. This mature supply chain encompasses construction,
operation, maintenance, decommissioning, and waste management for existing nuclear plants,
ensuring a reliable energy source and economic stability.
Looking ahead, the EU is focused on several strategic areas to maximise the potential of nuclear
energy. Long-term operation of existing reactors is crucial to maintaining a low-carbon supply while
meeting climate objectives. But so are new reactor builds, using established technologies, to
maintain and even expand the EU's nuclear capacity. Member States are actively planning and
proposing new large nuclear power plant projects, with varying degrees of progress. A strategic
priority for the EU is the development and deployment of next-generation nuclear technologies,
such as Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) and Advances Modular Reactors (AMRs). These innovative
reactors are expected to begin operations in the early 2030s, with a projected capacity ranging
from 17 GWe to 53 GWe by 2050. SMRs particularly offer advantages in integrated energy systems
with high renewable penetration, providing flexible generation and enhancing system efficiency.
However, transitioning to these new technologies is not without challenges. While nuclear energy
production costs are competitive, new plant builds require significant initial capital investments, and
the supply chain for these projects needs strengthening. Fuel supply sourcing, notably natural
uranium, is another area of focus, with efforts to establish secure supply chains through the
Euratom Supply Agency. Human resources are critical; a skilled workforce is essential to realise
future nuclear projects, and training programmes must keep pace with technological advancements.
Europe is actively advancing SMR research and development, with numerous start-ups and
established companies contributing to this effort. To support this, policymakers are creating a
supportive ecosystem for SMR innovation and deployment. Key initiatives include the formation of
an industrial alliance for SMRs, which aims to streamline collaboration between stakeholders. This
alliance is also working with national regulators towards harmonised licensing requirements early in
the design process, reducing delays and costs. Additionally, new policies to simplify access to
venture capital for European start-ups, helping them scale their technologies are required and
envisaged.
Collaboration between traditional nuclear firms and agile start-ups is vital to delivering the clean,
affordable, low-carbon energy solutions Europe needs. By combining the expertise of experienced
organisations with the innovation of emerging companies, the sector can accelerate the
development of SMRs to meet climate goals and energy demands.
However, the research and development needs across all aspects of nuclear fission technology—
spanning the front end of the fuel cycle (e.g., advanced nuclear fuels), reactor technology, and the
back end (spent fuel reprocessing and long-term waste management, including partitioning and
transmutation)—must not be overlooked. While the back end was beyond the scope of this report, it
remains a critical area. The safe, long-term storage of nuclear waste is a central concern in any

75

discussion of the risks and benefits of nuclear energy. Meanwhile, reprocessing spent nuclear fuel
could significantly enhance the EU’s resource and energy independence.
With strategic investment, robust policy frameworks, and strong collaboration across stakeholders,
the nuclear industry can—and will—achieve a strong position in the future. However, companies
must navigate intense global competition, not only for traditional nuclear power plants but also for
SMRs. This competition will test the EU’s ability to maintain, or in some domains establish,
technological autonomy in the nuclear sector.
Ultimately, alongside economic and technological considerations, public acceptance will be a
decisive factor in determining how significant nuclear energy’s contribution to the energy mix can
be in addressing energy security and climate change.

Annex 2: Energy System Model and Scenario:
Global CETO 2°C Scenario 2025
The Global CETO 2°C Scenario 2025 has been generated by the global energy model POLES-JRC
(Prospective Outlook for the Long-term Energy System).
POLES-JRC covers the entire global energy system with a high level of regional detail,
encompassing 66 countries and regions, from primary supply (fossil fuels, renewables) to
transformation (power, biofuels, hydrogen, and hydrogen-derived fuels, such as e-fuels and
ammonia) and final consumption sectoral demand (industry, buildings, transport). International
markets and energy fuel prices are calculated endogenously. The model comprises a comprehensive
portfolio of technologies, with technology dynamics and interactions across sectors modelled using
endogenous technology learning.
Detailed documentation of the POLES-JRC model is provided in [1]. Techno-economic assumptions
used in the current version of the model are provided in [2]. The latter report provides also a
comprehensive overview of the dynamics and interaction of various clean energy technologies until
the end of the century. POLES-JRC results are published in the annual "Global Climate and Energy
Outlooks” (GECO) report, which provides detailed country energy and GHG balances, and an online
visualisation interface.
The Global CETO 2°C scenario 2025 is designed to limit global temperature increase to 2°C at the
end of the century. It is driven by a single global carbon price. The Global CETO 2°C scenario 2025
builds on the POLES-JRC model version used for GECO 2024 [3] and has been enhanced as
following:
— Cost optimisation for electrolysers powered by renewables (PV, wind) has been
implemented considering an over-sizing of renewable capacities and battery storage.
— Electrolyser investment costs have been increased reflecting recent literature revisions.
— Updated investment costs for renewable technologies and utility battery according to [4] as
well as updated installed capacities for power generating technologies.
— Revised global wind profiles (off-shore and on-shore).
— Updated data on new vehicles and vehicle stock by transport mode (battery and fuel cell
vehicles, ICE, hybrid) and vehicle type (passenger cars, light and heavy trucks, buses).
— Updated hydrogen infrastructure cost related to road transport.
