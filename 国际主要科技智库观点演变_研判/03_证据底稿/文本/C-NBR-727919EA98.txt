T
he tensions between Indonesia, China, and the United States in the nickel market encapsulate the broader
challenges shaping the geopolitics of critical minerals. Indonesia’s resource nationalism, China’s investment
and technological lead in critical minerals processing, overcapacity, environmental issues, and the United
States’ weak industrial base all converge in the Indonesian nickel sector—making it a microcosm of the global
competition over strategic materials. Indonesia’s continued dominance of the global nickel industry, facilitated by
Chinese investment and technological innovation, presents a complex strategic dilemma for the United States that
Washington has been unable to successfully navigate to date. Ultimately, the United States faces a choice between
difficult realities rather than ideal outcomes .
The Rise of Indonesia: From Stainless Steel to Battery Materials
Indonesia’s rise in the global nickel market is inseparable from China’s industrial strategy, investment capacity,
and domestic demand. Jakarta’s “downstreaming” policies combined with abundant nickel reserves created the
framework for domestic processing. But Indonesia lacked the capital and technological capability required to build
out an independent supply chain. China provided the inputs and turned Indonesia into the world’s lowest-cost
nickel producer while reshaping the economics of the entire industry.
Kevin Brunelli is a Nonresident Fellow at the Center on Global Energy Policy at Columbia University, focusing on critical minerals
and the battery supply chain.

Two misconceptions pervade the story of
Indonesia’s rise as a nickel producer. First, despite
headlines linking Indonesia’s nickel boom to the rise of
electric vehicles (EVs), the country’s production began
and remains oriented toward the stainless-steel sector.
Globally, stainless steel accounts for approximately
70% of nickel demand, while EV batteries represent
10%–15%.1 Indonesia’s industry reflects this balance:
most of its nickel output feeds China’s stainless-steel
supply chain rather than the battery market. Second,
Indonesia’s resource nationalism, particularly export
bans on raw minerals, are sometimes viewed as the
sole driver of its success in global supply chains for
critical minerals. However, even before the 2014
export ban, Indonesia supplied roughly half of China’s
nickel ore imports, creating a level of dependency
that made withdrawal costly for Chinese firms. 2 The
throughline in Indonesia’s rise as a nickel producer
is China’s presence in the industry providing both
major investment and demand for nickel.3
China’s investment in Indonesia followed
Beijing’s “going out” strategy—a state-backed push
for Chinese firms to invest abroad. The strategy’s goal
was to secure supply chains, expand global influence,
and support domestic economic growth by investing
in natural resources, infrastructure, and strategic
industries abroad. The strategy was supported
by programs like the Belt and Road Initiative,
while state-backed financial institutions offered
lower-cost capital. The export ban and China’s need
for nickel to feed its stainless-steel sector, occurring
simultaneously with a global surge in Chinese state
financing, pushed Chinese firms to embed themselves
within Indonesia’s industrial policy.
China’s initial investment in Indonesia’s critical
minerals sector focused on rotary kiln-electric
furnace (RKEF) smelters producing nickel pig iron
and ferronickel for stainless steel. Tsingshan, a
Chinese metals and mining company specializing in
stainless steel and nickel, became the anchor investor
in Indonesia’s new industrial era. In 2013, at the launch
of the 21st-Century Maritime Silk Road, Tsingshan
signed a memorandum of understanding—witnessed
by Xi Jinping and then Indonesian president Susilo
Bambang Yudhoyono—to develop what became
the Indonesia Morowali Industrial Park (IMIP).
By 2015 –17, the IMIP was operational, functioning
not merely as a smelter complex but as a sprawling
industrial city equipped with ports, coal-fired power
plants, and dedicated logistics systems.
While investment was spearheaded by private
firms, state support followed. Private-sector
investment for the IMIP and other industrial parks
was backed by Chinese state-directed financing,
including the China-ASEAN Investment Cooperation
Fund, Export-Import Bank of China, and Industrial
and Commercial Bank of China. This coordinated,
below-market financing ecosystem—unavailable
to Western companies—enabled rapid capital
deployment, high risk tolerance, willingness to
accept environmental degradation, and construction
timelines unmatched by any competitor. The IMIP
is now the largest nickel industrial hub in the world,
hosting at least fifteen operational smelters with
a combined capacity of 500,000–700,000 tons of
nickel per year, with another 300,000–500,000 tons
planned.4 This combination of private and public
investment successfully created a stable feedstock for
China’s domestic stainless-steel industry and drove
global nickel prices downward.
Yet China’s most consequential contribution
came in the next phase: the commercialization of
high-pressure acid leaching (HPAL) technology in
Indonesia. It was long considered uneconomical
to process the country’s low-grade but abundant
laterite deposits into higher-grade refined nickel
material necessary for batteries. Western attempts
in Australia and Papua New Guinea were plagued
by cost overruns and engineering failures. However,
Tsingshan (in partnership with GEM, CATL, and
Hanwa) successfully started operations on two HPAL
facilities in 2021. Huayou Cobalt and Lygend soon
followed. Due to the Chinese firms’ innovation and
engineering know-how, the plants convert low-grade

laterite into mixed hydroxide precipitate (MHP),
which is a key precursor material necessary for EV
batteries. This unlocked a massive new supply stream
that had previously been stranded, although with
significant potential risks to surrounding ecosystems.
Now over 80% of the country’s battery-grade MHP
output comes from Chinese-affiliated projects.5
In 2014, Indonesia had 3 smelters. More than
ten years later, it has 49 RKEF smelters producing
nickel pig iron and ferronickel for stainless steel
and 5 operational HPAL facilities producing
MHP for batteries with additional projects under
construction.6 Nickel export revenues grew from
$11.9 billion in 2020 to more than $38 billion by
2024, and now Indonesia accounts for roughly 60%
of global nickel production.7 Looking ahead, the
International Energy Agency projects that Indonesia’s
dominance will deepen, with the country expected to
supply 67% of the world’s mined nickel by 2030 and
75% by 2040. 8 Its surge in supply has pushed global
prices downward, forcing higher-cost competitors in
countries like Australia and New Caledonia to put
their facilities in care and maintenance, furthering
consolidation of the market. Indonesia has also grown
into the second-largest producer of cobalt, which is a
byproduct of the HPAL process.9
Chinese firms control roughly 75% of Indonesia’s
refining capacity, with Tsingshan and Jiangsu Delong
alone accounting for over 70%. 10 Tsingshan underpins
Indonesia’s stainless-steel industry through the IMIP
and is heavily involved in the battery supply chain
through ownership in the Weda Bay Industrial
Park, which is the second-largest nickel industrial
park in Indonesia. Chinese influence is increasingly
extending further downstream in Indonesia’s battery
supply chain through Huayou Cobalt, Lygend, and
most notably CATL, the Chinese battery giant.
CATL and partners broke ground in 2025 on an
approximately $6 billion integrated battery project
that will span the entire battery supply chain from
mining to battery recycling.11
China’s continued investment and innovation
in Indonesia’s critical minerals sector after trade
restrictions were implemented should be a warning
signal for Western policymakers. Though one school
of thought believes that Chinese firms can be forced
out of supply chains through trade restrictions or
environmental standards, Indonesia’s nickel industry
shows Chinese firms’ willingness to adapt to the
political realities and find ways to make the system
work in their favor. Additionally, policymakers
should not underestimate the ability for Chinese firms
to innovate across the supply chain. State financing
or lower regulatory standards support them, but
Chinese companies are also leaders in innovation.
Limited U.S. Engagement Entrenches
China’s Position in Indonesia
U.S. companies have a long history in copper
and gold mining in Indonesia, but their engagement
has diminished in response to resource nationalism.
Freeport-McMoRan began operating the Grasberg
copper-gold mine in the 1970s, which is one of the
world’s largest copper mines. Over time, however,
Indonesia’s growing emphasis on resource
nationalism reshaped the operating environment.
The Indonesian government forced the company
to divest a majority stake in Grasberg in 2018 after
years of domestic pressure. Freeport also committed
to build a domestic copper smelter as a condition for
retaining its mining rights. The other major U.S. actor
in Indonesia, Newmont Mining, exited Indonesia
entirely in 2016 after prolonged disputes over export
restrictions, divestment mandates, and processing
requirements. Mandatory divestment laws and
overall uncertainty in the operating environment
make it challenging for Western mining companies
to enter the country at scale.
Indonesia’s resource nationalism policies do
not pose the same challenges for Chinese firms.

Whereas Western companies depend on stable
regulations and predictable returns, China’s hybrid
ecosystem of state-owned and private enterprises can
absorb volatility, accept low margins, and operate in
environments that deter most foreign investors. Thus,
Indonesia’s policies have the effect of entrenching
China’s presence across the country’s mining
industry while also limiting the United States’ role in
a key Indo-Pacific country.
The only notable recent U.S. engagement in
Indonesian nickel came from Ford, the all-American
automaker. In 2023 the company announced a
$4.5 billion partnership with Vale Indonesia and
China’s Huayou Cobalt to build an HPAL facility in
Indonesia. The project aims to produce 120,000 tons
of MHP. Ford’s move reflects the pressure facing
Western automakers to secure critical minerals. But
it is equally telling that Ford, despite geopolitical
tensions, partnered with Huayou for the project to
be successful.
Political pressures also limited U.S. engagement
with Indonesia on critical minerals. The lack of a free
trade agreement prevented Indonesian nickel from
being eligible for the clean vehicle tax credit during the
Biden administration, while pressure from senators
and NGOs blocked a U.S.-Indonesia critical minerals
agreement. Now, the oversized role of Chinese firms
in Indonesia limits further engagement. China
hawks claim that the political walls work to limit
Chinese involvement in key supply chains, but these
regulations further entrench the power of Chinese
companies in Indonesia and prevent U.S. involvement
in Indonesia’s critical minerals sector.
The Unfortunate Reality
The unfortunate reality is that Indonesia’s and
the United States’s priorities are aligned, but the
United States’ view of Chinese influence prevents
further engagement. Indonesia wants to diversify
its customer base. The Indonesian nickel market
has been built largely by and for one customer,
China, but it no longer represents the largest growth
market. Despite stainless steel being the largest
sector of the nickel market, nickel products for
EV batteries are the largest growth sector of the
industry. But the Chinese EV market has pivoted
to and is now dominated by cheaper, nickel-free,
lithium-iron-phosphate (LFP) batteries.
Lithium-ion battery chemistries that include
nickel in the cathode offer extended range but are
more expensive than nickel-free chemistries. Despite
the cost, the market in the United States remains
focused on nickel-based batteries due to worries
around range and the reliance on South Korean and
Japanese battery producers that focus on nickel-based
batteries. This means the United States, not China,
is the largest growth market for nickel. Indonesia
is keen to access this market, and regardless of the
Trump administration’s views on EVs, U.S. factories
are already producing EV batteries that rely on
nickel-based chemistries. For the United States,
Indonesian nickel remains strategically important
because it anchors global pricing and supply for a
key input for stainless steel, defense-related alloys,
and batteries.
Given the reluctance of U.S. mining companies
to operate in Indonesia and the United States’ push
to expand domestic mineral processing, the most
realistic path forward is not upstream investment but
partnerships between Indonesian nickel producers
and U.S. midstream firms or end users. Despite China’s
current influence in Indonesia, a growing number
of mineral projects are not controlled by Chinese
companies. Majority Chinese-owned battery-grade
production is expected to decline from roughly
80% today to 54% by the early 2030s. 12 Additionally,
the Indonesian government has demonstrated a
willingness to mandate divestment. This creates
an opportunity for U.S. engagement—particularly
through offtake agreements, minority equity stakes,
and joint ventures focused on precursor production
or downstream conversion in the United States.

However, such engagement will not materialize
without sustained government-to-government
coordination to provide regulatory clarity, financing,
and political cover for U.S. firms. These efforts
could be embedded within broader trade or critical
minerals agreements, allowing the United States
to gain exposure to Indonesia’s dominant nickel
supply without abandoning its domestic industrial
objectives. It is impossible to eliminate China’s
influence, but these steps could give the United States
a foothold in a supply chain that it currently observes
from the sidelines. Alternatively, the United States
could decide China’s influence over the nickel market
is too great and U.S. companies should push forward
on LFP batteries to limit the demand for nickel.
However, there are still issues with this approach. The
LFP supply chain is highly concentrated in China,
which is responsible for nearly 99% of the global LFP
cathode market, and Chinese firms CATL and BYD
are leaders in LFP innovation. Whether the United
States avoids Indonesian nickel and depends on a
Chinese-dominated LFP supply chain or embraces
Indonesian nickel and potentially depends on
Chinese-affiliated HPAL, China retains decisive
influence over pricing and supply.
This dilemma in U.S. policy toward Indonesia
underscores the overarching challenges to securing
supply chains of critical minerals and countering
China’s influence. As Western financing found
Indonesia’s resource nationalism too risky,
state-backed Chinese investment paired with
innovative companies led to overcapacity in a key
supply chain that has left U.S. allies struggling and the
United States forced to catch up, without a coherent
strategy for achieving its goals .
U.S. Critical Minerals Strategy:
What Does Diversification Mean?
The goal of efforts by the United States and
other Western countries to secure supply chains of
critical minerals and counter China’s dominance
is diversification. The idea is to diversify supply
chains away from China to ensure they cannot be
weaponized, as Beijing has done through recent
export restrictions on rare earth elements. The
example of Indonesia’s critical minerals industry
raises fundamental questions regarding this
strategy: Are supply chains considered “diversified”
if production moves outside China, away from the
influence of export restrictions? Or is diversification
defined not by geography but by reduced dependence
on Chinese corporate control altogether?
If diversification is only about geography, then
Indonesia’s export ban is beneficial for U.S. interests.
If not for the export ban, the processing taking place
in Indonesia would occur in China, which is the
only other country with the midstream processing
ecosystem to absorb and refine large volumes of
nickel ore. The ore would not flow to the United States
or its allies because the United States does not have
the nickel-processing capability or the pre-cathode or
cathode active materials production required for EV
batteries. Even the only operational nickel mine in the
United States exports ore to Canada for processing.
However, if diversification is broader than
geography and includes measures to minimize
Chinese influence, then the United States and its allies
would need to build new supply chains excluding
Chinese firms. One potential option to support
such an endeavor is through a “green premium” to
offset higher-cost but more sustainable production.
Australian officials and producers have repeatedly
called for differentiated markets, and the Australia-
U.S. critical minerals framework included language
on a “standards-based systems” critical minerals
market.13 However, there is not strong market
demand for a differentiated product, nor is there a
widely accepted “sustainable” nickel standard. Buyers
remain sensitive to costs rather than environmental,
social, and governance attributes—particularly in
an environment of chronic oversupply created by
Indonesian expansion.

Building a separate non-Chinese nickel supply
chain would be enormously costly and require a
long-term industrial policy far more expansive than
current U.S. efforts. Policymakers are therefore left
with a difficult question: should scarce government
financing be devoted to standing up a separate nickel
supply chain or diverted toward other minerals and
technologies where China’s ability to weaponize the
market is stronger?
Engaging directly with Indonesia through
partnerships with nickel producers and increased
government coordination is the most efficient path
forward and would allow policymakers to focus
on immediate supply chain threats. Regardless
of how policymakers ultimately resolve the
question of prioritization, China’s approach in
Indonesia illustrates the importance of coordinated
public-private investment, long-term capital, and
industrial strategy in building resilient supply chains.
These are lessons the United States will need to learn
if it seeks meaningful diversification. •
