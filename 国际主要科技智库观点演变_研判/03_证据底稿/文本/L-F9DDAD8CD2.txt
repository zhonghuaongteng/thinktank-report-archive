About This Report
This report extends the framework from the RAND report Securing AI Model Weights to address the chal -
lenge of protecting algorithmic insights —that is, the techniques and design know-how that drive artificial
intelligence (AI) progress.1 Unlike model weights, which can be centralized and secured, insights are distrib -
uted across code, documentation, communications, and human expertise, making them far harder to contain.
Using expert engagements, a targeted literature review, and a gap analysis, we define five insight security
levels that are aligned with five adversary operational capacities. The framework is descriptive rather than
prescriptive, offering a structured way to assess what level of protection would be required to defend a given
insight against a specific adversary capability.
We explore three central themes: the broad and human-centric attack surface for insights, the role of
compartmentalization as a core security principle, and the organizational trade-offs that accompany higher
protection levels. With this report, we aim to inform policymakers, AI developers, and security practitioners
seeking to understand what securing algorithmic insights would entail and how such measures could be
implemented as AI capabilities advance.

Summary
Algorithmic insights—the novel techniques, methods, and design know-how that materially improve artifi -
cial intelligence (AI) systems—are among the most strategically valuable assets in the AI ecosystem. Across
language models evaluated from 2012 to 2023, the compute required to reach a fixed performance threshold
fell by half approximately every eight months due to algorithmic efficiency improvements, which is equiva -
lent to roughly a threefold annual gain in effective compute. Theft or leakage of these insights could compress
years of AI progress for adversaries, and some insights might also lower barriers to dangerous capabilities,
making their protection a matter not only of commercial interest but of national security.
Unlike model weights, which can be centralized on secured servers, algorithmic insights resist centraliza -
tion: They exist as part of code, documentation, communications, and human knowledge, and the insights
can leak through low-bandwidth channels, such as conversations or observable screens. Although insights’
distributed nature makes them harder to protect than weights, the challenge is not intractable. Organizations
can take concrete, practical steps to substantially reduce the risk of unauthorized access to such insights. Fur-
thermore, the measures required at lower security levels (SLs) align closely with industry best practices that
most frontier AI developers might already be implementing.
In this report, we extend a framework from the RAND report Securing AI Model Weights (SMW) to pro -
vide the first systematic roadmap for protecting algorithmic insights.
2 We define five insight security levels
(ISLs) aligned with five adversary operational capacities (OCs), identify 44 attack vectors, and provide bench-
mark security systems for each level. The framework is intentionally descriptive rather than prescriptive: It
provides a tool for answering the conditional question, “If an organization wishes to—or is expected
to—secure insight X against an adversary with capability level Y, what security posture would likely be
required?” After answering this question, organizations can make informed decisions about which insights
warrant security investments using their own assessments, priorities, and cost-benefit analyses.
Key Findings
• Some properties that make insights valuable also make them difficult to secure. A lgorithmic insights
are often conceptually compact, broadly applicable, and easily transferable, which means that they can
leak through a wide variety of channels. Unauthorized proliferation could erase competitive or strategic
advantages that took years and billions of dollars to establish, and overcoming this challenge requires a
comprehensive security approach rather than protections of a few obvious assets.
•
 Th
e attack surface is broad and extends well beyond cyberthreats. The attack vectors that we identi -
fied reflect the diversity of technical, organizational, and human pathways through which insights can
leak. Participants whom we engaged with as part of our study rated human intelligence threats and
other risks that are not cyberthreats, especially risks related to personnel and process weaknesses on the
defender’s side, as highly feasible across all adversary capability levels (OC1 through OC5). Employees
who inadvertently share sensitive information through casual conversations, conference presentations,
or social interactions represent major risks that technical controls alone cannot address.
•
 Co
mpartmentalization is the central organizing principle for insight security. Because transforma -
tive insights are often easily shared, organizations might not be able to rely on protecting just a few

Implications
Organizations developing frontier AI systems can use our framework to evaluate their current security pos -
tures, identify gaps, and prioritize investments. At the lower ISLs, the path forward is clear: Security mea -
sures for ISL1 and ISL2 represent “security hygiene” that most organizations handling sensitive AI techniques
could implement quickly. ISL3 adds meaningful protection against insider threats and organized cybercrime
while likely remaining achievable within recognizable corporate structures.
For organizations considering stronger protections, the framework maps out a spectrum of options along-
side the trade-offs involved. Whether the trade-offs involved in achieving a given SL are warranted (par -
ticularly for ISL3 through ISL5) depends on judgments about specific insights, specific adversaries, and the
broader trajectory of AI development; these are judgments that we do not attempt to make. What the frame-
work does provide is a structured way to reason about what each protection level would require, which lets
decisionmakers act with a clear-eyed understanding of the costs and benefits involved.

CHAPTER 1
The Case for Securing Algorithmic Insights
As frontier artificial intelligence (AI) systems continue to demonstrate increasingly sophisticated capabili -
ties, securing the key assets that enable these advancements becomes critically important. These assets rep -
resent proprietary intellectual property (IP) that underpins technological leadership, and responsibly man -
aging the assets’ security is essential for both competitive positioning and the broader goal of ensuring that
dual-use capabilities are developed and deployed safely.
Modern AI capabilities are largely a product of three key inputs: compute, data, and algorithms. 1 In
this report, we focus on the third: algorithmic insights , or the novel techniques, methods, and design
know-how that materially improve the efficiency, performance, deployability, or other desirable features
of AI systems.
We apply an analytical framework from the RAND report Securing AI Model Weights (SMW) to certain
threats and security issues that algorithms, as a distinct class of assets, face.
2 We identify potential attack
vectors, assess adversarial capabilities across operational capacity (OC) categories, and define benchmark
security levels (SLs) with tailored protective measures. The primary goal of SMW’s authors was to “promote
more-robust AI security strategies by facilitating [a] shared understanding” of “how an organization’s secu -
rity measures translate into actual security.”
3 Our goal with this report is one and the same.
Throughout this report, we refer to SLs for protecting algorithmic insights as insight SLs (ISLs 1
through 5), distinguishing them from the weight SLs (WSLs 1 through 5), which were established in
SMW to describe model weight protection. We apply the umbrella term SL to both frameworks. Protecting
algorithmic insights requires meeting an appropriate WSL for the underlying models then adding further
measures on top.
4 ISLs should therefore be understood as building on and extending the SMW weight secu -
rity framework rather than replacing it. Although the two frameworks are complementary, an organization
might reasonably target different SLs for weights and insights that are based on the organization’s assessment
of relative asset value and threat exposure. For instance, an organization might pursue WSL4 for its model
weights (the learned parameters of trained models) while maintaining ISL2 for its algorithmic insights if the
organization judges the weights to be at a greater risk of adversary targeting.
Algorithmic insights present security challenges that differ substantively from the challenges of model
weight protection. Unlike model weights, which can be isolated as discrete digital artifacts, algorithmic
insights resist centralization . AI researchers’ code exists on developers’ hard drives and in documentation
and collaboration tools; findings might be shared via diverse communication channels; and critical knowl -
edge resides in the minds of researchers, executives, and a wide variety of other personnel.

CHAPTER 2
Defining the Security Object
The findings that we present in this report should be understood within the broader context of the ecosystem
of AI security, which encompasses such components as model weights, architectural design, training data,
operational infrastructure, and the algorithmic innovations that enable these systems to achieve frontier
capabilities.
1 Within this ecosystem, we focus on one critical component: algorithmic insights . In practice,
algorithmic insights and other components of the ecosystem (such as architectural design) can overlap; we
discuss the boundaries of what we include in our definition.
We specifically address threats related to theft, leakage, or unauthorized replication of algorithmic
insights—that is, loss of confidentiality.
2 We touch on other security objectives, such as integrity and avail -
ability, only so far as they intersect with protecting confidentiality. This decision reflects the primary con -
cern that unauthorized dissemination of key insights could enable adversaries to rapidly replicate or build on
frontier AI models’ capabilities.
We describe measures that frontier AI developers themselves can implement to protect algorithmic
insights. Developers at the cutting edge of AI development have the primary capacity to implement security
controls when such controls are warranted. We aim to make the discussion accessible to security profession-
als, policymakers, and AI company leadership, although our recommendations focus on internal security
measures rather than external regulatory requirements or industrywide standards.
As a general principle, we take no normative stance on the broader question of which algorithmic
insights should be shared or secured. Some insights warrant protection because of their strategic impor -
tance or dual-use potential, while others could be shared publicly to advance scientific understanding and
beneficial applications. Deciding the appropriate security posture for any given insight must account for fac-
tors that are beyond the scope of this report, including the insight’s potential to enable harmful capabilities,
the likelihood of independently discovering the insight, the competitive landscape, and broader consider -
ations about the overall pace and direction of AI development.
We assume that organizations can use the insights’ security characteristics (size, access, medium, observ -
ability, transferability, shelf life) to inform which insights to classify, compartmentalize, and protect. The
security characteristics framework serves a second function: It shows why we do not attempt to map indi -
vidual security controls to specific insight types. Because individual insights frequently exist in multiple
forms simultaneously, each with different security characteristics, a type-to-control mapping would be both
incomplete and potentially misleading. A training recipe that exists as code, as internal documentation, and
as knowledge held by its developers is simultaneously vulnerable to code exfiltration, document theft, and
elicitation through conversation. The ISL benchmarks in Chapter 5 address this dilemma by defining com -
prehensive security postures at each threat level rather than prescribing controls per insight category.

We do not attempt to assess or quantify the impact of the theft of particular insights. Although some
forecasting studies have pursued such quantitative approaches, 3 our framework takes a qualitative and
guardrail-focused approach, concentrating on identifying vulnerabilities and cataloging mitigations. The
underlying assumption is that organizations will determine which algorithmic insights, if any, they deem
sensitive and that the controls we recommend in this report should be applied to those insights at an SL
appropriate to their assessed sensitivities. 
We do not prioritize security measures within each SL. Our categorization of SLs is not a full, ordered
priority list, but we aim to provide enough information to assist security teams in prioritizing next steps
no matter what their existing security posture is, and we do so while avoiding overprescription for tacti -
cal decisions that are context-dependent. Organizations should triage and prioritize according to their own
circumstances.
The field of AI security continues to evolve rapidly. Readers should view this framework as a starting
point for calibrating organizational security approaches rather than as a definitive or permanent standard.
Defining Algorithmic Insights
What Are Algorithmic Insights?
Algorithmic insights are the novel techniques, methods, or design know-how that materially improve the
efficiency, performance, deployability, or similar desirable features of AI systems. This definition is inten -
tionally broad and covers innovations across the AI development life cycle. Concretely, we divide algorithmic
insights into the following three major categories:
•
 Pr
etraining and training-time insights. These insights include innovations that improve AI models
before and during training, including architectural designs (e.g., the transformer 4), tactical improve -
ments (e.g., normalization and regularization techniques), training algorithms, scaling-law discoveries,
and data strategies (e.g., new methods of cleaning or curating data at scale). These insights have histori-
cally delivered large, durable gains and often require broad access within research teams to implement
and iterate.
5
• Post-training insights. These insights are te chniques applied after a base model is trained that sig -
nificantly enhance its capabilities, typically at a low cost. These insights include such methods as
reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and parameter-efficient fine-tuning approaches.
In practice, these techniques can enable major capability jumps without the expense of full retraining.
6
• Inference and deployment optimization insights. T hese insights are methods that make trained
models run faster or more efficiently in production or that improve models’ capabilities and output

Key Security Characteristics of Algorithmic Insights
Algorithmic insights are a heterogeneous category, varying widely in their forms and in how they might leak
or be protected. Some insights are small and simple; for example, they can be a new prompt format or tuning
trick that can be conveyed in just a few sentences as part of ordinary conversation. Other insights are large
and complex, such as an end-to-end bespoke training pipeline requiring tens of thousands of lines of code
to implement. Likewise, some insights live only in a few researchers’ minds or in confidential design docu -
ments, whereas others are widely embedded in codebases, configurations and parameters, model weights,
hardware, or even marketing material.
9 
In general, four baseline properties influence an insight’s security profile:
• Co
mplexity and size. How complex is the insight in terms of information content or technical intricacy?
An idea consisting of a few lines of pseudocode or a short verbal description is easier to communicate
or copy than a complicated engineering system. All else being equal, smaller and simpler insights are
harder to secure—a single individual could memorize or exfiltrate them—whereas larger or more com -
plex innovations are inherently harder to steal.
•
 Acces
s. Who within an organization needs to know or interact with the insight to do their job? Some
trade secrets can be tightly held by a small research team, but many algorithmic techniques must be
shared broadly with engineers, DevOps or information technology personnel, or other staff to be
applied in practice. Broader internal access translates into a larger attack surface for adversaries. Gen -
erally, the more employees or contractors who work with a particular insight, the higher the risk of its
theft or leakage.

conversation at a party, as workshop participants attested. This lack of a natural “size defense” means that
organizations might not be able to rely on shoring up protections around just a few obvious major insights
(e.g., model weights), and organizations might require more-comprehensive approaches to safeguard even
small-but-critical insights.
The Strategic Importance of Securing Algorithmic Insights
Algorithmic insights act as force multipliers for compute and data, sharply reducing the resources needed
to achieve a given level of AI performance. 10 Algorithmic progress alone has been delivering approximately
threefold annual gains in effective compute, and training compute for frontier AI models has simultane-
ously grown at roughly fivefold per year, meaning that effective compute has been increasing by more
than an order of magnitude annually.
11 This force-multiplier property has two important implications for
global security.

and straightforward to implement, they could enable small teams or even individuals to amplify the capabili-

That said, the calculus around insight diffusion is not one-sided. Some insights might be capability-
specific, so the risks and benefits of their diffusion should be assessed with that specific capability in mind.
Many algorithmic insights broadly improve AI capabilities or efficiency, and whether it is better to broadly
diffuse such insights or to control their access is highly contested; it is a question of policy and strategy, and
the answer might change as beneficial or dangerous capabilities emerge from AI systems.
The downstream value of stolen algorithmic insights differs qualitatively from that of stolen model
weights. Possession of a frontier model’s weight file gives an adversary immediate, deployable capability. The
adversary can run the model, fine-tune it, and extract value without making any additional research and
development investment. Possession of an algorithmic insight, by contrast, confers a generative advantage: A
novel training methodology, a more efficient architecture, or a breakthrough in data curation can be applied
across multiple model generations and adapted to the adversary’s own infrastructure. This makes certain
algorithmic insights, in some respects, more strategically valuable than any single model. Insights represent
the capacity to build frontier AI, not merely to use a particular instance of it.

CHAPTER 3
Research Approach and Expert Engagement
We used a mixed-methods approach to (1) synthesize existing knowledge on unauthorized access risks to AI
algorithmic insights and (2) develop a structured framework for mitigating those risks. We integrated expert
input, a targeted literature scan, and a structured synthesis within a single analytical workflow. Our process
followed the same five-step structure that was used in the SMW framework, which we adapted for algorith -
mic insights.
Research Design and Data Collection
We collected experts’ input through workshops and interviews. We conducted a targeted literature scan to
identify threat pathways and evaluate mitigation strategies. This design captured both practitioner experi -
ence and empirical evidence to support a repeatable process for assessing AI security risks.
Expert Input
The primary data-collection activity was the RAND-sponsored Securing AI Algorithmic Insights workshop
held in San Francisco in May 2025. Twenty subject-matter experts participated, including ten in person and
ten asynchronously. Participants were experts in security engineering and in AI research, safety, hardware,
and software security. We also conducted follow-up interviews to clarify and expand on workshop findings.
Before the workshop, participants completed a structured assessment that asked them to evaluate adver -
sary OCs, the effectiveness of five incremental SLs, and areas of disagreement or refinement. These inputs
informed the workshop discussions and subsequent analysis.
In workshop sessions, we used a semistructured format to balance systematic coverage with open debate.
Experts focused on three central questions:
•
 Wh
at constitutes an AI algorithmic insight?
• Wh
ich actors are capable of compromising such insights?
• Wh
ich mitigations meaningfully reduce risk?
We coded facilitator notes, interview transcripts, and participant materials using a structured codebook
that aligned with the study objectives. Codes captured attack types, OCs, and rationales for security ratings.
We complemented this qualitative analysis with quantitative aggregation of experts’ judgments on the feasi -
bility of attack vectors and the adequacy of SLs. Experts rated each vector’s feasibility and potential for miti -
gation on a binary scale, which we aggregated and converted to a five-point scale. We calculated “gap scores”
by comparing the perceived adversary capability with the perceived mitigation strength to identify how SLs
were too weak or too strong. These results guided our refinement of the final security benchmarks.

Analytical Framework and Synthesis
Framework Extension
We extended the SMW framework to include algorithmic insights. The core architecture of attack vectors,
OC categories (OC1 through OC5), security measures, and SLs (SL1 through SL5) remained consistent across
AI asset types. To distinguish between the application of these levels to different asset types, we introduce
specific terminology: WSL1 through WSL5 refer to security requirements for model weights as described
in SMW, while ISL1 through ISL5 refer to the security requirements for algorithmic insights as we define
them in this report. The underlying framework and organizational capacity levels are shared, but the spe -
cific controls differ to reflect the distinct characteristics of each asset type. Algorithmic insights differ from
model weights in their heterogeneity and exposure pathways, which necessitated deeper treatment of such
disciplines as supply chain security, human intelligence (HUMINT), and physical surveillance. Algorithmic
insights share some vulnerabilities with model weights, and in those cases, we carried the corresponding
SMW measures into the ISL framework and adapted their application as needed. We developed new mea -
sures to address any of the algorithmic insights’ distinct vulnerabilities.
Defining Algorithmic Insights
The targeted literature scan and expert input helped us develop a working definition and taxonomy of algo -
rithmic insights. The taxonomy emphasizes the types of algorithmic knowledge that materially advance AI
performance, such as architectural innovations, optimization techniques, and scaling laws. This taxonomy
supports the threat and mitigation mapping that we describe in the next sections.
Identifying Attack Vectors and Security Measures
We identified attack vectors and security measures through an integrated analysis of the literature scan,
workshop discussions, interviews, and prior frameworks. We also relied on our own experience in AI secu -
rity, threat modeling, and intelligence operations. We evaluated each vector for relevance to algorithmic
insights and mapped them to one or more security measures. The resulting catalog represents a substantial,
though not exhaustive, set of plausible compromise pathways. Full descriptions of the attack vectors and
security measures appear in the appendixes.

CHAPTER 4
How Adversaries Acquire Algorithmic Insights
In this chapter, we identify the methods (attack vectors) that adversaries might employ to acquire algorithmic
insights without authorization. 1 These attack vectors serve two functions in our framework: First, they help
determine which security measures would be required to protect against specific threats. Second, because we
map vectors to the OC level at which an attack becomes feasible (Figure 4.1), they inform the SL benchmarks
that we present in Chapter 5.
To systematically evaluate potential attack vectors, we developed a five-part test examining whether
each vector (1) directly advances an attacker in accessing or learning insights, (2) is feasible (and/or whether
there is empirical evidence supporting that claim of feasibility), (3) requires security measures that are dis -
tinct from other vectors, (4) either differs from SMW vectors or requires modification for insight protection,
and (5) can be clearly distinguished by security professionals.
We emphasize that this catalog should not be considered exhaustive. Adversaries continually develop
novel techniques, and the rapid evolution of AI capabilities and organizational practices creates new vul -
nerabilities. Organizations should treat this analysis as a starting point for threat modeling rather than as a
comprehensive enumeration of all possible risks.
Landscape of Attack Vectors
We organized attack vectors according to their SMW categories, and we then identified 44 vectors within
those categories. Most of these vectors are inherited directly from SMW: The threats that apply to model
weights generally apply to algorithmic insights. In addition, we identified six attack vectors that are specific to
algorithmic insight security; these attack vectors apply more specifically to insights primarily because insights
can be communicated. Several existing attack vectors also take on particularly important or distinctive forms
in the context of algorithmic insights; we discuss these insight-specific manifestations in detail in Appendix B.
Table 4.1 summarizes these categories and vectors. Descriptions of each vector, along with real-world
examples, can be found in Appendix B.
As in the SMW framework, the categories in Table 4.1 group vectors by different organizing dimensions
depending on which dimension is most operationally useful: Some vectors are categorized by target (e.g.,
vectors in the “Undermining the access control system itself” category), by method (e.g., the “HUMINT”
category), or by the access required (e.g., the “Nontrivial access to data or networks” category). Individual
vectors within each category are defined to be distinct from one another. It is important to note that attack
vectors are individual techniques, not complete operations. A real-world attack campaign will typically chain
multiple vectors from different categories in sequence or in parallel.

Key Findings on Attack Vectors
The findings below draw on a structured capability assessment in which subject-matter experts rated the fea-
sibility of each attack vector at each OC level (OC1 through OC5) on a scale of 1 (low feasibility) to 5 (high
feasibility). Our assessment followed the same methodology that the authors of SMW used, although we
adapted the methodology for algorithmic insights. Full scoring results appear in Appendix D; we describe
the methodology in detail in Appendix A.
The attack surface for algorithmic insights is broader and more distributed than for model weights.
Although the same nine attack categories apply, applying the methodology to insights introduces six addi -
tional attack vectors and heightens the significance of several threat areas that were a lower priority for
weight security. Insights can exist across code, documentation, human knowledge, visual artifacts, commu -
nications, and numerous other locations, making the defender’s task fundamentally more challenging. Our
key findings are as follows:
•
 Co
nventional security concerns and low-bandwidth channels are strategically significant. Many
vectors represent conventional security concerns (e.g., endpoint compromises, communication inter -
ception, development environment exploits) rather than exotic intelligence tradecraft. Because insights
are distributed across endpoints, devices, and people rather than centralized in secured model-serving
infrastructure, these conventional vectors become primary threats. Many valuable insights can be con -
veyed through low-bandwidth channels: brief conversations, observed visual displays, or short written
descriptions. As a result, many attack vectors that are relevant to weight security only as an instrumental
step in a far-more-complicated operation, such as visual and audio surveillance, become high-priority
vectors for insight security. Although organizations should centralize and compartmentalize insights
to the maximum extent that is operationally feasible, some endpoint distribution appears unavoidable.
•

veral attack vectors were assessed by experts as feasible for adversaries at all capability levels,
even for the least capable adversary class (OC1). These universally exploitable vectors (e.g., exposed
credentials, misconfigurations, former employee risks, social engineering, and software supply chain
compromises) succeed primarily because of human and process failures on the defender’s side rather
than because they require technical sophistication from the attacker’s side. This finding underscores
that securing algorithmic insights requires robust baseline security practices even before addressing
more-sophisticated threats.

CHAPTER 5
Calibrating Defenses Across Five Security Levels
The SLs that we describe in this chapter and their benchmarks offer a preliminary framework for calibrating
the relationship between the implementation of security measures (and their expected security outcomes)
against adversaries of varying capabilities. Although the optimal security setup for a specific organization
might differ from the benchmark security setup for a given SL, each benchmark can assist in identifying
whether an organization’s security posture consistently falls short of experts’ expectations for that SL
(as opposed to achieving similar goals in a slightly different way) and therefore might not provide adequate
security for the relevant OC category.
Each ISL is designed to thwart an adversary’s OC level of equal value: ISL1 addresses OC1-level threats,
ISL2 addresses OC2-level threats, and so on. Additional details on how the ISLs map to OCs appear later in
this chapter.
A critical consideration for all ISLs is the relationship between security and operational flexibility. Lower
ISLs (ISL1 and ISL2) implement foundational protections that align with industry best practices and impose
minimal notable operational constraints. As security requirements increase, however, organizations face
progressively more-difficult choices: Measures that effectively defend against sophisticated adversaries often
constrain collaboration, slow development velocity, limit work flexibility, and increase operational overhead.
At ISL3, these constraints become noticeable; at ISL4 and ISL5, the constraints are likely to fundamentally
reshape how organizations operate. Throughout this chapter, we note when and how security measures begin
to impose significant operational costs.
We describe a benchmark system for each ISL, listing concrete measures and policies that represent the esti-
mated minimum requirements of a system that conforms to that level’s security goals. As mentioned previ -
ously, these SL benchmarks do not represent a complete standard or a comprehensive compliance regime.
It is important to consider some context for reading the benchmarks: ISLs are cumulative, and each
level builds on the SMW framework (see Figure 5.1). The tables that follow list only measures that are new
or substantially modified for algorithmic insights; they do not repeat measures inherited from SMW’s cor -
responding WSLs. Our ISL1, for example, includes all of SMW’s WSL1 measures by default, with additions
specific to insights. In this chapter, we focus on measures that are unique to insights or those requiring sub -
stantial modification from the weight security context. Full details on all security measures, including inher -
ited ones, can be found in Appendix C.

Compartmentalization represents the most significant departure from weight-security approaches. Insight

CHAPTER 6
Implications and a Path Forward
Organizations that are developing frontier AI systems face a fundamental challenge: The insights that pro -
vide a competitive advantage are inherently difficult to secure, yet their unauthorized proliferation could
eliminate technological leads that have taken years and billions of dollars to establish.
The framework that we presented in this report—which comprises 44 attack vectors across nine cat -
egories, five ISLs, and corresponding benchmarks—provides organizations with a structured approach for
assessing their security postures and identifying the areas in which investment is most needed. Our analysis
also clarifies some of the practical trade-offs involved, particularly with higher ISLs, and can help organiza -
tions make deliberate choices about which protections to implement depending on the value of the insights
they hold.
The ISLs represent a spectrum of increasing protection with corresponding increases in operational costs
and organizational changes. ISL1 and ISL2 security measures align with established security best practices
and impose minimal operational burdens; it seems likely that many organizations developing proprietary
AI systems have either already implemented these measures, or the organizations should be interested in
doing so. ISL3 introduces more-substantial constraints, including formal compartmentalization, enhanced
personnel screening, and dedicated security infrastructure, but these measures are within the range of what
security-conscious organizations in defense contracting and other sensitive and regulated industries already
maintain. Beyond ISL3, the trade-offs become more pronounced. The measures associated with ISL4 and
ISL5, such as isolated compartmented facilities with emanation shielding, government-standard personnel
vetting, supply chain compartmentalization, and restrictions on researcher travel and communication, rep -
resent a fundamentally different mode of operation.
Whether these trade-offs are warranted depends on the value of the specific insights an organization aims
to protect and the capabilities of adversaries the organization expects to face. Organizations considering ISL4
or ISL5 protections would likely need to explore partnerships with the national security community.
Several categories of security measures require significant lead time and are unlikely to be quickly spun
up when circumstances change. Organizations that might need to operate at ISL3 or higher within the next
several years should consider preparing in at least three areas: physical infrastructure (including secure facil-
ities, environmental controls, and access systems), personnel security programs (including screening pipe -
lines, behavioral monitoring, and structured offboarding), and supply chain assurance (including vendor
auditing, SBOM management, and hardware verification). Starting these efforts early preserves optionality
even if the timeline and ultimate ISL remain uncertain.
This report represents a first step toward a systematic understanding of algorithmic insight security. The
threat landscape continues to evolve, and the specific assessments in this report will need to be revisited as
capabilities advance and as organizations gain experience implementing these frameworks. We hope that
this analysis facilitates more-informed decisions about which algorithmic insights warrant protection and at
what level and cost—decisions that might prove consequential for competitive positioning, national security,
and the trajectory of AI development itself.

These are known vulnerabilities that have been reported and have working patches, but some systems remain
unpatched because of lax update policies or because of the use of products for which the vendor is no longer
providing patches.
As detailed in Appendix A of SMW, this vector remains one of the most common entry points for attackers
of all capability levels. For algorithmic insights specifically, the distributed nature of research environments
(in which developers might use personal devices, work from home, or operate in less controlled environments
than production data centers) creates expanded opportunities for the exploitation of unpatched systems.
Exploitation of Reported but Not Fully Patched Vulnerabilities
These are known vulnerabilities for which the vendor has either not provided a patch or provided a patch that
only partially addressed the issue. During the window in which vulnerabilities remain unpatched, malicious
actors can readily exploit them because their existence is already known.
SMW provides extensive examples, including the Spectre and Meltdown vulnerabilities and various
virtual private network (VPN)–secure sockets layer flaws. For insights security, this vector is particularly
concerning because research environments often prioritize functionality and collaboration over aggres -
sive patching cycles, and the broad diversity of software in ML development stacks creates numerous
potential gaps.
The Finding and Exploitation of Individual Zero-Days
Zero-days are vulnerabilities that a vendor or the broader cybersecurity community has not yet identified.
Therefore, patches do not exist, and it is harder to detect their exploitation.
As documented in SMW, individual zero-days can be bought in markets for prices between $10,000 and
$2.5 million depending on the platform. According to a 2017 RAND report, the mean time from initial
detection of a zero-day by an exploit developer to public disclosure by an outside party is 6.9 years.

Undermining the Access Control System Itself
An attacker could use various tools to undermine the mechanisms responsible for reliably providing (or
withholding) access. We use the term access control system loosely to include any security component that is
responsible for preventing unauthorized access to data, such as encryption, authentication, and the permis -
sions system.
The Authentication or Bypass of Vulnerabilities (in the Access Control System)
This vector entails breaking the existing encryption or authentication scheme underlying the security of the
access control system. The most direct approach is finding a flaw that enables someone to decrypt a secret,
forge credentials, or gain access to a resource despite not being an authorized user.
SMW provides extensive technical details on this vector, including examples from Wired Equivalent Pri -
vacy algorithm vulnerabilities, collision attacks against the message-direct algorithm MD5, the Logjam vul -
nerability, and the SolarWinds breach. These apply equally to insight security.
Access to Secret Material That Undermines a Protocol
An attacker can gain illegitimate access to a key or other secret that undermines the reliability of a protocol
or platform, even if that secret does not belong to the victim. This might involve gaining access to the private
key of a certificate authority or to parameters used to generate code-signing keys.
As documented in SMW, examples include the Comodo and DigiNotar certificate authority breaches and
the Content Scramble System key exposure. This vector is equally relevant for insight security.
Bypassing the Access Control System Altogether
Attackers might not need to cope with the systems in place for security, authentication, or controlling access
if they can find an alternative path to the information they seek.
Incorrect Configuration or Security-Policy Implementation
Configuration errors might unintentionally enable access. Similarly, a security policy might be established
but not actually implemented. For instance, a database could be accidentally left accessible even though it
contains sensitive information.
SMW documents this vector extensively, noting that more than 11 percent of reported U.S. data breaches
involved unintentional disclosure by the breached organizations themselves.

AI-Specific Attack Vectors
Some attacks target AI infrastructure itself and are only relevant to AI systems. Because AI is a relatively new

state of the field likely implies that AI infrastructure is more vulnerable than other types of infrastructure

Adversaries might exploit preexisting vulnerabilities in the ML software and hardware stacks to execute
malicious code or otherwise undermine security. This is an AI-specific subset of the running unauthorized

the existing state of ML infrastructure) and more ML expertise.
Prompt-Triggered Code Execution
This vector consists of bypassing restrictions of a model that can execute code by specially crafting prompts
to manipulate the model’s behavior (i.e., prompt injection ). Some prompt-injection attacks might be used to
execute code and overlap with code-execution attacks.
As documented in SMW, examples include the MathGPT vulnerability and prompt-injection vulnerabili-
ties in LangChain. For insight security, AI coding assistants and research tools with code-execution capabili-
ties represent potential attack pathways.

The answer to each query of a model bears information on the weights that helped produce the answer. At
least in theory (and to some extent in practice), someone could infer weights or additional information from
examining enough query responses.
SMW documents this vector, noting that experts varied widely in how plausible they thought such attacks
were and that researchers have successfully extracted embedding matrices of OpenAI models using typical

Bribes and Cooperation
People can be paid or persuaded to take actions on behalf of an adversary. This includes getting an authorized

obtaining information and continuous access to people with information.

If premises are well secured, attackers could try to access an organization’s devices in other locations by
breaking into an employee’s home, stealing or accessing a laptop in a public space, or using false pretenses to
access devices left in hotel rooms.
As detailed in SMW, this vector includes both opportunistic theft and sophisticated operations. Coun -
tries often access people’s devices at airports under the guise of a security inspection, and capable actors have
infrastructure to access devices left in hotel rooms.

Malicious Placement of Portable Device Implants
A common strategy is to leave devices (e.g., USB flash drives, hard drives, computer peripherals) around the
target facility or in locations that employees frequent. These devices could be marked with a company’s logo
to give them credibility, or they could be left unmarked.
As documented in SMW, while awareness of these risks is slowly increasing, so are attackers’ capabilities.
USB cables with hidden chips that provide advanced access are available for as little as $180.
Direct Physical Access
If no exceptional security practices are in place, an attacker can often directly access physical servers or
devices. This might involve social engineering, breaking in, impersonating those with legitimate access, or
achieving authorized access by acting as maintenance or service workers.
SMW documents examples of this vector: The Stuxnet operation allegedly involved a mechanic plug -
ging a USB device into control systems, and attackers posed as janitors to install keyloggers at Sumitomo
Mitsui Bank.
Removal of Devices and Storage
Employees might remove devices and storage from sensitive areas either inadvertently or to share informa -
tion, or adversaries with access might remove devices from an organization’s premises and control. This can
be done in tandem with other vectors, such as the “Bribes and Extortion,” “Risks from Former Employees,”
“Front Operations and Ruses,” or “Direct Physical Access” vectors.
For algorithmic insights, this vector has heightened relevance because insights can be found on laptops,
USB drives, and other portable devices.

Evasion of Physical Access Control Systems
Many physical security systems rely critically on digital systems (e.g., cameras, alert systems, digital locks).

Armed Break-In
This vector is a variation on the “Direct Physical Access” vector but requires more-capable agents who are
better trained in gaining access and have broader toolsets, including using violence if necessary.
SMW notes that this vector is significantly more costly and higher risk than simple direct physical access
attacks.
Military Takeover
At the extreme end of physical attacks, an attacker might use military force to take over a facility.
As documented in SMW, during the 2021 coup in Myanmar, the military directed armed soldiers to break
into data centers nationwide.
Penetration of Hardware Security
In high-security contexts, some devices will have physical hardware protections (e.g., hardware security
modules, smartcards). An attacker would need to overcome physical hurdles to access information. Capable
actors have a large toolset, including drilling, EM probing, acid dissolving, clock glitching, freezing, and
timing or power attacks.
SMW provides detailed examples, including cold boot attacks, integrated circuit decapsulating, and laser
fault injection.
Shoulder Surfing
Shoulder surfing is the direct, in-person observation of sensitive information visible on a screen or in a work-
space. It is often opportunistic, low cost, and difficult to detect—especially in open offices, shared work -
spaces, and conferences.
This vector is specific to algorithmic insight security and was not in SMW.

APPENDIX D
Progressive Compartmentalization Across Insight
Security Levels
Compartmentalization represents the most significant departure from weight-security approaches, and we
present it as both an aspiration and a pragmatic compromise. 
The aspiration is to centralize and strictly control sensitive insights, thereby minimizing their distribu -
tion and the amount of surface area exposed to theft. The compromise is recognizing that, unlike weights,
insights will be extremely difficult to fully centralize.
Securing algorithmic insights requires managing multiple distinct categories of information with differ -
ent sensitivity and access requirements. In practice, compartment boundaries can be drawn around differ -
ences in access needs and medium (e.g., code versus documents versus tacit knowledge) and tightened for
techniques with unusually high transferability or shelf life. The progressive compartmentalization measures
across ISLs—from organizational policies to technical enforcement to stricter cryptographic separations to,
finally, full architectural isolation—reflect the unique challenge of protecting diverse insights while enabling
authorized work.
The Compartmentalization Challenge
SMW isolates a single crown-jewel asset (model weights) from unauthorized access. Insight security must
instead separate multiple categories of insights and rigorously control information flows between them.
Organizations should determine their own compartmentalization structure that is based on their spe -
cific operational contexts, capability portfolios, and risk assessments. The number of compartments, their
boundaries, and which insights belong in each compartment will vary significantly across organizations.
As an illustrative example, an organization might choose to maintain strict boundaries between the
following:
•
 no
vel architecture designs and training-data processing techniques
• in
ference-optimization methods and deployment-infrastructure details
• res
earch prototypes and production-system implementations
• ex
isting capabilities and future research directions
• al
gorithmic innovations that are applicable to general AI development versus innovations that are spe -
cific to potentially dangerous capabilities.
However, other organizations might reasonably adopt different compartmentalization schemes
depending on their threat models, operational structures, and the nature of their research portfolios.
These boundaries serve two purposes: limiting the “blast radius” from any single compromise and reduc-
ing the number of personnel who possess complete knowledge of an organization’s most sensitive techniques.
If an adversary gains access to one compartment, strong compartmentalization ensures that the adversary

Illustrative Scenarios of Compartmentalization in Practice
The following scenarios illustrate how compartmentalization operates at each ISL. Each scenario traces the
same moment at a hypothetical frontier AI company (“FrontierOrg”): the training-process team has devel -
oped a proprietary training recipe (a specific combination of techniques and configurations) that allows the
lab to train models to a given capability level using substantially less compute than what competitors use.
This recipe resides in the Training Process compartment. A researcher on the Data Curation team, Dr. Chen,
needs to understand how the recipe’s efficiency depends on the training data’s composition because her team
is making decisions about what data to prioritize next.
Baseline: No Compartmentalization
FrontierOrg has no formal compartmentalization. Dr. Chen sends a Slack message to a colleague on the
Training Process team who pastes the full training recipe—including the specific techniques, hyperparam -
eter configurations, and the internal analysis that produced them—into a shared Slack channel that is visible
to dozens of employees. Dr. Chen downloads the document to her laptop.
The complete training recipe is now on a personal device, was transmitted over a commercial messaging
platform, and sits in a channel visible to people with no operational need for it. An attacker who compromises
any one of those accounts—or Dr. Chen’s laptop—obtains an insight worth hundreds of millions of dollars
in compute savings. A state-sponsored actor running a spear phishing campaign needs to compromise only
a single employee with access to the Slack channel. There is no logging of who accessed what, no detection
mechanism, and no way to determine after the fact how many copies of this recipe exist.
Insight Security Level 1: Current Best Practice
FrontierOrg has standard cybersecurity hygiene, including single sign-on processes, MFA, endpoint protec -
tion, and encrypted enterprise communications, but it does not have compartmentalization. Dr. Chen mes-
sages her colleague on the managed enterprise platform, which logs the exchange. She accesses the Training
Process team’s internal repository and reads the full recipe.
The infrastructure is secured: Communications are encrypted and logged, documents are centralized,
and only authenticated employees can reach internal systems. But any authenticated employee can access any
insight. An attacker who compromises a single employee’s credential via phishing or credential stuffing gains
access to the same repositories that Dr. Chen browsed. The attack surface is the entire employee population,
and a single compromised account exposes insights across all domains.

Enforcement
Compartments are now designated by nondescriptive codewords (e.g., the codeword for the Training Pro -

filesystem-level tagging enforces I/O restrictions on insight-bearing data.
Dr. Chen never sees an ALPINE artifact directly. The ALPINE compartment owner authorizes a medi -
ated transfer: An ALPINE-cleared researcher prepares a sanitized summary describing how the recipe’s effi -
ciency varies by data composition without revealing the recipe’s actual techniques or configurations. This
summary is transferred through a logged cross-compartment channel, tagged with its origin and classifica -

An attacker who fully compromises SUMMIT’s environment obtains a sanitized description of data-
composition sensitivity, not the recipe itself. More importantly, the monitoring system creates a new obstacle:
An attacker attempting to systematically exfiltrate insights by requesting material from multiple compart -

is itself a signal. A sophisticated insider threat (perhaps an employee recruited by a foreign intelligence ser -
vice) would face a paper trail of compartment-specific NDAs, logged transfers, and monitored information
flows that would constrain the insider threat’s ability to collect information broadly without detection.

Each compartment operates in its own cryptographic domain with separate encryption keys and separate
key-management systems. Kernel-level DLP with file tainting tracks insight-derived data across the file

Dr. Chen’s request now requires dual approval from two authorized ALPINE personnel. The sanitized
summary is fingerprinted with a unique marker and cryptographically re-encrypted from ALPINE’s key

even root access to every SUMMIT server—yields nothing about ALPINE because ALPINE’s data are

hoc attribution, and if the material surfaces in a competitor’s systems or on the open internet, it will trace
back to a specific transfer, recipient, and date.

Insight Security Level 5: Physical and Personnel Compartmentalization
ALPINE and SUMMIT researchers work in physically separated, isolated facilities with emanation shield -
ing and stringent access controls. Staff cannot hold concurrent clearances for multiple top-level compart -
ments, and personnel transferring between compartments are required to undergo cooling-off periods. No
remote work on algorithmic insights is allowed, and supply chains are compartmentalized across vendors
and infrastructure.
Dr. Chen cannot contact an ALPINE researcher because she does not know who works in ALPINE, and
ALPINE personnel cannot discuss ALPINE material outside their secure facility. Her request routes through
an administrative channel staffed by personnel cleared for metadata but not technical content. Within
ALPINE’s secure area, two researchers prepare the sanitized summary under multiperson controls, which a
security officer then reviews. The transfer occurs under a two-person rule via controlled removable media at
a designated physical handoff point. The media is sanitized after transfer.
For a sophisticated state-level adversary—the kind of threat actor that ISL5 is designed to resist—the
attack surface has collapsed almost entirely. There is no digital pathway between compartments: no shared
network, no shared infrastructure, no shared supply chain. A cyberoperation that compromises SUMMIT’s
entire digital environment, no matter how sophisticated the operation is, learns nothing about ALPINE. The
adversary must mount a separate physical or HUMINT operation against each compartment independently
via recruiting or coercing personnel who work exclusively in that compartment, or the adversary must pen -
etrate an isolated, physically hardened facility. Even a successfully recruited insider can access only their own
compartment. The cost to the adversary scales with the number of compartments rather than collapsing to
a single point of compromise.

governance strategies to inform national programs and institutional decisionmaking on AI evaluation, risk
