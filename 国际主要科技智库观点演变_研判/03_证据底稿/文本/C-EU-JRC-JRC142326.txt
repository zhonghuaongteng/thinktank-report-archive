Abstract
This report provides an overview on the state of play for small modular reactors (SMRs) in a techno-
economic perspective by presenting a preliminary mapping of the SMR ecosystem using the Techno-
Economic ecoSystem (TES) approach. This methodology allows for a comprehensive mapping of the
complex ecosystem constituting the SMR industrial landscape. Through the identification of
activities and public and private actors interplaying, we can outline the EU strategic positioning as
well as interdependencies, vulnerabilities, and opportunities for the development of these types of
advanced reactors. The study offers insights on the EU role in the SMR network through
collaboration and participation in the information flows.
The main findings exhibit the critical need to transform European research into innovation to stay at
the competitive forefront. A well-developed research network is a valuable asset to keep up with
SMR technological progress, but the EU needs to foster its business network. Observing SMR
activities that are related to other industrial ecosystems, we highlight the importance of these
reactors to support the development and decarbonisation of energy-intensive industries and meet
other objectives of the Clean Industrial Deal.
While the present study offers a first overview of the results of the SMR techno-economic
ecosystem mapping, the TES approach allows for exploring further analytical avenues and fine-
tuning classification criteria. This novel analysis could provide strategic guidance to support public
policies and international collaboration initiatives for further SMR development and deployment.

Executive summary
The world needs more power. The International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that global energy
demand will increase more than 6% –or around 0.5% annually– by 2035 compared to 2023 levels
(IEA, 2024b). At the same time, current geopolitical tensions and the political pledge to contain
global warming have led to a push for a clean energy transition. Reducing fossil fuel dependency is
not only environmentally necessary but also relevant for strategic autonomy. Nuclear energy can
generate clean power, combine hybrid energy systems with renewable sources, provide baseload
power to stabilise the electricity grid, and increase energy security through locally-produced SMR
power that reduces exposure to global energy markets. These capacities are providing new nuclear
technologies with momentum (IEA, 2022).
Small modular reactors, or SMRs, are advanced nuclear reactors that incorporate state-of-the-art
features into production and safety systems. There is growing interest around the globe in SMR
technology owing to its flexibility, adaptability and low cost. They can be mass produced and
provide up to 300 megawatts of electric capacity per unit, equivalent to one third of traditional
nuclear reactors.
According to the Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA), there are at least five operational SMRs –at least
two known reactors in Russia, two in China and an experimental one in Japan–in the world (NEA,
2024b). Even in the most conservative scenario with the current policy settings, cumulative
investment estimates in SMRs amount to USD 30 billion by 2050 (IEA, 2025b). This capital would
translate in an increased deployed SMR capacity of 40 GW by 2050. In the EU, there are 15
countries with active interest in SMRs. A growing number of countries outside the EU also share an
interest in the development of SMRs. The US, Canada, China, Japan, Russia and the UK are the most
advanced countries in the development of SMRs, although most of their projects are in the design
phase. Many developing nations such as India have also expressed interest in this technology.
Policy context
The Draghi report outlined three major actions as the EU’s roadmap for sustainable growth: closing
the innovation gap with China and the US, decarbonising the economy while keeping up with global
competitiveness, and reducing dependencies (Draghi, 2024). To achieve these objectives, it is
necessary to renovate the EU common energy market.
SMRs offer opportunities to contribute addressing these objectives. Europe needs energy to power
up the digital transformation and close the innovation gap. These advanced reactors provide clean
energy and offer the possibility to combine with other renewable energy sources, providing stable
energy supply in the path towards a clean Europe. In addition, their scalability, modularity and size
make them a suitable option to reduce energy costs and reach isolated areas where energy is
expensive. For example, large nuclear plants impose sizeable construction costs. Investors must
often bear the bulk of these sunk construction and performance investment risks. SMRs permit a
more incremental investment than larger units of the past. They also claim to be a particularly
suitable and affordable option for non-electrical applications such as hydrogen production (IAEA,
2024c).
This report presents the state of play in the SMR techno-economic ecosystem at the global and EU
level, with the aim of supporting informed policy decisions on how to better support SMR
development and deployment in the context of a new Clean Industrial Deal and productivity boost
(European Commission and Leyen, 2024).

Key conclusions
— SMRs have the capacity to contribute to achieve EU’s strategic economy objectives, as they
offer a clean, flexible and affordable solution to propel energy generation.
— The SMR ecosystem comprises nearly 4,000 players, 85% of whom are private firms. The rest
of the ecosystem includes about 500 research institutes and a relatively small number of
government institutions.
— In the EU, the number of private firms comprises less than 80% of the total number of players.
This share drops even further when excluding participants in EU-funded projects.
— The share of EU business activities (excluding EU-funded activities) is in line with the US and
other Western countries, and larger than in Russia and Asian countries. The EU also dedicates
more activities to research than the global average. However, the share of innovation activities
in the EU is below 60%.
— At the global level, the focus is on innovation activities. Apart from the UK, more than 75% of
the activities of all major EU competitors such as China and the US are dedicated to innovation.
UK players allocate more activities into both business and research. The activity composition
depicts an industry that prioritises the protection of innovation and the licencing of commercial
rights for concepts and technological developments prior to production and commercialisation.
— Firms develop and register most patents on their own. Only one in four innovation activities is
collaborative, and science-to-business collaboration accounts for less than 4% of patenting
activities.
— High-temperature gas-cooled reactors (HTGR) and pressurised water reactors (PWR) account for
the highest number of activities among SMR models. Pressure vessels are the most frequently
captured SMR component.
— The EU stands out for the capacity of its research network. The EU leads in scientific
publications, and collaborations within the SMR field. The EU also generates more autonomous
research than any other region. Challenges lie in the EU’s ability to transform its research
excellence into innovation and to expand its business network.
— Member State participation in the European SMR landscape differs, as a large pool of players
are in countries with a positive position on SMRs. EU-wide public support to SMRs would help
optimise EU networks, resources, and knowledge to develop a competitive SMR ecosystem.
— The number of foreign-controlled EU firms has not increased since 2018 despite a growing
number of businesses. Out of the 40 EU firms controlled by non-EU countries, American capital
dominates (45%), whereas China controls three firms.
— The network analysis of the SMR ecosystem confirms a well-connected European scientific
network. However, the EU needs to strengthen international scientific collaboration with leading
institutions to play a strategic role in frontier research.
— The EU has limited influence in innovation knowledge flows across the SMR ecosystem. Similar
to research, the EU needs to establish strategic partnerships in patenting activities as a previous
step to commercial development.
— SMRs have a potential to play a key role in supporting energy-intensive industries.

6
— While SMRs can help to meet energy demand from the digital transformation, they can also
benefit from innovation in areas such as AI and cybersecurity.
Related and future JRC work
The European Commission Joint Research Centre has a portfolio of activities dedicated to small
modular reactors. Portfolios bring together scientific disciplines from across the JRC and, through a
cross-disciplinary approach, create new insights, new knowledge and new methods to better
coordinate policy support. The SMR portfolio helps EU Member States to assess the benefits of
SMRs, examine the capacity and long-term sustainability of the supply chain for components and
critical materials, and evaluate the impact on decarbonisation, the industry, and energy security.
Future related work would further explore activities and players, considering a selection of
categories, and examine firm characteristics.

1 Introduction
Nuclear policy has been present since the very beginning of the European Union. The six founding
nations signed the Treaty on the European Atomic Energy Community, or Euratom Treaty, in 19570F
1.
It is one of the three founding treaties establishing the EU and all Member States are party to it.
Over the years, the Euratom Treaty has remained largely unchanged. Euratom secondary legislation
establishes common rules and standards on the use of nuclear energy – Article 194(2) of the Treaty
on the Functioning of the EU
1F
2–. However, each Member State independently decides whether to
include nuclear energy in its own energy mix.
Currently 12 out of 27 EU Member States – Belgium, Bulgaria, Czechia, Finland, France, Hungary,
The Netherlands, Romania, Slovakia, Slovenia, Spain, and Sweden – host nuclear power plants in
their territories. Furthermore, other countries such as Poland are proposing to develop nuclear
energy for the first time. According to Eurostat data, nuclear energy made up 16.4% of the EU
energy mix in 2024, accounting for 23.4% of the total electricity produced2F
3.
The EU’s ambition to reach net-zero emissions by 2050 is a major challenge for incoming and
future Commissions (European Commission, 2025). This objective requires a very low carbon energy
system, based on both renewable and nuclear energies, the two backbones of the future energy
mix. The deployment of small modular nuclear reactors (henceforth, SMRs) across the EU could help
countries reduce their dependence on fossil fuels, complement existing nuclear and renewable
power plants, and contribute to Europe’s future energy security. SMRs also offer several types of
configurations and temperature ranges to create heat that can be used directly, or generate
electricity, to offer mobility and decarbonise hard-to-abate sectors such as steel, cement, chemical,
and refining.
This report leverages the Techno-Economic ecoSystem (TES) approach to map the worldwide SMR
ecosystem, provides a first look at the global SMR industrial landscape and highlights EU strategic
interdependencies, vulnerabilities, and opportunities
3F
4. It highlights where the EU has invested in
SMRs and presents a network analysis of global SMR players to zoom in on innovation hubs and
gatekeepers. This exploratory analysis could be used as a first step to further investigate supply
chain dependencies, analyse them in relation to public perception and engagement, and provide
strategic guidance to support government policies and international collaborations for SMR
development and deployment.
Under today’s global policies, world energy demand estimates suggest a 9% and 20% increase by
2030 and 2050 compared to 2023 levels, respectively (IEA, 2024b). However, these estimates
might increase further depending on the pace of digitalisation, particularly in economies fostering
data-driven AI tools

in nuclear power between 40% and 250% by 2050. These numbers reflect the relevance of nuclear

Drivers for the development and deployment of SMRs are also coming out in a growing number of

first global stocktake under the Paris Agreement, brought nuclear energy back in the spotlight, as
nuclear was declared as a low-emission technology (UNFCC, 2023). More particularly, the interest in
SMRs rests mainly in their claimed flexibility, feasibility and capability to combine with other energy
sources, such as variable renewable energy. The reduced size of these reactors makes them in
principle more affordable, thus reaching a wider range of users, from smaller countries to private
companies. Their modularity would allow for on-site installation even in small and remote locations.
Moreover, SMRs incorporate advanced passive safety systems, which make them more appealing
for users with less experience in nuclear power. The potential hazards of reactor meltdown with
subsequent ecological and humanitarian consequences, issues with nuclear waste disposal, and
fissile material diversion and proliferation have often led to criticism on nuclear energy fission.
SMRs comprise scalable nuclear power plant designs that could reduce investment risks through
incremental capacity expansion and proportional risk management as they contain substantially
smaller radioactive inventory (Kessides and Kuznetsov, 2012).
The IEA estimates that SMRs could provide a total capacity of approximately 40 gigawatts (GWe) by
2050 in the most conservative scenario. In the most ambitious scenario, which sets out a pathway
for the global energy sector to achieve net zero carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions by 2050, there
would be more than 1 500 reactors, with a total capacity of nearly 200 GWe (IEA, 2025b). The US,
Canada, China, Japan, Russia and the UK currently adopt the most advanced SMR technology,
although most of their projects are in the design stage of development. Many developing nations
have also expressed their interest in this technology (IAEA, 2022). Indonesia, Saudi Arabia and

Box 1. What are Small Modular Reactors?7F
8
According to the IAEA, small modular reactors are reactors incorporating advanced engineered features
with a maximum output of 300 Megawatt electric (MWe) – while microreactors are any SMR under 10 MWe
– and can produce up to 7.2 million kilowatt-hours (kWh) per day (IAEA, 2023b). While SMR is the generic
term for these reactors, advanced modular reactors (AMRs) refer to designs based on non-light water
technology. They all harness nuclear fission reactions to generate heat that can be used as-is or for the
generation of electricity. The Generation IV International Forum (GIF) selected six concepts that target
improved safety, sustainability, efficiency and costs (WNA, 2024a). The designs, which represent 'the future
shape of nuclear energy', are the gas-cooled fast reactor, the lead-cooled fast reactor, the molten salt
reactor, the sodium-cooled fast reactor, the supercritical-water-cooled reactor and the very high-
temperature reactor.
Compared to conventional reactors, their small and modular features make SMRs and microreactors a
potential flexible and tailored solution especially when it is not possible to use nuclear power reactors. Due
to their smaller size, SMR developers claim that they need less space and less cooling water, while their

also deployable either as a single or multi-module plant. Small size and modularity also make SMRs easy

SMRs also incorporate advanced safety features and passive safety systems. Theoretically, these systems

1.1 The strategic relevance of SMRs
The EU’s new political agenda envisions a renovated single market that integrates critical sectors,
such as digital, energy and finance (European Commission and Leyen, 2024). The lack of integration
of these areas within the single market currently represents a major hurdle to European
competitiveness (Letta, 2024). The Draghi report also hints at restructuring the common energy
market as a critical element in accomplishing the necessary actions in the EU’s roadmap for
sustainable growth. In combination with other measures, these proposed changes will support the
completion of three main actions: 1) closing innovation gaps with China and the US 2) decarbonising
the economy; 3) and aiming to keep up with global competitiveness and reducing dependencies
8F
9.
Nuclear energy, specifically SMRs, offers an opportunity to address these needs.
Today, the prosperity of societies is strongly associated with access to reliable and affordable
energy, mainly in the form of heat, electricity and hydrogen. Energy enables effective food
production; ensures clean water; provides lighting, heating, and cooling for homes and buildings;
supports industrial processes; and powers transportation, communication and entertainment. As the
world population continues to grow, human right institutions increasingly recognise access to energy
as a fundamental right for the people.
Megatrend urbanisation also exacerbates the movement to higher energy consumption density. The
extensive use of renewable energy alone has difficulties in keeping pace with this trend. Even in
concert with energy efficiency measures, renewable sources cannot provide the high energy
densities of fossil fuels and nuclear energy.
The digital revolution goes hand in hand with a dramatic rise in the energy needed to build,
transport, deploy and power software and hardware systems. The International Energy Agency
estimates that global data centres alone will need more than 800 terawatt hours of power by 2026
(IEA, 2024a). This is double the amount required by 2022. SMRs offer a means to address
sustainability concerns that may undermine the productivity gains and benefits to social well-being
of the green and digital twin transitions. With the acceleration of climate change, European citizens
and politicians are becoming increasingly aware of the negative impacts of continued reliance on

By enhancing the understanding of the SMR ecosystem, this study aims at providing policy-relevant
insights to ongoing discussions on the industry’s state of play. To do so, the study highlights the
predominant advanced reactor models in the global ecosystem and the most common technologies
across leading players. The goal is to facilitate an informed reflection on how to use European
resources for the deployment of SMRs. This report can also inform the European Industrial Alliance
on SMRs by providing an overview of the SMR industry in terms of technologies, global competitors
and the impact of EU funding programmes.
The JRC’s Digital Economy Unit has developed and applied the TES analytical approach to map and
explore the complex dynamics of industry’s segments driven by rapidly evolving emerging
technologies that are difficult to capture using traditional metrics or classifications. In this study, we
apply the TES methodology to the quickly developing SMR industry, in order to be able to produce
new metrics and insights at the global and EU level. This methodology relies on natural language
processing methods and a system of keywords and queries used to define SMR activities, players
and technologies (Calza et al., 2022). Keywords are curated by experts and extracted from relevant
literature. The keyword list is provided in Table 2 of Annex 1. To avoid ambiguity and potential
false positives, search queries are often constraint by additional SMR-specific keywords, like
“reactor”, “advanced nuclear” or “generation IV”. This is particularly relevant as nuclear technologies
and processes can have applications in other industries, such as aerospace and defence.
Activities are identified by several micro-level data sources and categorized into three groups based
on their nature and outcomes (De Prato et al., 2019; Righi et al., 2020):
(a) Business activities are derived from companies’ information on core operations,
encompassing the production, supply or exchange of goods and services, or
investments. Company data are obtained from multiple data sources (see Calza et
al. 2022) for details).
(b) Innovation activities consist of priority patent application filings obtained from
EPO´s PATSTAT database
17F
18.
(c) Research activities, referring to scientific publications in top journals, proxying
frontier research. These activities are extracted from a set of publications from the
Scopus repository18F
19.
Players stand for entities that are engaging in the above-mentioned activities across the SMR
ecosystem, in the sense that they participate in the design, development, deployment and
commercialization of knowledge and products (goods or services) within the boundaries of SMR
technologies. In identifying players, emphasis is placed on organizations rather than individuals, i.e.
the applicant or developing organization in the context of patents, affiliation instead of individual
authors in conference proceedings and publications, and always within the scope of SMRs.
An example of a player is a company that produces and/or deploys advanced modular reactors, like
Westinghouse Electric Company. An example of an activity is a research publication on the cost-
benefit analysis of the “modular construction” and SMR decommissioning (Mignacca and Locatelli,
2020).

The TES mapping of the SMR ecosystem offers a global picture of research, innovation, and
business activities related to SMR technologies from 2009 to 2023, and of the related actors. By
merging several sources of micro data19F
20 into a graph database where the connections across
datasets enrich and amplify the information contained in each individual source, thus proxying the
structure of a complex ecosystem, the application of the TES approach offers a wealth of
information on the global SMR landscape. The generated database enables the production of a set
of indicators and metrics that provide a compelling characterisation of the key elements of the SMR
ecosystem and of their interlinkages (Calza et al., 2022; Calza et al., 2023).
Therefore, the TES approach allows for several analytical avenues, to address policy-relevant
questions regarding the design, diffusion and deployment of SMR technologies and components.
The ultimate goal is to provide necessary evidence-based insights that support policy initiatives
aimed at strengthening the competitiveness of the SMR industry.

9 255 activities. The mapping exercise captures the growing interest in SMRs over time. The growth

reactors (PWR), the most common SMR design (Figure 2). Other SMR types are in the top-10 list of

reactors (LWR). All these types of reactors are bellwethers in the SMR field, as their design started
back in the late 1940s.

2.2 The strategic position of the EU on Small Modular Reactors
One of the main challenges the EU faces for a smooth development of the SMR industry is the lack
of unanimous consensus. This is reflected in their National Energy and Climate Plans (NECPs) and in
the current differences in deployment of nuclear infrastructure. Germany, Greece, Ireland, Italy and
Austria have no operating nuclear reactors. Germany has historically opposed nuclear energy for a
long time and closed its three remaining nuclear plants in April 202327F

power plants in 2011 after a referendum, but it is experiencing renewed interest. Austria banned

a nuclear phase-out. On 15 May 2025, the Belgian Chamber of Representatives approved the
amendment to the Nuclear Phase-Out Law. However, European public opinion and certain
governments seem to be changing their positions. The Project Europe Research poll in nuclear
energy shows that EU citizens’ opinion about nuclear energy has significantly improved since
202228F

2.3 Network Analysis of the SMR ecosystem
The granularity of the microdata used to generate the database of players and activities in the SMR
ecosystem allows for the identification of specific groups of players or entities and assigns them to
roles and positions. These assignments let the TES approach build a representation of the global
network (Calza et al., 2022). Nodes, aggregated by geographical area, give shape to the SMR
network when considering their collaborations in research and innovation activities – that is, co-
authorship of scientific publications or co-patenting across geographical areas33F
34. By forming a web
of collaborative research and innovation activities, these explicit and formal interconnections depict
the SMR ecosystem as a network of interdependent entities connected by a flow of knowledge.
The network projection thus delineates the features and structure of the SMR ecosystem as a whole
or for specific groups, and at various levels of aggregation, such as individuals or geographical area.
The analysis of the structure of the network can reveal how knowledge flows through and across its
parts, detecting whether certain nodes occupy a particularly relevant and strategic position (i.e.
bottlenecks, gatekeepers) and whether this may reveal the existence of important relations and
connections from the perspective of strategic autonomy.

Next, the TES approach can identify players that act as “bridges” and “control points.” The indicator
for betweenness centrality goes a step forward by measuring the influence and control that a node
can exert on the rest of the SMR ecosystem (strategic position). In other words, this indicator proxies
the ability of a node to influence the rest of the network by acting as an intermediary. This is
strategic in two ways. First, a node with high betweenness centrality acts as a bridge connecting
non-otherwise connected players in the network whose structure would be changed and disrupted
without an intermediary node. Second, this node also acts as a bottleneck, as it can exert a relevant
influence on the rest of the network and can steer, control or even interrupt the knowledge flowing
to and from other nodes (Righi et al., 2021; Samoili et al., 2020).

3 The influence of SMRs on industrial ecosystems
SMRs can contribute to the development of other industrial ecosystems by providing a stable supply
of energy. Looking at the industrial composition of SMRs is a useful and policy-relevant exercise, as
it can help design better policies for the EU’s industrial competitiveness. The presence of SMR
activities offers a raw indication of the “critical mass” of SMR capabilities in a given industrial
ecosystem. In this section, we crosscheck the data on SMRs with those of each industrial ecosystem,
to identify the level of integration across them34F
35.
This analysis reveals the specialised nature of SMRs. Only 15% of SMR activities are strictly linked
to others belonging to one of the industrial ecosystems. In other words, only a small percentage of
SMR’s activities are related or applied to an industrial domain different for the strictly considered
SMR one. Figure 14 shows the distribution of SMR activities showing an overlap with activities in
industrial ecosystems. As expected, SMR activities mostly fall within the industrial ecosystems
associated with power generation.
‘Energy-intensive Industries’ is the most concurrent industrial ecosystem with 43% of overlapping
activities. SMRs are reliable tools in energy-intensive industries. This is also the case for digital and
agri-food ecosystems. Growing energy demands from data centres are a major opportunity for
SMRs (see sub-3.232). Companies such as NuScale are collaborating with energy solution firms to
explore the use of SMRs as a reliable, low-carbon power source for data centres. The agri-food
ecosystem also relies on energy-intensive operations. This is the case for food processing, which
requires energy for heating, cooling, and electricity. Drying agricultural produce uses process-
heating systems like heat pumps and desalination systems used for irrigation in water-scarce areas.
Such processes require vast amounts of energy.
To reduce energy dependency and ensure decarbonisation, the EU also needs to increase hydrogen
production at the required large (Mt/year) scale. Hydrogen is particularly valuable as both feedstock
and reactant to decarbonise hard-to-abate sectors in heavy industries (steel, cement, chemistry,
and fertilisers) and transportation (aviation and maritime transport). A recent study demonstrated
the cost-effectiveness of SMRs in hydrogen production
35F
36. However, deploying and integrating SMRs
into an independent energy market requires massive initial investment in Europe’s infrastructure
networks.
The second-largest overlap is with the ‘Renewable Energy’ ecosystem. This ecosystem includes
hydrogen production, where experts believe that small and microreactors can be a critical source of
power supply. In addition, SMR developers claim that their models can be plugged together with
renewable energy sources in energy systems. This feature would allow SMRs to function as
complementary components in the transition to a low-carbon economy. Thus, SMRs can
theoretically provide stability and resilience to the energy system during the twin transition, where
the energy-intensive digital transformation relies on hybrid energy systems. That is, when a
renewable energy supply is paired with small nuclear reactors.

3.1 A relevant supplier for energy-intensive industries
European Member States are increasingly interested in maintaining their existing critical energy-
intensive industries (EIIs) infrastructure for geostrategic reasons. Industries such as steel, chemistry,
cement, fertilisers, pharmaceutical products can effectively help countering the disadvantages of
globalisation, thus granting a higher degree of strategic technology autonomy. Furthermore,
competitiveness in these industries can prevent the loss of large investments to countries outside
the EU and the relocation of industry branches to regions with lower energy costs. These regions
also tend to have less restrictive emissions policies, and thus relocation can lead to carbon leakage.
The EU could thus preserve skills and jobs within its borders, and the industry could continue to
produce according to European standards and ethics (e.g., avoidance of child labour).
In the following analytical exercise, we identify activities that fall concurrently within the domains
of EIIs and SMRs (Figure 15). From the activities, we extract the players –firms, research centres or
government institutions– participating in them and the registered location. China accounts for 59%
of the identified activities, most of them related to patenting. Like the SMR ecosystem, the activity
composition in the overlap with EIIs is dominated by innovation with 81% of the activities. Russia,
Japan and South Korea also dedicate most EIIs activities to innovation. The captured business
activities in this domain are, in broad terms, equally distributed across regions.
Except for China, the EU shows a superior performance compared to other competitors. However, it
struggles to convert research into innovation. It is the only region with the largest share of activities
allocated into research. At the country level, Germany and France lead in number of EII-SMR
overlap. Germany has more research institutes, while France has more firms than any other EU
country. French firms working at the junction with EIIs includes Cyclife Digital Solutions –formerly
Oreka Solutions–, a subsidiary of the EDF Group.

3.2 Increasing interest in SMRs by digital leaders
To close the innovation gap, Europe needs energy, including for its digital transformation. The frenzy
of Artificial Intelligence (AI) development partly explains this rise. Data centres underpinning AI
models require additional power. The International Energy Agency expects that global data centres
alone will need more than double the electricity they currently consume, with estimated
consumption around 945 TWh by 2030 (IEA, 2025a). In Europe –the EU plus Norway, Switzerland
and the UK–, power demand for data centres is expected to grow to approximately 35 GW by 2030,
up from 10 GW in 2024 (Granskog et al., 2024). To meet this new IT load demand, more than $250
to $300 billion of investment will be needed in data centre infrastructure, excluding power
generation capacity.
This rise in energy demand for data-driven tools is boosting private investments from high-tech
industries into SMRs. On 14 October 2024, Google announced the first corporate agreement to
deploy multiple SMRs –six to seven reactors, according to the announcement– in the US, the first to
be deployed by 2030. Google will power its data centres with 500 MWe of the Kairos Power’s
fluoride salt-cooled high-temperature reactor. Governments and other big tech companies such as

4 Conclusions
The global energy landscape is undergoing a significant transformation, with increasing demand for
clean energy sources that at the same time mitigate vulnerabilities and enhance strategic
autonomy. Small modular reactors (SMRs) have emerged as a promising solution, offering a flexible
affordable alternative to traditional large nuclear reactors. As the EU strives to achieve its strategic
economy objectives, SMRs have the potential to contribute firmly to the clean energy transition.
This report provides some details on the current situation in the SMR field and analyses the results
of a first test run using the Techno-Economic ecoSystem (TES) methodology. A set of keywords
identifying SMR reactor types, technologies and components lays the basis for the mapping of
related activities across various databases. From the extracted data, TES builds a graph database
that allows generating a network representation of the ecosystem.
Our TES analysis of the global and EU SMR ecosystem reveals a dynamic landscape, with a growing
number of players, largely consisting of private firms. In total, there are approximately 4 000
players, when also accounting for research institutes, and government entities. China accounts for
nearly 65% of the players and is particularly dominant in innovation. Overall, countries focus on
patenting activities, highlighting the importance of registering designs and technologies prior to
commercialization. While the EU boasts a robust research network and a strong presence in
scientific publications, challenges remain in transforming research excellence into innovation and
expanding the business network. This effect can be observed not only in nuclear but in many other
technology innovations, where Europe is strong in innovation and regulation, but comparatively
weak and slow in economic implementation.
The scoping study presented here goes beyond the JRC’s classical assessments of nuclear safety
and waste for SMR. It clearly shows the high potential and added value of the TES methodology to
analyse the SMR ecosystem, specifically those techno-economic aspects that are crucial for
assessing the credibility of certain SMR concepts and the involved supply chain.
Future application of TES to SMR will refine, broaden and update the exploration of activities and
players, considering a selection of categories, and examine firm characteristics with the aim to
ultimately suggest and support well-informed policy actions.
However, the development and deployment of SMRs also faces significant challenges. Amongst
others, these include:
1. Safety assessment capability and regulatory harmonisation: Several projects at the
IAEA (IAEA, 2023), the OECD-NEA (NEA, 2021), the Generation IV International Forum (its
Risk and Safety Working Group
37F
38) and several Euratom indirect actions (e.g., HARMONISE38F
39)
are dealing with this subject. Harmonised regulatory frameworks at the international and EU
level and clear licensing processes are required to enable the development and deployment
of SMRs (NEA, 2022). In this area, the European Commission recommends an approach that
was successfully tested on the early Nuward SMR design, namely cooperation between
regulators of different EU Member States interested in deploying a specific SMR design.

2. Uncertainty regarding cost and financing: Nuclear energy requires significant upfront

Several SMR demonstration projects are affected  by large cost increa ses and uncertainties.
These uncertainties and the related risks can only be removed once SMRs are built in

overnight cost, access to financing can still be a market entry barrier for developers. A number

3. Public engagement: The attitude of the public regarding nuclear energy is varying strongly
across EU Member States. The debate is mostly on safety and waste, both subjects are
strong elements of the Euratom direct actions performed by the JRC. The scientific technical
results can support a public discussion based on facts.

very different levels of technology readiness. Several of them claim safety and
performance features that still require analytical confirmation and ultimately

cybersecurity threats. Some of their claimed features still need to be proved in practice.
