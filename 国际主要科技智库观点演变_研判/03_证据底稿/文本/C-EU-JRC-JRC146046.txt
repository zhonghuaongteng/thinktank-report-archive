ABSTRACT
Virtual worlds are reshaping the boundaries of human interaction and reality and impacting production
process and business models. This report provides a comprehensive analysis of the global and European
Union (EU) virtual worlds ecosystem, mapping over 88,000 activities across business, innovation,
and research domains led by 68,000 players, including firms, research institutions, and government
organisations.
The EU is the global leader in virtual world related research publications. The EU’s strengths in
foundational research paired with strategies to accelerate innovation-to-market pipelines could foster
cross-sector collaboration and address regional fragmentation.
Globally, venture capital funding into virtual worlds reveals stark disparities. The US predictably
dominates the the virtual worlds landscape, with roughly EUR 17 billion invested by the end of 2024.
China ranks second worldwide with nearly EUR 3 billion invested. While the EU accounts for 17% of the
global share of virtual worlds deals, ahead of China (8%) and second to only the US (40%), it comes in
fourth behind the UK in terms of the total amount invested. Public funding and cross-border ownership
patterns highlight the EU’s growing role as both an investor and recipient of foreign capital.
The report identifies significant concentrations of virtual world activities across industrial ecosystems,
with Creative Cultural Industries, Tourism, and Retail leading adoption both in the EU and worldwide.
However, critical industries like Healthcare, Aerospace & Defence, and Energy-Intensive Industries remain
underutilised with opportunities for EU expansion, despite research on their potential impact across these
industries. The report also presents the matrix of key enabling technologies like Extended Reality (XR), AI
and IoT that comprise global activity in virtual worlds.
By leveraging its research capabilities and fostering strategic alliances, the EU can strengthen its global
competitiveness to shape the next generation of virtual worlds.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The EU’s leadership in virtual world (VW) research
contrasts with its lag in business innovation
and patent activity, raising risks of dependency
on non-EU tech giants and limited influence
over global standards, values, and governance
in immersive environments. While early-stage
sectors like healthcare and mobility show
foundational research and patenting potential,
this has not yet translated into scalable EU-led
ecosystems.
The global landscape is already evolving from
virtual worlds (VWs) to augmented physical
reality (APR), sometimes called personal/humanist
superintelligence, where immersive technologies
are increasingly embedded in everyday life.
Companies like Meta are investing heavily in AR
glasses (e.g., Oculus and upcoming AR headsets)
to bridge digital and physical realities, signalling
a shift from isolated virtual environments to
digitally enhanced physical spaces. This transition
presents both opportunities (e.g., industrial
applications, smart cities) and risks (e.g., data
monopolies, unequal access) for the EU.
The EU’s limited ownership of global VW/APR
firms and high foreign ownership of EU-based
VW companies threaten control over data, IP, and
decision-making in critical value chains. European
start-ups and SMEs face challenges in securing
scale-up funding and avoiding acquisition by
dominant non-EU platforms (e.g., Meta, Apple),
risking long-term strategic autonomy in VR/XR.
The rise of APR technologies (e.g., AR glasses)
may marginalize EU efforts if current strategies
remain narrowly focused on virtual worlds rather
than integrated physical-digital ecosystems.
In presenting these gaps, this report highlights
how the EU could tackle these challenges.
Prioritising foundations in horizontally
integrated innovation ecosystems (e.g. ICT,
manufacturing, and retail) could help accelerate
the commercialisation of EU-developed VW/APR
technologies. The EU could also look to targeting
key sectors (e.g., healthcare, mobility) with sector-
specific funding and public-private partnerships to
bridge research-business gaps.
Securing strategic assets is integral, and this
includes expanding foreign investment screening
to protect EU-based VW/APR firms from hostile
takeovers and ensuring ownership of critical
assets (e.g., IP, data infrastructure). This should
go hand in hand with investment incentives and
strategic equity participation to promote EU VW
business growth.
Internet penetration is a core need to build our
virtual worlds. The EU could look to accelerate
5G/6G deployment and semiconductor
development (via the European Chips Act)
to underpin scalable VW/APR infrastructure.
Furthermore, Horizon Europe and Digital Europe
Programme funding for SMEs and R&D in key
enabling technologies (e.g., advanced displays,
optoelectronics) could be scaled in strategic
sectors, industrial ecosystems, and patents to
support EU virtual world activity.
The EU’s Web 4.0 and VW strategy must evolve
to address the transition from virtual worlds to
augmented physical reality, ensuring European
businesses and users thrive in a rights-respecting,
interoperable digital future. By addressing
ownership gaps, strengthening industrial
ecosystems, and leveraging regulatory leadership,
the EU can secure a competitive, values-driven
position in the global VW/APR landscape.
Main findings
 ▶ The EU makes up the third largest share
(11%) of global virtual world players, and
9% of research publications, priority patent
filings and business operations (collectively
called activities). China leads with 46% of
players and 45% of activities, followed by
the US (16% of players, 18% of activities).
South Korea comes in fourth with 6% of
global VW players and 9% of activities.

 ▶ South Korea (6% players, 9% activities)
outperforms the EU in activity intensity
(1.5 activities per player vs. 0.82 in the EU),
with South Korean players 1.81 times more
active on average.
 ▶ Globally, over 68,000 firms, research
institutions, and government entities
(collectively called players) engage in more
than 88,000 research publications, priority
patent filings, and business operations
related to virtual worlds across the digital
ecosystem.
 ▶ The EU leads in virtual world research
publications (30% to the US’ 16% and
China’s 15%), driven by Germany, Italy,
and Spain, but lags significantly in priority
patent filings (just over 1,500 of more
than 42,000 global priority filings). This
mismatch signals a missed opportunity
to translate academic excellence into
competitive business assets.
 ▶ Within the EU, Germany (20%), France
(14%), and Spain (10%) dominate virtual
world activities. Globally, China, the US, UK,
and South Korea are the central hubs, with
China and the US leading in patents and
business activity.
 ▶ The US and China dominate VC funding for
virtual worlds, with EUR 16.8 billion and
almost EUR 3 billion invested respectively
(2009–2024). The EU’s smaller share of
just over EUR 1 billion focuses on balanced
seed-to-late-stage funding.
 ▶ The EU is the second-largest foreign
investor worldwide (16%), with Germany,
Sweden, and France targeting the UK and
US. However, the US (31% global ownership)
holds disproportionate control over virtual
world firms, raising sovereignty risks.
China’s domestic investment has reduced
foreign ownership from 27% in 2009 to 4%
in 2025, signalling shifting global power
dynamics.
 ▶ Over 3,000 players and 800 projects have
benefitted from EU funding (2009–2025).
Within the EU, a majority of public funded
virtual world activities are located in
Germany, Italy, Spain and France. In
comparison to its engagement in virtual
worlds activities overall, Greece has also
significantly benefitted from EU public
funds.
 ▶ XR (85% of activities) is the core of
virtual worlds, but AI and 5G related to
virtual worlds receive limited attention in
EU research and business—despite their
critical role in scalability and intelligence.
This underinvestment risks hampering
competitiveness in next-gen immersive
ecosystems.
 ▶ Creative cultural industries (20% globally,
33% in the EU), tourism (14% globally, 27%
in the EU), and retail (13% globally, 24%
in the EU) are the most active industrial
ecosystems. However, high-potential
industries like healthcare, aerospace/
defence, and energy remain under adopted,
despite the EU’s relative strength in
healthcare research (4% global share).
 ▶ Manufacturing stands out as a growing
sector, with virtual world activity exceeding
the global average—a strategic opportunity
to leverage for industrial modernization.

01 INTRODUCTION: PROMISES
AND PERILS OF VIRTUAL
WORLDS

9
Imagine that it is 2035. A student slips on a
lightweight headset and steps into a virtual
biology lab. Moments later, she is participating
in a training lab exercise that her school could
not have afforded in physical form. For these
students, virtual worlds have become a bridge
to opportunity: granting access to high quality
instruction, global collaborations, and immersive
environments that spark curiosity and build real-
world skills. Virtual worlds have become engines
of inclusion across EU countries and regions,
boosting research, innovation, productivity, and
economic growth, offering low-cost training,
telepresence workspaces, cross-border and
intraregional knowledge spillovers, and new
arenas for cultural and civic participation.
Yet, these promises of virtual worlds come
with the perils of economic coercion and
dependency, harassment, surveillance, exclusion,
and manipulation at scale. The same virtual
environment built to support a student’s
education could include subtle behavioural
nudges built into their analytics dashboards to
shape student interactions. Student data could
be collected without explicit consent. Algorithms
could remain opaque. Harassment could occur
in shared virtual spaces where enforcement
mechanisms lag. The power to empower via
virtual worlds could rest with a handful of large
companies, undermining global competition and
EU digital sovereignty.
The EU’s reliance on a handful of dominant
foreign tech giants in virtual worlds poses
significant risks to strategic autonomy, data
sovereignty, and fair competition. The EU risks
major supply chain dependencies in IoT and
critical hard and soft infrastructure needed to
enable virtual worlds on a handful of countries
including China, the US, and South Korea. Their
dominance risks entrenching technological lock-
in, limiting EU businesses’ ability to innovate
or compete in global markets, and suffer
uncertainty in the face of global tensions and
volatility. Without proactive measures to diversify
ownership and invest in homegrown capabilities,
the EU risks ceding influence over the future of
immersive technologies—technologies that are
already reshaping work, healthcare, mobility, and
even democratic engagement. This underscores
the urgency of strengthening industrial
ecosystems and strategic equity participation to
ensure Europe’s virtual worlds reflect its values
and serve its citizens.
Virtual worlds thus stand at a critical crossroads.
They offer transformative societal benefits, but
without evidence-based, thoughtful governance,
transparent design, and enforceable protections
for rights and safety of users, they may amplify
existing inequities and create new vulnerabilities.
The good news is that activity in virtual worlds is
still growing. The choices made today by the EU
could determine whether these immersive digital
landscapes become public goods that expand
human potential, or fragmented spaces that
monopolise power and erode trust.
This report presents the EU virtual worlds
landscape at a critical juncture to inform policy
decisions on virtual worlds activity at the EU,
national, and regional levels. It describes global
and EU virtual world business, research, and
innovation patenting activity to build and analyse
the picture of EU global competitiveness in this
domain. It further offers insights on EU ownership
of foreign virtual world firms, as well as foreign
stakes in EU virtual world firms to illustrate
dependencies and opportunities for strategic
partnerships.
Virtual worlds encompass Extended Reality (XR),
Virtual Reality (VR), Augmented Reality (AR), and
Mixed Reality (MR). These immersive technologies
further draw together multiple enabling
infrastructures and technological components,
including artificial intelligence (AI), next-generation
connectivity (5G and 6G), cloud and edge
computing, and the Internet of Things (IoT). As the
global race to build the ‘next internet paradigm’
accelerates, questions of governance, market
structure, and public safety have become central to
strategic planning across the European Union (EU),
the United States (US), China (CN), and other major
economies. This report presents insights on how

10
these key enabling technologies support virtual
worlds, and how virtual world business, research,
and innovation patenting activities are distributed
across major geographic players including the EU,
US, China, and South Korea.
The United States relies on a predominantly
market-driven ecosystem in which major private-
sector firms steer the direction of XR development.
China is a key player in XR as well, along with
South Korea, particularly with regard to patenting
activity. Chinese technology giants—including
Huawei, Tencent, Baidu, and ByteDance—play
strategic roles in linking VR development to cloud
platforms, AI frameworks, and 5G networks.
China’s approach blends state steering, national
standard-setting, and strict content regulation.
The global virtual worlds market is shaped by
a handful of dominant firms. Meta is building
out expansive platforms designed to create an
all-in-one social, commercial, and immersive
ecosystems. Apple’s Vision Pro signals a
shift toward premium mixed reality systems
emphasising spatial computing and enterprise
integration. Sony remains a major force in gaming
through PlayStation VR2. Microsoft’s HoloLens
and HTC’s Vive devices anchor the enterprise and
industrial segments, while Chinese firms such as
Pico and DPVR maintain a strong presence in their
domestic market.
Core technologies are also shaped by foundational
software developers and network enablers.
NVIDIA provides essential processing power and
AI-driven rendering tools, while Unity and Unreal
Engine underpin much of the immersive content
creation ecosystem. European firms such as
Siemens, Dassault Systèmes, Bosch, and Varjo
play important roles in industrial simulation, high-
fidelity headsets, digital twins, and engineering
applications. Telecom innovators—including
Ericsson, Nokia, Huawei, ZTE, and Qualcomm—are
critical in advancing 5G and 6G networks optimised
for immersive and interactive workloads.
Virtual worlds offer substantial economic
benefits, helping industries streamline processes,
reduce costs, and accelerate innovation. Digital
twins, for instance, allow manufacturers to model
factories and supply chains in real time, improving
predictive maintenance and operational planning.
In construction and engineering, collaborative
virtual design accelerates decision-making and
reduces material waste. The entertainment and
gaming sectors continue to expand rapidly, driving
consumer adoption and advancing the underlying
technologies. Environmental sustainability is
another area where virtual worlds have made
contributions. Remote collaboration tools
reduce the need for business travel, and virtual
prototyping minimises physical waste in design-
heavy industries. As organisations track climate
impacts more carefully, virtual approaches to
planning, modelling, and communication can
help lower carbon footprints. These benefits can
be realised in the EU through careful analysis
of this growing ecosystem, and targeted policy
interventions to maximise gains while mitigating
adverse impacts.
A strong European VW ecosystem can support
and be supported by strategic autonomy. The
global VW market size is expected to grow from
EUR 27 billion in 2022 to over EUR 800 billion
by 2030 (European Commission, 2023). VW
technologies are expected to transform business
and employment trends across critical sectors,
like healthcare, education, art and design, logistics
and manufacturing. This rapid development brings
opportunities and risks that the EU needs to
address (European Commission, 2023a).
This report supports the Web 4.0 and virtual worlds
strategy and the Digital Decade policy programme
by: i) exploring how the EU stands in the global
virtual worlds domain, identifying the most active
countries in business, research and innovation;
ii) analysing EU public support in the VW field
and the capacity to attract private investment in
VW firms compared to other leading countries;
including an overview of the EU VW startup
landscape and their access to capital; iii) examining
strategic dependencies through investments and
firm ownership structures; and iv) depicting VW
adoption across industrial ecosystems.

1.1 Definitions and Typologies
Web 4.0 is the fourth generation of the internet
that fully integrates physical and virtual
worlds and provides better communication and
accessibility across devices.
Thus, virtual worlds are a key component of
the transition to the Web 4.0. Also known as
metaverses, VW refer to computer-simulated
immersive environments. Overall, there is a lack
of consensus about its definition, but certain
aspects across definitions are recurrently stated,
like the virtual-real interplay as well as its
societal and human-centric features. (Hupont
Torres et al., 2023). The EC defines virtual worlds
as ‘persistent, immersive environments based on
technologies including 3D and extended reality,
which make it possible to blend physical and
digital worlds in real-time for purposes such as
designing, simulating, collaborating, learning,
socialising, transactions, or entertainment’,
(European Commission, 2023a).

To better understand virtual worlds, it is
necessary to explain extended reality (XR), which
is considered a building block of virtual worlds.
The concept of XR leans on the reality-virtuality
continuum developed by Milgram and Kishino
(Milgram and Kishino, 1994). The boundaries of
this continuum are delimited by the physical reality,
which corresponds to a fully real environment,
and a fully virtual reality. Specific terms refer to
technologies within this continuum, depending
on the level of blending of real and virtual
environments (Figure 1). XR is the overarching
term that covers the set of technologies within the
spectrum, that is, all technologies that combine
virtual and real environments and human-machine
interactions at different degrees (Fast-Berglund
et al., 2018). Virtual reality (VR) is an immersive
technology embedded in the spectrum of virtual
worlds, as it projects an interactive and fully digital
environment. VR applications include videogames
or applications that generate digital simulations of
real-life situations (Vigkos et al., 2022).

12
Along with VR, augmented reality (AR) is considered
a key technology of virtual worlds as allow for
innovative immersive experiences. However, AR
technologies project digital information into the
real world. For example, smartphone camera filters
that superimpose computer-generated effects are
a form of AR application.
Another important concept is augmented
virtuality (AV). AV technologies provide an hybrid
but more virtual experience compared to AR (Xi
et al., 2023). Mixed reality (MR) usually refers to
technologies in the centre of the reality-virtuality
continuum, where virtual augments the real
world and vice versa. However, MR technologies
can also be understood as a highly interactive
AR application, where virtual objects realistically
blend into, and interact with, real objects and/or
with the user (Ecorys, 2021).
1.2 The Digital Techno-Economic
Ecosystem Approach
The Digital Techno-Economic EcoSystem
(DGTES) methodology combines and harmonises
multiple data sources into a network database
representation of the digital ecosystem. The
figures presented in this analysis includes data
released between 2009 and 20251. These
databases are then used by DGTES to combine
and build a wealth of economic indicators and
micro-level information, encompassing industrial,
geographical, and technologies dimensions.
At the core of the DGTES approach is a system
of keywords and queries used to define and
generate an ecosystem (and related database) of
activities, players, technologies, and interactions
(Calza et al., 2022). Keywords and queries reflect
the boundaries of the ecosystem to be analysed,
so in these cases identify virtual world related
technologies.
Activities are identified by several micro-level
data sources and categorised into three groups
based on their nature and outcomes (De Prato et
al., 2019; Righi et al., 2020):
1. Business activities, derived from
information on a company’s core
operations, encompassing the production,
supply or exchange of goods and services,
or investments.
2. Innovation activities, represented by
priority patent application filings and, with
regard to the EU, participation in EU funded
R&I projects.
3. Research activities, reflecting journal
publications.1
Players are entities that are engaging in the
above-mentioned activities across the digital
ecosystem, in the sense that they participate in the
development, generation, exchange and deployment
of knowledge and products (goods or services)
within the boundaries of virtual world related
technologies. In identifying players, emphasis is
placed on organisations rather than individuals
i.e. the applicant or developing organisation in the
context of patents, affiliation instead of individual
authors in conference proceedings and publications.
Note that within the scope of this analysis, players
and activities were identified for their engagement
in activities directly related to VW.
An example of a player is a company registered
as producing a virtual reality headset. An example
of an activity is a research publication on the
impact of virtual worlds on education, or a patent
for method and system for eye tracking software.
Players are classified along with the following 3
types:
1. Companies
2. Academic institutions and research centres
3. Governmental authorities and bodies
1. Note that only journal publications published in English are
included in this analysis.

13
Companies with subsidiaries engaged in virtual
world activities are captured in this analysis as
separate entities i.e. distinct players engaged in
the relevant virtual world activity. It may happen
that subsidiaries are listed in different countries
e.g. a company headquartered in the US which
may have a subsidiary in the EU. If the EU-based
subsidiary is engaged in a virtual world activity, it
is captured as an EU activity.
Business activities are identified via the analysis
of micro level data sources, namely Crunchbase,
Dealroom, Dow Jones, Orbis, Pitchbook, and
Standard & Poor’s. Research publications are
identified through Scopus. Priority patent filings
for innovation activities are identified through
Patstat. Activities funded by EU Framework
Programmes are identified through Cordis, and
cover part of FP7, Horizon 2020 and Horizon
Europe.
Thanks to its granularity and flexibility, the
DGTES methodology can map a variety of techno-
economic segments, fields and topics that have
a clear technological connotation, such as virtual
worlds. This yields insights into the VW industry,
such as the geographical location of VW firms,
the technological and industrial composition
of their activities, and the structure of foreign
ownership or venture capital flows.
To trace the technological perimeter of the
VW ecosystem, this methodology considers
technology areas associated with enabling or
enhancing VW processes. The DGTES approach
associates each technology area with a unique
set of keywords, which feed into the natural
language processing pipeline. In total, this study
considers 346 keywords across 7 technology
areas: Extended Reality, Artificial Intelligence
(AI), 3D Printing, Additive Manufacturing; 5G
and Connectivity, Blockchain, Online Platforms,
Internet & Cloud, and the Internet of Things (IoT).
Through search queries, these keywords identify
documents relevant to our VW definition from
various sources.2 By implementing the DGTES
approach, the documents refer to three types
of activities: business activities (containing
information on companies’ core business or
investments); innovation activities (filing of
priority patents or participation in EU-funded
projects); and research activities (publications
in selected journals, reflecting academic
contributions to frontier research). This report
covers data and documents published between
2009 and 2025.
The identification of actors from the retrieved
documents follows a multi-step process that
involves cleaning, disambiguation, classification
and validation of the data. As mentioned,
actors belonging to the same entity but based
in different geographical locations are treated
as distinct (therefore DGTES treats subsidiaries
as different entities from the parent or funding
company).

3.2 Venture Capital Funding: Global
and EU Trends
Venture capital (VC) is a critical driver of
innovation, scalability, and market transformation.
VC flows are key for competitiveness as they
fuel innovation. VC financing provides essential
funding and strategic support to startups and
small businesses, scaling up growth. They are
pivotal for businesses to gain a competitive edge
in the digital economy by enabling investment in
R&D and new VW technologies. Understanding
VC flows also allows policymakers to assess a
region’s innovative capacity and identify gaps to
better target public support strategies.
Figure 21 illustrates the trajectory, in euros, of VC
investments in virtual world firms in the EU, US,
China, UK, and South Korea. The US predictably
dominates the landscape with EUR 16.8 billion
invested, driven by peaks in 2016 and 2022. Early
investments in virtual worlds between 2013-
2016 correspond with the period that Facebook
Inc. (now Meta Platforms Inc.) acquired Oculus
for approximately 2 billion USD (Facebook, Inc,
2014), and Unity Technologies expanded strategic
partnerships with Oculus (Unity Technologies,
2014). Unity began making significant investments
in the emerging VR market, including Oculus Rift,
HTC Vive, and Samsung Gear VR in 2014-2015
(Allidina, 2025). Niantic released Pokémon Go, in
collaboration with the Pokémon Company, in 2016
(The Pokémon Company & Niantic, Inc., 2016).
In summary, 2014-2016 saw several US-based
companies making strategic investments in the
virtual world-related firms.
Investment in virtual worlds technologies peaked
again at EUR 3 billion peak in the US in 2022.
Epic Games raised US$1 billion in 2021 and

28
an additional US$2 billion in 2022 in growth-
equity rounds that signalled investor confidence
in large-scale, persistent virtual environments
(S&P Global Market Intelligence, 2024). The
Roblox IPO in 2021, alongside soaring valuations
for gaming and creator-platform companies,
reinforced the perception that immersive,
user-generated virtual worlds were becoming
mainstream (McKinsey & Company, 2022; Roblox
Corporation, 2021). At the same time, corporate
spending surged: Meta dramatically expanded
its Reality Labs investments (Dean, 2022), and
Microsoft’s announcement of the Activision
Blizzard acquisition further legitimised virtual
world ambitions for both investors and industry
analysts (Microsoft Corporation, 2022; Reuters,
2022). This period also coincided with a strong
Web3/NFT boom, during which many blockchain
gaming and crypto-based virtual-world projects
attracted venture funding by branding themselves
as ‘metaverse’ players (CryptoSlate, 2022;
Decrypt, 2021; Xiang et al., 2016). Together,
these forces created a self-reinforcing cycle of
enthusiasm, capital, and media attention that
defined the height of the virtual world investment
in the US before conditions tightened in 2023.
China ranks second worldwide with nearly EUR 3
billion invested in virtual worlds over the period
2009-2024. Chinese VC investment in virtual worlds
has been more gradual between 2009-2024, with
a significant peak in 2015 and increased funding
in 2021 and 2022, possibly following market
narratives of increased investor confidence in
virtual worlds following investments in Epic Games,
Roblox, Meta’s Reality Labs, and Activision Blizzard

As virtual worlds and immersive technologies
rapidly evolve into foundational components of
the future digital economy, understanding the
ownership structures that shape this emerging
ecosystem has become a strategic imperative
for the European Union. Virtual worlds firms—
encompassing developers of virtual reality (VR)
and extended reality (XR) hardware, software,
platforms, engines, content infrastructure, and
immersive services—are no longer niche players in
the tech landscape. Instead, they are increasingly
central to sectors such as education, industry,
culture, health, and public administration. As
these technologies scale, ownership and control
over the firms developing and operating them will
profoundly influence economic competitiveness,
data governance, security, and the cultural and
societal values embedded in digital environments.
The ownership of virtual worlds firms determines
who controls the intellectual property, data flows,
platform governance, technological standards,
and economic rents associated with immersive
technologies. Examining both directions of
ownership—EU firms investing in or acquiring
virtual worlds companies abroad, and non-EU
firms buying or investing in EU companies—is
therefore crucial. EU ownership of firms overseas
can strengthen Europe’s global influence, deepen
its participation in global value chains, and ensure
access to key technologies and talent pools.
Conversely, foreign ownership of EU-based firms
can bring capital, expertise, and market access, but
it can also result in strategic dependencies if critical
capabilities migrate outside the Union or if decision-
making is ultimately guided by non-European
regulatory, commercial, or political priorities.
This dual analysis is especially important in the
context of EU debates on digital sovereignty. Digital
sovereignty impacts the EU’s capacity to shape,
regulate, and control its own digital ecosystems,
ensuring that critical technologies reflect European
values, security requirements, and economic
interests. In an environment where virtual worlds
are expected to become important infrastructures—
hosting everything from industrial simulations to
social interaction, commerce, education, and public
services—sovereignty concerns intensify. Ownership
concentration by non-European actors could limit
the EU’s ability to regulate platform architecture,
safeguard sensitive data, mandate interoperability,
or prevent lock-in to closed ecosystems. At the
same time, insufficient levels of European outward
investment may weaken the EU’s position in global
technology governance and reduce its influence over
the standards and norms shaping future immersive
environments.
Studying foreign ownership trends in virtual worlds
firms therefore provides a critical lens through
which to assess Europe’s long-term technological
autonomy and strategic resilience. It offers insights
into where value is created and captured, how
global competitive dynamics are shifting, and what
structural vulnerabilities may emerge as immersive
technologies become ubiquitous. Understanding
these patterns will be essential for informing
investment screening mechanisms, R&D strategies,
industrial policy interventions, and broader efforts
to secure a robust, competitive, and values-aligned
European presence in the rapidly developing virtual
worlds ecosystem.
Note that this section reports on firm ownership
and excludes government and research
organisations. Foreign ownership is defined as
ownership of more than 50% of shares (IMF,
2009) by an entity located in a country different
from the country of residence of the firm. We
further distinguish foreign, domestic, and local
ownership from an EU perspective. Therefore,
foreign-owned EU firms  are EU-based firms
owned by investors located in countries outside
the EU. Domestic ownership refers to EU firms
that are owned by entities located within the EU
(e.g. a French firm owned by a German investor).
Locally-owned firms are those owned nationally
(e.g. an Italian firm that has Italian ownership).

5.2 Virtual Worlds Across Sectors
Sectoral analysis of virtual worlds is distinct
from that of industrial ecosystems. The
European Commission distinguishes between
sectors (classified by NACE codes) and industrial
ecosystems as complementary but conceptually
distinct frameworks for understanding
economic activity and policy design. Sectors are
standardised classifications of economic activities
defined by the NACE (Nomenclature of Economic
Activities) system. NACE codes categorise
industries e.g. Manufacturing, Information
& Communication. Sectors are defined by
predefined boundaries (e.g., ‘manufacturing’,
‘mining & quarrying’, ‘real estate activities’)
and NACE codes standardise how economic
data (e.g., GDP, employment, trade) is collected,
analysed, and reported across the EU. Industrial
ecosystems on the other hand are dynamic,
interconnected networks of sectors, stakeholders,
and technologies working collaboratively to
achieve systemic goals. They transcend traditional
sectoral boundaries. This section presents both to
help address how virtual world activities perform
in both industrial ecosystems and sectors, with
granular level analysis on the former.

5.3 Key Digital Technologies Enabling
Virtual Worlds
At their core, virtual worlds are powered by a
convergence of technologies that enable dynamic,
scalable, and user-centric experiences. These
technologies not only define the functionality
of virtual worlds but also drive their potential
to transform industries and redefine human-
machine interaction. Understanding how these
technologies intersect is critical to grasping the
future trajectory of virtual worlds and their role in
shaping the digital economy.
Extended Reality (XR), encompassing virtual
reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR), provides
the hardware and software infrastructure for
immersive user experiences. They are at the core
of virtual worlds, as demonstrated in Figure 35.
VR headsets and AR glasses bridge the physical
and digital, enabling users to interact with virtual
environments in real time. In industrial contexts,
AR overlays digital information onto physical
workspaces, enhancing remote collaboration
or equipment maintenance. Meanwhile, VR
facilitates high-fidelity simulations for sectors like
healthcare (e.g., surgical training) or education
(e.g., virtual classrooms). The integration of haptic
feedback and eye-tracking technologies further
enhances realism, making virtual interactions
indistinguishable from physical ones.
AI is a foundational enabler, driving intelligent
automation, real-time decision-making, and
adaptive experiences within virtual environments.
AI algorithms power procedural content
generation, allowing virtual worlds to dynamically
create landscapes, characters, and narratives
tailored to user behaviour. For example, AI-driven
avatars can simulate human-like interactions,
enhancing social and professional collaboration
in virtual meetings or training simulations. In
industrial applications, AI optimises virtual
simulations by analysing real-time data to
improve predictive maintenance or supply
chain modelling. Additionally, machine learning
personalises user experiences, such as adaptive
difficulty levels in virtual training or AI-generated
recommendations in digital marketplaces.
Blockchain and decentralised technologies
underpin the ownership, security, and governance
of virtual worlds. Blockchain enables tokenised
digital assets, allowing users to own, trade,
and monetise virtual goods (e.g., NFT-based
land in metaverse platforms) securely and
transparently. Smart contracts automate
transactions and governance rules, fostering
decentralised economies where users retain
control over their data and assets. For instance,
blockchain-based identity systems ensure privacy
and interoperability across platforms, while
decentralised autonomous organisations (DAOs)
empower communities to co-govern virtual
spaces. These innovations align with the EU’s
push for digital sovereignty, reducing reliance on
centralised platforms and ensuring ethical data
practices.
The Internet of Things (IoT) connects virtual
worlds to the physical environment, enabling
real-time data exchange and environmental
responsiveness. IoT sensors feed real-world
metrics into virtual simulations, such as
monitoring energy usage in smart factories or
tracking user biometrics for health analytics. This
integration allows virtual worlds to reflect and
adapt to real-time conditions, creating hybrid
environments where digital and physical systems
coexist. For example, a virtual city model might
use IoT data to simulate traffic patterns or
environmental changes, aiding urban planning
and sustainability efforts.
5G, 6G, and edge computing ensure low-latency,
high-speed connectivity, which is essential
for seamless virtual world interactions. These
technologies reduce lag in real-time multiplayer

48
environments, support high-resolution graphics
streaming, and enable distributed computing for
resource-intensive simulations. As connectivity
expands, it will unlock massively multiplayer
virtual worlds with thousands of concurrent users,
fostering global collaboration and economic
activity.

The global virtual worlds ecosystem, spanning over
88,000 activities and 68,000 players, is a dynamic
and rapidly evolving domain with transformative
potential for industries, economies, and societies.
This report has mapped the evolution and state of
virtual world activities, highlighting the uneven but
accelerating adoption of immersive technologies
across industrial ecosystems, regions, and sectors.
While the EU demonstrates strong research
capabilities, it lags in translating these strengths
into commercial innovation and business activity
compared to the US and China. The concentration
of virtual world activity in sectors such as Creative
Cultural Industries, Tourism, and Retail underscores
their natural alignment with immersive
technologies but also reveals fragmentation and
missed opportunities in other critical areas, such
as Healthcare, Aerospace, and Energy-Intensive
Industries.
The EU is a leader in research publications on
virtual worlds. However, it trails in patenting and
business activity, reflecting the need to bridge
academic research and industrial application. The
dominance of the US and China in VC funding and
patenting patterns highlights the need for the EU
to strengthen its innovation-to-market pipelines,
foster cross-sector collaboration, and strategically
position itself in the global virtual worlds landscape.
The report also underscores the transnational
nature of virtual world ownership, with the EU’s
growing investments abroad and its role as a
destination for foreign capital. However, ensuring
digital sovereignty, ethical innovation, and equitable
economic benefits remain pressing challenges.
The interplay of key enabling technologies—XR,
AI, 5G, IoT, and Blockchain—forms the backbone
of virtual worlds, yet disparities in adoption and
investment persist. For the EU, leveraging its
research strengths in XR, IoT, and Blockchain, while
addressing gaps in AI and 5G & Connectivity is
critical to maintaining global competitiveness. The
findings call for a coordinated approach to policy,
funding, and industrial strategy to ensure the EU
can harness the full potential of virtual worlds
while aligning with its strategic priorities: digital
sovereignty, sustainability, and inclusive growth.
6.1 Summary of Key Findings
GLOBAL AND EU ACTIVITY DISTRIBUTION
Virtual world activities account for 2% of global
digital ecosystem activities, with 88,000 activities
and 68,000 players (firms, research institutions,
and government entities).
China leads with 46% of players and 45% of
activities, followed by the US (16% of players,
18% of activities) and the EU (11% of players, 9%
of activities).
Within the EU, a majority of activities are carried
out in Germany (20%), followed by France (14%),
and Spain (10%), with Germany, Italy, Spain, and
France leading in business and research activity.
The EU leads in research publications (30% to the
US’ 16% and China’s 14%) but lags in patenting
(only just over 1500 of more than 42,000 global
filings) and business activity. With regard to
patent activity, future research could look into
assessing the quality of EU patents versus other
geographic countries to assess potential areas for
strategic development.
China and the US dominate patenting and
business activity, with China’s EUR 3 billion in VC
funding and the United States’ EUR 16.8 billion.
EU PUBLIC FUNDING
Over 3000 business, research and innovation
players and over 800 projects have benefitted from
EU funded support on virtual worlds between 2009-
2025. Within the EU, Germany, Italy, Spain and
France are the most active in EU-funded initiatives.
Relative to its number of virtual worlds players and
activities, Greece hosts a significant number of EU-
funded virtual worlds players and projects, coming
up behind France on both accounts.
VENTURE CAPITAL FUNDING
With EUR 16.8 billion in funds received over the
period from 2009 to 2024, data on VC funding

55
rounds demonstrate the US's ability to attract
high-risk, high-reward investments. China follows,
but in comparison, captures just below EUR 3
billion. Notably, the US and China are also ahead
in scale and late-stage investments. The EU has
raised EUR 1 billion over the same period, with a
relatively balanced composition of seed (23%),
early (30%) and late-stage (44%) funding. The
remaining 2% of European VC funds comes from
private equity funds.
OWNERSHIP AND GEOPOLITICAL DYNAMICS
The US is the largest foreign owner of virtual
world firms (31%), with significant investments in
the UK and EU.
The EU is the second-largest foreign investor
(16%), with Germany, Sweden, and France leading
investments in the UK and US.
China’s rapid domestic growth has reduced
foreign ownership from 27% in 2009 to 4% in
2025, signalling a shift in global power dynamics.

XR (85% of activities) is the cornerstone of

complementary but underdeveloped roles.
AI and 5G receive less attention in EU research
and business activity related to virtual worlds,
despite their critical role in driving scalability and
intelligence in virtual environments.
Creative Cultural Industries dominate virtual world
activity (20% globally, 33% in the EU), followed
by Tourism (14% globally, 27% in the EU) and
Retail (13% globally, 24% in the EU).
Healthcare, Aerospace & Defence, and Energy-
Intensive Industries show low adoption
despite high potential for applications like
remote diagnostics, training simulations, and
sustainability planning.
The EU’s engagement in Healthcare (4%) is
relatively higher than its global counterparts, but
only 3% of this translates into business activity.
6.2 Policy Implications
Virtual world technologies can bring substantial
economic gains. They have evolved from
niche gaming spaces into broad digital
domains affecting economic governance and
competitiveness. For example, digital twins
—the virtual representation of real objects—
have the capacity to optimise manufacturing
and automotive production cycles, redesign
industrial processes and reduce waste. The
analysis of global and EU activity in virtual worlds
reveals a striking contrast between Europe’s
research strength and its comparatively modest
commercial output. While the EU accounts
for roughly one-third of worldwide scholarly
publications on virtual-world technologies, it
contributes only a few percent of the global
patent pool and lags behind the US and China
in venture-capital-driven business formation.
To turn the EU’s ideas-rich environment into a
market-leading sector, a coordinated set of policy
measures is required.
Europe’s ability to generate high-impact research
must be matched by mechanisms that translate
those findings into protectable intellectual
property to help close the research-to-patenting
gap. Embedding IP counsel within EU funded
research clusters—particularly in Germany,
Italy, Spain, and France, where business and
research activity is already concentrated—could
give researchers early guidance on protection
strategies and reduce the risk of missed
commercial opportunities. Finally, the EU could
also link outcomes to market-readiness incentives.
Venture capital support should be rebalanced. The
current venture-capital landscape shows the United
States and China dominating large-scale, late-stage
financing, while EU capital is spread thinly across
the seed, early, and late stages. This dispersion
hampers the ability of European start-ups to reach
the scale necessary for global competition.

56
Virtual worlds are built on a blend of key enabling
technologies. Extended reality (XR) already
accounts for the overwhelming majority of
activity in virtual worlds, but artificial intelligence,
5G, the Internet of Things, and blockchain
remain under-exploited. The EU could look to
fund the integration of these complementary
technologies into XR solutions. Dedicated
integration grants should be earmarked for
projects that embed AI-driven agents, edge-AI
processing, or low-latency 5G connectivity into
immersive environments. A common blockchain
interoperability framework will also lower entry
barriers for SMEs wishing to develop secure
virtual economies.
Cultural and creative industries dominate the
current virtual-world landscape, accounting for
a third of EU activity. Yet industrial ecosystems
with high transformative potential—healthcare,
aerospace and defence, and energy-intensive
industries—remain largely untapped. The EU
should also look to addressing the gap between
research and commercial activity in healthcare—
where only three percent of research output
translates into business.
In terms of ownership and geopolitical control,
the US now owns the largest share of foreign
virtual-world firms, while the EU is the
second-largest foreign investor. China’s rapid
domestic growth has sharply reduced its foreign
ownership, signalling a shift toward self-reliance.
To safeguard strategic autonomy, the EU should
pay close attention to ‘critical virtual -world
assets’. Finally, the EU must strengthen
supply-chain resilience by mapping dependencies
on hardware such as XR headsets and 5G
chips, and by incentivising the development
of European-based production capacities or
diversified sourcing strategies that reduce
reliance on any single external supplier.
In conclusion, Europe’s research leadership and
smattering of unicorns in virtual worlds provides
a foundation, but without a concerted effort
to bridge the gap to patents, venture capital,
and market adoption, the continent risks being
left behind by the United States and China. By
aligning public funding with private investment,
fostering the integration of AI, 5G, IoT, and
blockchain, expanding the sectoral reach beyond
cultural industries, and protecting strategic assets
through ownership safeguards and supply -chain
resilience, the EU can transform its virtual -world
ecosystem into a globally competitive, innovative,
and strategically autonomous sector.

Annex 3. DGTES Digital Area
Definitions
This report makes reference to several digital
areas which together comprise Virtual Worlds.
Extended Reality makes up the largest technology
that enables Virtual Worlds, as discussed at
length in the report, along with others such as
AI, 5G & Connectivity, Blockchain, the Internet of
Things (IoT) and 3D Printing. These technologies
are defined within the scope of the Digital
Techno-Economic Ecosystem Approach (DGTES)
as follows:
Extended Reality (XR)  encompasses a range
of immersive technologies, such as virtual reality
and augmented mixed reality. Extended reality
blends the physical and digital worlds, creating
immersive experiences and computer-generated
environments, in which users can interact with
and manipulate digital objects or simulations.
External portable devices like glasses and
headsets often provide support, allowing to track
movements and interact with virtual objects.
The Artificial Intelligence (AI)  digital area
encompasses the development, deployment, and
uptake of AI techniques including but not limited
to machine learning, deep learning, computer
vision, natural language processing, and neural
networks.
The 5G & Connectivity digital area
encompasses the facilitation of devices,
machines, and systems to communicate and
share data seamlessly, supporting a fast and
reliable means of connectivity. Enhanced
connectivity is ensured by the latest generations
of wireless network technology (i.e. 5G and 6G),
which offer faster data transfer speeds, lower
latency, and the capacity to connect a massive
number of devices simultaneously, essential
to the integration of advanced technologies.
The 5G & Connectivity digital area enables the
implementation of Internet of Things (IoT) by
fostering the connection and communication
across devices and sensors, as well as gathering
and storing generated data.
3D printing, or additive manufacturing, is a
manufacturing process that uses special printers
to build three-dimensional physical objects
from digital designs. 3D Printing is a dual-use
technology that finds applications in various
manufacturing sectors, such as aerospace,
automotive, and consumer goods.
Blockchain and distributed ledger technologies
(DLT) represent a broader category – including
blockchain – of decentralised systems where
data is stored and synchronised across multiple
nodes or computers in a network, without relying
on a single authority for data management. They
represent secure, transparent, and decentralised
methods for recording, storing, sharing and
managing data and transactions.
The Internet of Things (IoT) is a network
of interconnected physical devices, machines,
and objects that are embedded with sensors,
software, and connectivity capabilities. Often
complemented by Automatic Identification and
Data Capture (AIDC) technologies (i.e. barcodes,
RFID, QR codes), these devices are able to
‘sense’ their environment, identify, collect, store
data from objects and products, and exchange
them over the internet or other networks via
standard communication protocols and without
human intervention (i.e. machine-to-machine
communication).
Online Platforms, Internet & Cloud:
Online platforms include websites, web
applications, marketplaces, social media, and
mobile apps. Internet & Cloud comprises of
digital environments that enable interactions,
transactions, and exchanges between individuals,
business, or organisations through the Internet.
This digital area also includes IT solutions that
leverage cloud-computing infrastructures to give
ubiquitous and on-demand network access to
scalable computing resources, such as storage
capacity and processing power for large volumes
of data, software applications, and a range of
services accessible over the internet - hosted on
remote servers (data centres) by cloud service
providers.
