Monitoring and evaluation of mission-oriented
innovation policies: From theory to practice

Missions are nested entities involving multiple interventions at different
levels and unclear and evolving boundaries, making traditional evaluation
approaches ill-suited to capturing their additionality. This paper proposes
mission-evaluation processes and tools that are consistent with their
specific features. It notably proposes mission criteria related to their
different expected systemic effects; a mission theory of action to support a
developmental evaluation that tracks the evolution of the mission design
and processes; and a monitoring tool to assess and compare mission-
readiness levels across missions and at different stages of the mission life
cycle.

Authors: Philippe Larrue, Piret Tõnurist and David Jonason.

mission-evaluation process

Executive summary
Effective monitoring and evaluation (M&E) of mission -oriented innovation policies (MOIPs) helps
governments and partners improve evidence-informed policy making and mission design, co-ordination
and implementation, thereby enhancing economic and societal impacts. It promotes continuous learning
across different stages of the mission life cycle and accountability for the significant resources invested in
these policies. Even more importantly , in the current early stage where missions ha ve yet to produce
measurable impacts, M&E should help substantiate the added value of MOIPs as an additional framework
in already complex and crowded policy landscapes. This is especially important because MOIPs – along
with other proactive policies aimed at transforming sociotechnical systems to address societal challenges
– are subject to growing criticism from some policy makers and stakeholders.  The evolving complex,
multidimensional and multilevel nature of MOIPs , however,  makes it difficult to establish causal
relationships between actions and outcomes.
Developing an evaluation framework that is valid for different types of MOIP s is challenging, due to
their highly diverse types, objectives and governance structures. Evaluators must adapt their approaches
to each MOIP, focusing not only on outcomes, but also on the processes and governance structures that
shape these initiatives. Developing a theory of action that clarifies the hypotheses and rationales
underpinning the mission design could be instrumental in this regard.
Accounting for the complex geography of MOIPs is crucial because of the ambiguous boundaries of
missions, especially those with a broad systemic scope. These missions involve a wide range of actors,
interventions and activities, often co -ordinated through rather loose arrangements, making it difficult to
delineate their scope clearly and assess their influence. Evaluators must map out the mission’s scope,
identify who was engaged in its design and implementation, and a ssess the sphere of influence to
determine how effectively the mission integrates and co-ordinates the various activities needed to fulfil the
mission.
Combining the evaluation of the different nested MOIP entities : MOIPs are multilevel, nested
structures where individual projects are part of portfolios within broader missions, all contributing to a larger
MOIP initiative. Each entity within this hierarchy  – projects, portfolios, missions – has different objectives
and expected effects. This requires combin ing different evaluations in a tailored, co -ordinated and
balanced approach. Notably, evaluations should build from the bottom up, with results at each level
informing higher -level evaluations (“capillarity”) . Evaluations at each level should focus on effe cts that
cannot be captured at a lower level (“subsidiarity”).
Assessing the double additionality of missions : Due to their complex , multilevel nature, the
additionality of MOIPs cannot be measured against a “no-intervention scenario” (“first-order additionality”),
as is traditionally the case in public policy evaluation. Evaluators should examine a mission’s impact
relative to a scenar io where traditional policies would have been used instead (“second -order
additionality”), i.e. a counterfactual scenario with less directionality, holistic co-ordination and policy mix
integration. Evaluators must also consider any potential negative effe cts, such as  an increased
administrative complexity or the displacement of new initiatives.
Embracing the evolutive nature of MOIPs : Missions often begin with broad objectives that are refined
over time through negotiations, growing trust among partners an d the inclusion of new stakeholders. This

ongoing evolution complicates evaluation, as both the objectives and the mission's structure are moving
targets. Evaluators must assess not only the mission's outcomes but also its evolution in terms of design,
governance and processes. This involves a developmental evaluation approach to track how the mission
becomes more aligned with its purpose, focusing on the adaptation and learning of both policy makers and
partners. Over time, as missions mature, summative ev aluations can complement this process by
assessing progress toward final objectives.
Adopting a nuanced approach : When evaluating MOIPs, it is crucial to recognise that more extensive
orientation, co-ordination and integration are not always better. Mission partners should identify the
aspects of co-ordination that add value in each specific case, taking into account the mission's context and
needs. Evaluators should therefore consider the unique structural features of the challenge area addressed
by each mission, such as its complexity, uncertainty and contestability, to determine the most appropriate
design and avoid a one-size-fits-all approach.
The paper proposes mission -evaluation criteria, processes and tools that are consistent with the specific
features of missions, as laid out above.
First, it presents four main groups of mission effects: directionality effects, governance effects,
portfolio effects and material effects. For each of these, specific criteria are derived to guide the
evaluative work (for instance mobilisation, intentionality and flexibility for directionality effects). These
criteria define the standards and benchmark by which missions ’ achievements, processes a nd practices
should be assessed.
The paper then delves into the “mission theory of action”, a tool specifically developed to track the
specific mission processes and the systemic effects they are expected to generate to tackle a
specific challenge . It pres ents as an example a  “theory of action” of missions that aim to support
governments in achieving their net-zero targets .
Finally, the paper presents a complementary tool to monitor mission -readiness levels, i.e. how the
mission’s level of maturity, using the mission “ideal type” as a flexible benchmark. The tool, based on the
OECD “MOIP design principles” framework, allows the different mission communities to self -assess their
achievements, compare them wit h missions from other  communities, and use these results to discuss
necessary actions and possible ways forward. The tool was successfully tested in the context of  Austria's
efforts to mobilise relevant communities of actors around the five EU Missions.
These recommendations, criteria and tools for mission M&E have already been applied in several
experiments. They will require more thinking and interactions , and will be applied and stress -tested in
different contexts, at different levels. The OECD Mission Action Lab is currently in discussions with several
missions to improve and apply these frameworks and tools in the near future . This is the condition for
delivering effective and practical tools and methods capable of assessing what a French mission policy
maker has called the “supplément d’âme des missions”.1

An increasing number of countries have adopted a mission -oriented innovation policy (MOIP) approach
under different (often experimental) manners in the last five years to address complex systemic challenges
(see definition in Box 1). Monitoring and evaluation (M&E) of mission-oriented innovation policy (MOIP) is
critical not only to the success of each mission initiative, but also to the adoption and development of this
policy approach – and, by extension, a range of other proactive policies aiming to transform sociotechnical
systems.
M&E should help governments and their partners improve evidence-informed decision-making about the
design and implementation of these new public policies – which in turn, will lead to more rapid and/or
higher economic and societal impacts. More generally, it will promote learning within each initiative (from
one stage of implementation to the next) and between initiatives (mutual learning). Finally, as for any other
public polic y, M&E will promote governmental accountability for the significant efforts devoted to
implementing these policy initiatives.
M&E of MOIPs is also particularly important to justify the added value of this policy approach in already
crowded and complex national governance and policy landscape s. This new policy approach raised
significant expectations and attracted considerable political attention in the second half of the 2010s, not
least after the launch of the EU Missions advocated by the political economi st Mariana Mazzucato
(Mazzucato, 2018 and 2019). This high visibility now translates into a strong demand for evidence of
achievements and successes, and a strong imperative for missions to “prove themselves”.
Missions2 are often seen as part of a broader trend of a return to proactive industrial policies (Juhász, Lane
and Rodrik, 2023), notably to support the green and digital transitions. Against this backdrop, missions are
also very much under scrutiny from those politicians, policy makers and academics who , based on
evidence and ideology, doubt that a purposive, strategically planned and integrated approach can be an
effective policy option. This is increasingly the case in the current context of growing criticism of missions
in particular , and policies aiming to address societal challenges in general, and a pervasive call to
(re)prioritise a bold competitiveness and growth agenda. MOIPs are therefore embedded in and coloured
by a broad and cyclical debate that goes far beyond their remit and is not directly linked to their real (proven)
performance. The M&E of missions will take place  – and their results will be closely scrutinised – in this
new context.
The practice of mission M&E is in itself also challenging, even though – at first glance – missions should
make policy evaluation easier. Arguably, for “ ideal-type” missions, the success or failure of a mission
should be self-evident because these policies are result-oriented, and their objectives are expected to be
“specific, measurable, achievable, realistic and timebound ( SMART)”.1 Mazzucato and Dibb (2019)
describe missions as “granular and specific in that you must be able to answer whe ther or not they have
been achieved.” One could therefore expect that m ission results are measurable, meaning “quantifiable
with existing metrics, or achievements that are evidently yes or no ” (ibid). Simply put, and leaving aside
the potential measurement problems, either the net greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions targets are reached
at a certain date or they are not; the rate of fatalities from brain cancer can be below, equal or above some
mission targets.

However, the reality of missions most often does not conform to this  logic. Many missions do not have
clear mission statements and targets – at least at the start – and their objectives are often too long term to
allow measuring outcomes in a time  frame that satisfies both accountability and learning requirements
(OECD, 2024 a). Also, different types  of missions can have different types of objectives  and blurred
boundaries, and may be composed of different nested levels  (MOIP initiative s, mission s, intervention s
within the mission portfolio) (OECD, 2024b and 2024c). These features make the causal-effect relationship
non-linear, and therefore difficult to measure in a detailed and robust manner. Furthermore, many missions
are still very much in an exploration and learning stage. A recent OECD study showed that 81% of missions
aiming to reduce GHG emissions directly or indirectly were initiated between 2018 and 2021 (OECD,
2023a). Since it often takes one or two years before missions start implementing activities, most missions
currently have only a few years of concrete implementation and have not yet produced any tangible impact
beyond their own structuring. Another important implication of this is that these “young missions” evolve in
scope and shape, gradually forming the structures, processes and rules that will determine what they are,
who is participating and how to achieve their objectives.
All these features  which are the essence of missions complicate their evaluatio n and require specific
efforts. This difficulty is compounded by the wickedness of the challenges they address, particular ly their
complexity and long time span (Baarslag, Geurts and Van Der Zee, 2024). However, although mission
M&E is increasingly drawing attention and mobilising resources, it remains largely “uncharted territory ”
(OECD, 2021). Various rationales explain this situation , beginning with the complexity of these policies.
The difficulties associated with their design monopolises most public resources and attention at the
expense of other matters, including M&E.  Other reasons might lie in some policy makers’ risk aversion,
which hinders the development and application of new evaluation methods (Arnold et al., 2018).  More
practically, there was little time and attention available to think about M&E during the first years of the
design and implementation of most MOIPs. These were often designed in a context of urgency and political
pressure stemming from the need to respond to societal challenges, chiefly global warming.
So far, the few M&E activities around missions have used traditional approaches  that are ill -suited for
evaluating them (Rohracher, Coenen and Kordas, 2023). While the need for a systemic approach to public
policy evaluation is well acknowledged (OECD, 2022), established methodologies and practices fall short
of capturing the comprehensive benefits these missions are expected to offer at the system level, stemming
from the integration of diverse knowledge, sk ills, interventions and funding streams across sectors.
Capturing these “systemic effects”, which is critical for being fair to missions at a critical moment of the life
cycle of this policy approach , requires developing new processes, methodologies and to ols (Wise et al.,
2022). The most promising initiatives in this respect come from academic work , but remain  rather
conceptual and have yet to be applied in real evaluation cases (Elzinga et al., 2023; Janssen et al., 2022;
Wittmann et  al., 2022 ; Haddad and Bergek, 2023 ; Molas-Gallart et al. 2021) or do not use a formal
evaluation framework (Janssen, 2020; Haddad et al. 2023; Roth et al., 2021).
Against this backdrop, this paper first identifies the  specificities of MOIPs , and how these determine the
main features of a suitable approach to monitoring and evaluating them. Building on these broad framing
elements, it then draws concrete implications in terms of  evaluating the systemic effects  and developing
the corresponding evaluation criteria. The paper also proposes two specific tools respectively for the
developmental evaluation of missions (the mission “theory of action”) and iterative monitoring of missions
(the “mission-readiness monitoring tool”).
The work was carried out using a desk review, approximately 30 interviews and various interactions with
mission practitioners and experts . A meeting organised at the OECD with selected mission -evaluation
experts was a  key milestone in the project (see Annex A). This paper als o draws input from a  study
commissioned by the OECD Mission Action Lab in 2022 to an evaluation expert 3 to examine a range of
identified cases of MOIP evaluation (Arnold, 2022).

Box 1.1. OECD definition and typology of missions
OECD definition of MOIPs
One of the first task of OECD when it started working on missions was to establish a consensual definition
of this new policy approach based on discussions within the CSTP Steering Group on MOIPs  and through
interactions with academics. As a result, the OECD defines MOIPs as a co-ordinated package of policy and
regulatory measures tailored specifically to mobilise STI to address well -defined objectives related to a
societal challenge, within a def ined time frame. These measures possibly span different stages of the
innovation cycle, from research to demonstration and market deployment; mix supply-push and demand-pull
instruments; and cut across various policy fields, sectors and disciplines.
More succinctly, missions are defined as a space for collective action to address complex and systemic
challenges. Collective action is the common thread that cut across the three building blocks of MOIPs:
Strategic orientation: an MOIP involves a collectively d eveloped agenda; Policy coordination: a dedicated
structure of governance to take and monitor the effects of common or mutually consistent decisions; Policy
implementation: a deliberately designed “joined-up” set of interventions. What is unique about MOIP s is not
the presence of any of these building blocks taken in isolation – they are found in all STI systems – but rather
their integration into a common organisational and institutional framework. Neither a whole -of-government
national strategy for green hydrogen, nor a high-level cross ministerial committee for industry decarbonisation
or a large-scale cooperative programme for a circular economy are sufficient to form an MOIP.
The MOIP building block
Although they can differ significantly in their design  and types of objectives, MOIPs revolve around three
building blocks
• Strategic orientation: They are oriented toward the achievement of clear objectives, formalised in
terms of expected results (not inputs invested or actions to be implemented) and develo ped
collectively.
• Policy co -ordination: They align the plans of various government actors in charge of different
components (e.g., knowledge, technologies, funding, instruments pertaining to different policy fields,
regulations) that are essential for achieving the collectively agreed-upon objectives.
• Policy implementation: They are implemented via a comprehensive mix of policy interventions and
various initiatives to support a range of activities, from research to market launch and the formation
of the r equired skills. These packages of interventions are deliberately designed to achieve the
policy’s objectives. They can include joint implementation initiatives (from joint calls for proposal to
cooperative, co -funded, schemes and programmes) that bring tog ether multiple partners across
policies fields and sectors.
Collective action lies at the core of each of these building blocks: an MOIP involves a collectively developed
agenda; a dedicated structure of governance to take and monitor the effects of common  or mutually
consistent decisions; and, finally, a deliberately designed “joined-up” policy mix.
The key challenge of assessing missions consists in identifying and grasping the added value of this holistic
dimension in missions, from the definition of th e missions to their implantation. This is essential for i) the
systematic collection of data that help mission managers understand progress in execution and performance
in real time (monitoring of missions); ii) determine judgments about various criteria r elative to how they
produce their intended effects and the nature of these effects (see section 3 on mission evaluation criteria)

Developing mission monitoring and ev aluation (M&E) processes and methods requires factoring in  their
complex, multidimensional and multilevel, and evolving nature. Understanding these specificities provides
essential insights into developing suitable evaluation processes, methodologies and tools. This section
reviews the main characteristics of missions and draw their implications for mission M&E.
Developing an evaluation framework that can be applied to different types of
mission-oriented innovation policy (MOIPs)
By definition, all MOIPs share a similar approach , which consists in setting up a  tailor-made institutional
space for collective action towards resolving a societal challenge. However, this common policy approach
comprises a variety of types which can differ significantly not only in scale and scope, but also in terms of
objectives, type of governance, level of integration, etc.
Larrue (2021) has identified three main MOIP types : overarching mission-oriented strategic frameworks ,
challenge-based programmes and schemes , and ecosystem-based missions. Their main characteristics
have significant implication for their M&E. Overarching mission-oriented strategic frameworks are the
typical national missions that intend to mobilise a large part of a national innovation system towards a
specific challenge (climate change, cancer, etc.). These broad, systemic and open -ended missions often
resemble large , loosely co -ordinated and significantly decentralised umbrella programmes. These
missions gather different authorities around shared goals , and it is often difficult to identify which
interventions and activities concretely fall within the scope of the mission. The main difficulty in evaluating
these entities is how to delineate these missions and assess their actual influence on the policies they
include, especially those that are more remote from the organisations leading the mission.
Challenge-based programmes and schemes might be easier to evaluate at first glance : they are more
limited in scale and scope, their objectives are clearer and more tangible (often of a technological nature),
and they are most often project -based. Measuring their effectiveness , however, is difficult owing to the
high risk of failure of the projects they support : a high level of failure of projects might not be an indicator
of success at the overall programme level. In this case, i t will be essential for eval uators to consider
achievements at the level of the entire portfolio of projects. The impact of these technology -focused
programmes is also difficult to assess , since diffusion and deployment are generally not included in their
scope, while they are essential to MOIPs. While not specific to missions, this is a methodological challenge
with which evaluators are still struggling.
Finally, ecosystem-based missions are initiatives that promote participatory approaches to mobilise large
groups of actors around a common challenge. The ecosystems are empowered to define and update their

Overall, this disparity of MOIP types calls for adapting the evaluation questions and criteria to the type of
MOIP, differentiating for instance between a challenge-based scheme or an overarching systemic mission.
However, referring to the broad MOIP types presented above is not sufficient to adapt the evaluation to
the specific mission at stake. Nor it is possible to rely exclusively on the mission objectives to understand
what effects were expected and simply evaluate whether these were actually delivered. A key initial task
for evaluators is to set the baseline to reconstruct the initial ambitions and how the different partners aimed
to fulfil these through specific mission design, governance and processes. Mission evaluation needs to go
beyond the “what” and explore the “how”, since it is the “how” that justifies the existence of these initiatives
created to add directionality and co-ordinate existing policy interventions. Furthermore, in the early stages
of MOIPs, evaluating the “how” is more informative and important to the long-term success of the mission.
A theory of action that accounts for the systemic effects would be instrumental in achieving this. If not
made available by the mission itself, it should be developed as part of the evaluation , with the evaluation
questions and criteria clearly linked to this theory of action (see below).
Accounting for the complex scope of MOIPs
While some missions (notably the challenge-based programmes) have a well-defined scope, this is rarely
the case for the wider, more systemic and less integrated missions (most often the overarching mission-
oriented strategic frameworks and the ecosystem-based missions).
The mission boundaries are, therefore, often not clear -cut. The formal mission documents (plan, agenda,
annual report , etc.) should in theory help to delineate the “mission space ”, but thes e documents  –
especially the forward-looking agenda and plans  – are more often than not a description of “what should
be” than “what is” or “how to get there” in terms of the contribution of different actors and their respective
interventions within the missions. Similarly, the documents used for broader communication to outsiders
often tend to overemphasise the scope of the mission and the degree of co-operation within it, for obvious
reasons of legitimacy and self-promotion.
The mission scope relates to one of the most critical processes  – i.e. how missions enlist new actors,
interventions, competencies, budgets and portfolios of projects and activities, beyond the initial coalition of
committed partners who  started the initiative – that largely determine their added value, hence the
necessity to feature this aspect prominently in their evaluation. Previous OECD work has shown that many
missions strive to go beyond the traditional science, technology and inno vation ( STI) policy actors and
“attract” sectoral and regulatory authorities in charge of other interventions and activities (not least those
related to deployment at scale , including through regulations, industrial policy measures and economic
incentives).
In this view, missions are often better understood as composed of concentric circles  (Figure 2.1) ,
comprising a core which includes the activities that are directly governed by the mission ( mostly those in
the STI portfolio) , and wider circles where poli cy instruments and activities are less influenced by the

mission lie the activities that are relevant to the mission’s challenge but are not connected to it in practice,
i.e. they have been initiated, programmed and implemented in isolation from the mission decision -making
processes.

In most cases, delineating the mission scope will therefore be (at least to some extent ) a matter of the

simple parameter of the evaluation. In light of the collected evidence, evaluators need to assess the sphere
of influence of the mission  along the three main dimensions of MOIPs:  what interventions, projects and
activities are steered by the mission’s shared goals (mission orientation), and are mutually aligned towards
these goals (mission co-ordination) and combined to contribute jointly to these goals  (mission
implementation)?
One key task for evaluators should be to “map” the missions and define as precisely as possible “what it
is in and what is out” in terms of programmes, projects and activities. To this end, it is useful to distinguish
the mission’s formal scope and the mission’s sphere of influence:
• The evaluator should identify the mission scope, i.e. who (and to what extent) were the partners
engaged in the mission design (for example , the development of the strategic agenda) and
implementation (the actors having committed policy instruments and resources to fulfil t he
mission’s goal). This can be traditionally done using budget data, governance body information
(including meeting attendance lists and activity reporting) and interviews.
• The sphere of influence is more difficult to assess and involves a higher degree o f appreciation
from the evaluator: how far does the influence of the mission extend? Has the mission
overpowered the traditional vertical ( i.e. silo) dynamics that predominantly steer interventions? Is
the mission the strategic framework of reference for m ission partners, or do they still act primarily
towards the specific objectives of their own organisation in this area? Put simply, what is the extent
and intensity of collective active action towards achieving the mission?
This mapping should consider the various layers of mission activities, as presented in Figure 2.1: the core
activities, which are directly decided and funded “as part of the mission”, within the sphere of control of the
mission partners; and the other activities , which are less influenced by the mission or totally outside its
realm, but contribute significantly to the achievement of the mission’s goals.

Given its nested nature, the success of a MOIP initiative will depend on the achievements of its different
entities. For instance, a given project’s impact depends in part on its embeddedness in a portfolio of other
projects designed by the mission; in turn, the mi ssion will depend on the ability of individual projects and
activities to achieve their specific objectives and combine their respective outputs. Against this backdrop,
there exists a need to combine, co-ordinate and balance the evaluations of the different nested entities at
different levels.
Logically, the combination of the evaluations of the different entities participating in a MOIP should obey
three principles:
• “Capillarity”: evaluations should build from the bottom up, with the results of each evaluation at
one level informing the evaluations of the upper levels. The evaluations of the missions themselves
are carried out as part of the evaluation of the MOIP initiative and inform the latter.
• “Subsidiarity”: evaluation at each level should focus on effects with maximum added value which
cannot be achieved at a lower level. For instance, the evaluation of a mission should focus on the
systemic effects resulting from the combina tion of various interventions, projects and activities.
Such systemic effects cannot be captured at the level of any of these interventions, projects and
activities in isolation.
• “Consistency”: the different focuses of evaluations at each level should be c omplemented by
considerations of their immediate lower and upper level s to ensure overall consistency. For
instance, when evaluating individual policy interventions, attention should also be paid to their
degree of alignment with the mission in which they are embedded. Similarly, the evaluation should

consider each project’s fitness in the portfolio. As the mission approach develops and becomes

be modified (or new instruments created) to fit better within missions and match each mission’s

portfolio.
Assessing the double additionality of missions
Additionality is essential in public policy evaluation. It measures what would not have been invested (input
additionality), produced (output additionality) or performed by actors (behavioural additionality) without a
government intervention. Although it most often cannot be measured precisely in the case of innovation
policy because it involves information and counterfactual data that are barely accessible in most cases,4 it
is important at least to structure evaluators’ fieldwork and analysis.
The multidimensional and multilevel nature of MOIPs makes additionality even more difficult to discern. It
involves assessing the additional costs and effects of adopting this particular policy approach relative to
what would have happened if a more traditional STI policy had been used. Since missions build upon and
integrate a package of existing interventions, the baseline (counterfactual) scenario is not the absence of
intervention but the effects of these interventions without a MOIP. Mission evaluations should therefore
assess whether the produced effects are superior to the mere addition of the effects of the combined policy
interventions in their policy mix.
This is one of the most important challenges in evaluating missions. As an additional layer of co-ordination
on top of existing instruments  (and, in rare cases, new instruments  specific to the mission) , mission
evaluation should provide a reliable assessment of “what that better coordination will yield for a better
functioning system compared to a world in which that coordination would not take place”, which is “far more
complex and complicated than evaluating the effect of a single instrument focused more or less directly on
influencing the behaviour of individual entities” (Ter Weel et al., 2022).
Missions are, therefore, confronted with a “double-additionality” challenge (Figure 2):
• The “first-order” (traditional) additionality relates to the additional net effects of any given
intervention included in the mission relative to what would have been t he case in the absence of
this particular public intervention (a “no-intervention scenario ”). This additionality is in principle
measured at the level of interventions (see above).
• The “second-order” additionality is specific to missions. It captures the a dditional effects of the
whole set of interventions taken together as part of the mission, compared to a “no mission/policy-
as-usual scenario”. This additionality should be compared to the additional transaction costs of
missions resulting from the mission’s (expected) stronger and broader co-ordination efforts.

The additionality of missions includes the additionality of each intervention it encompasses (first -order
additionality) and the additionality of the bundling of these interventions within a mission (second -order
additionality). Assuming that the first-order additionality is being taken care of at the level of the evaluation
of individual policies, the evaluation of missions should focus on their “second -order additionality”. While
no quasi-experimental methods can be used to that effect  (even less so than for traditional STI policies) ,
evaluators mainly rely on qualitative evidence drawn from desk reviews and interviews with mission leaders
and partners.  The evaluation questions underpin ning these fieldwork activities should be designed to
capture additional (systemic) effects: are the objectives clearer than in other , more traditional
schemes/programmes in relevant areas? Are the objectives more ambitious? Has the mission accelerated
the development of new solutions or allowed developing solutions (e.g. with a higher performance or wider
reach) that would have been out o f reach of transitional interventions? The evaluation  questions should
also consider the possible “negative additional effects” of the mission (displacements, crowding out, lock-
in, etc.). For instance, has the mission missed the emergence of new areas because its funding is overly
targeted? The evaluation should also take into account a potential increase in the level of administrative
complexity, rigidity and costs owing to the additional co-ordination requirements.
Embracing the evolutive nature of MOIPs
Many missions start with broad objectives, which are gradually refined by the partners as negotiations
progress, trust grows, and additional information is provided and analysed. Once they are set, objectives
can still be revised to reflect not only changes in context and strategy, but also the interests of new actors
who come on  board to complement the initial partners. This is particularly true for ecosystem -based
mission approaches, since the partners themselves define and revise the strategic agen da regularly. In
these types of initiatives, the objectives and the strategic agenda (or roadmap) in which they are embedded

are not the starting point, but rather an important result of the mission. This leads to a “moving target” issue
for evaluators, who need baseline objectives against which to assess the mission achievements.
Furthermore, not only the objectives but the mission itself is evolving. Changes are particularly significant
in their three main building blocks (see Box 1) during their first ye ars, when they gradually negotiate their
objectives and scope (strategic orientation), shape their governance and composition (policy coordination),
and establish corresponding processes and, possibly, joint implementation practices and instruments
(policy implementation). However, missions keep on evolving continuously towards their ‘desired state’
throughout their life cycle.  Following an iterative design process, missions may start with design features
and processes that are far from the mission ‘ideal type’ and incrementally improve, increasing what might
be called their ‘mission-readiness level’.
In a pattern that has been long studied in evolutionary studies, the mission as an institution co-evolves with
the solution it is supposed to generate (e.g. Saviotti, 2005). In this case, the evaluators cannot assume
consistency in the mission design and implementation, as it evolves over its life.
This feature does not lend itself easily to traditional evaluation approaches – especially of the summative
type – that consider mainly the baseline and the endline. Following a developmental evaluation approach,
an important part of the assessment should be the extent to which “the mission becomes a (‘real’) mission”
as it deploys its activities and learn s. The evaluation should consider not only the effects of the mission ,
but also i) how it evolves in terms of design, governance and processes (objective-setting, funding, portfolio
management, etc.); and ii) whether these evolutions correspond to what was expected and motivated the
adoption of a mission approach, and the reasons for any observed gap.
This process dimension of the evaluation in the context of mission yields a broader understanding of
behavioural additionality. The success of the mission will depe nd on the policy makers and their partners
(i.e. not only the beneficiaries) changing their daily practices, routines and mindsets. In a sense, in
missions, policy makers should be considered as a policy target group of their own, since important
behavioural changes are also expected on their side. Evaluation could therefore also cover questions such
as, has the mission become the framework of reference for strategic thinking for the actors involved? Does
the mission overcome vertical thinking and practice?  Is it a small “experimental bubble ” within
organisations, or has it become more central and pervasive?
This evolving nature of missions calls for a developmental approach where the evaluators are engaged in
the intervention to be evaluated on a more conti nuous and long -term basis. The evaluation is ongoing ,
rather than one-off or carried out at specific points in time. It provides continuous input through feedback
loops, from the evaluation to the intervention design and implementation. The evaluation should serve the
mission’s progression, clearly identifying the gaps and pointing towards needed improvements, without
discouraging efforts and hindering learning processes.
Another implication is the need to “ stage” different types of evaluation s with different expectations. Early
in the  mission’s life, the focus should be on the formative/developmental evaluation of the mission
approach (i.e. how the mission processes evolve to produce the systemic effects). As the mission unfolds,
a summative evaluation of the mission (i.e. how it progresses along its milestones towards the realisation
of the final objectives) can complement these evaluations.
Adopting a nuanced approach: More is not always better
While a mission’s evaluation should be underpinned by an ambitious ideal  type to assess its mission -
readiness level (i.e. its progression towards a more “fully fledged” mission), as mentioned above,  the
precise evaluation benchmark needs to be adapted to each specific mission case. Not all missions should
aim for the highest position in all three mission dimensions:

• Regarding orientation, some missions might consider a less directional approach  (at least at the
outset) to give mission partners more autonomy to set their strategic agenda (this is particularly
the case for ecosystem-based missions).
• Regarding co-ordination, there exist different types and intensities of co-ordination, and the
broadest and strongest form of co-ordination is not adequate for all missions. 5 The need for co -
ordination will largely depend on the national institutional specificities and the characteristics of the
challenge (level of uncertainty, complexity, contestability). Excessive co-ordination can generate
heightened transaction costs, compl exity, rigidity, slowdown of action, lack of leadership, dilution
of priorities and blurred accountability. Experiences in some of the MOIPs that have deployed a
fully systemic approach, such as the Dutch Top Sectors and Mission -Driven Policy, shows that
complex co-ordination can generate significant “mission fatigue”.
• Similarly, when it comes to mission implementation, full integration of the different instruments is
not always either possible or desirable. In some cases, a smart combination of interventions can
be sufficient, less costly and more flexible.
These three arguments  call for a more nuanced and strategic approach, grounded in an ex -ante
assessment of interdependencies and the need for information exchange, plan alignment and joint actions.
It is important to consider the structural features of the challenge area  being addressed by the mission.
The level of complexity, uncertainty and contestability, in particular, will have different implications in terms
of the appropriate design of the missions . While it is not possible to propose a deterministic view which
holds that an optimal design exists for any given challenge, understanding how the characteristics of an
area have influenced the mission design and challenges should be one task of the evaluators.

The previous section focused on the specific features of mission-oriented innovation policies (MOIPs) and
their implications for monitoring and evaluation (M&E). Notably, it points towards the use of a
developmental evaluation approach that focuses on tracking the evolution of the mission design and
processes, and assessing their systemic effects. This ass essment of the mission additionality – which
firmly establishes its raison d’être – requires a good understanding of the unique “theory of action” that
underpinned the chosen mission design and processes in a particular institutional and thematic context.
This section proposes mission -evaluation criteria, processes and tools that are consistent with this
evaluation approach.
Setting mission-evaluation criteria
As mentioned in Section 2 , MOIP evaluations should focus on assessing the specific “second-order
additionality” of th is policy approach, meaning the extent to which missions generate systemic effects
(positive or negative, intentional or not) on top of those produced by the instruments they intend to steer,
co-ordinate and integrate. Missions’ expected systemic effects – i.e. the effects produced by the collective
actions within missions (collective development of a strategic agenda, alignment of plans toward these
objectives, bundling of instruments and portfolio management). These effects are the reason for this policy
approach, which addresses challenges for which traditional ( i.e. less directional, co -ordinated and
integrated) innovation policy approaches are considered less effective. Since missions are by nature
tailored to a specific challenge in a particular context (subnational, national, international), no two missions
are alike in principle , and the evaluation will need to be adapted to each mission. However, there exist
archetypal effects that are expected from any mission, in di fferent proportions and at different degrees of
intensity, depending on the mission objectives and context.
Based on previous work and fieldwork conducted as part of this study, the OECD proposes four main
groups of mission effects:6 directionality effects, governance effects, portfolio effects and material effects.
For each of these, specific criteria can be derived to guide the evaluative work. These criteria define the
standards and benchmark by which missions ’ accomplishments or suitability should be assessed (Figure
3). These criteria are complementary to traditional evaluation criteria , such as those of the OECD
Development Assistance Committee (DAC), 7 which are used at the level of the evaluation of individ ual
interventions.

Directionality effects
Directionality effects refer to the strategic orientation and focus of publ ic interventions targeting specific
societal challenges. This involves setting clear, ambitious, measurable/evaluable and legitimate objectives
to drive research and development (R&D) in a purposeful direction, often addressing complex ,
multidimensional issues. This contrasts with traditional neutral or functional STI policies , which focus on
specific market failures to address predominantly growth- and competitiveness-related objectives.
The proposed criteria under this group of effects are:
• Mobilisation: the extent to which the mission brings together a set of public and private actors with
the resources, skills and authority necessary to address a complex challenge  (regardless of their
belonging to specific institutions, sectors or disciplines) to devel op and implement a common
strategic agenda. The r elated effects proposed by evaluation experts during the Mission M&E
workshop (see Annex A) were “inspirational” and “motivational” effects.
• Intentionality: the extent to which the mission has involved a wid e array of public and private
stakeholders (including citizen s) in the collective development of clear and ambitious objectives
with accompanying SMART, as well as a strategic agenda that spells out the different possible
pathways to meet these objectives.
• Flexibility: a mission’s capacity to revise as regularly as necessary its objectives and strategic
agenda, based on information derived from strategic policy intelligence and lessons learned from
implementation.
• Relevance: the extent to which the strategi c agenda is pertinent to the underlying problem and
effectively addresses the underlying causal issues; the level of alignment of the envisioned actions
with the scale and scope of the complex challenge.

Governance effects
Governance effects refer to the collaborative steering, resource mobilisation and allocation, management
and monitoring of various public interventions aimed at the mission objectives  to ensure that innovation
efforts are aligned with the mission and are conducted efficiently responsibly , transparently and
accountably.
The proposed criteria under this group of effects are:
• Alignment: the extent to which the investment and activity plans of the mission’s public and private
partners are coherent and consistent with the mission objectives
• Commitment: the extent to which the mission attracts resources ( e.g. financial, technical, in-kind)
from various public and private actors to implement the mission, regardless of their specific
institutions, sectors and disciplines
• Efficiency: the extent to which the mission delivers or is likely to deliver results in an economic and
timely way, and with a manageable level of complexity.
Portfolio effects
Portfolio effects describe the various synergies stemming from a combination of innovation projects and
investments that were strategically selected and supported by different public interventions to collectively
meet the mission objectives. This approach recognises that no single project or activity can solve
multifaceted issues . It  enhances the likelihood of fulfilling the mission by distributing risk, leveraging
different types of complementarities (e.g. along different stages of the value chain, different generations of
technologies, different components of a socio technical system), and promoting a holistic approach to
innovation.
The proposed criteria under this group of effects are:
• Policy-mix integration: the extent to which the mission combines efficiently and effectively a variety
of complementary policy interventions to achieve the mission
• Portfolio consistency : the extent to which the mission supports a coherent and aligned set of
innovation projects and activities that collectively contribute to the mission’s objectives
• Exploration: the extent to which the mission investigat es a wide range of innovative  (including
unconventional) ideas, solutions and pathways and cultivates openness, creativity and
experimentation in the pursuit of the mission’s objectives.
Material effects
Material effects refer to the concrete outcomes and impacts of missions in relation to their objectives. As
highlighted in Section 2, missions must not only fulfil their objectives, but also fulfil them more effectively
and efficiently than would have occurred with traditional approaches – or they should fulfil objectives that
are beyond the reach of these traditional approaches.
The proposed criteria under this group of effects are:
• Effectiveness: the extent to which the mission has achieved, or is expected to achieve, its
objectives and meet its targets in the relevant time frame, including any differential results across
groups or components of the mission
• Transformation: the extent to which the mission significantly alters existing technical, economic,
social or environmental systems, practices and structures to meet its objectives. The contribution

Setting the mission baseline: The “mission theory of action”
What is a theory of action?
Theories of change are increasingly used to guide the design, implementation and evaluation of complex
and adaptative interventions. They are logical diagrams that display the expected processes of change on
the material side, i.e. the objects upon which the policy interventions act. They link, for instance, the
development of new carbon capture and storage systems to a reduction of GHG emissions through a
succession of causal linkages.
Alongside these theories of change, theories of action are  tools that track the specific mission processes
and the systemic effects they are expected to generate to tackle a specific challenge. In a nutshell, a theory
of action is inward-looking and process-oriented. It is “the delivery model for a Theory of Change” (George,
2024). It describes how the mission is designed and set up to achieve the mission. It maps the chains of
causality that connect the way missions are designed and governed to the impact they are expected to
produce. Rather than focusing only on the material dimension of missions (is the mission being achieved
and the selected challenge solved?), mission theories of action track the key processes that lie at the heart
of the MOIP approach, and allow assessing whether they unfold as expected and produce their “systemic
effects” (i.e. is the adopted approach effective at tackling the selected challenge?)
Theories of change and theories of action are therefore two faces of the same coin, providing
complementary views on the expected process of change that should lead to fulfilling the mission. Mission
theories of action can arguably prove particularly instrumental to both mission managers and evaluators in
a developmental approach. In the case of MOIPs, theories of change  should surface the expected causal
relationships between the problems these policies tackle and their desired goals, inputs, outputs, outcomes
and impacts (Janssen et al., 2021; Hekkert et al., 2020). These maps of mission “contribution claims” can
prove particularly instrumental in guiding and monitoring their portfolio of activities, to verify they are
coherent and aim in the right direction.
An example of a mission theory of action
The OECD used a theory of action to assess whether and under which conditions MOIPs are better suited
than traditional STI strategic and policy frameworks to support ing countries’ efforts to meet their net -zero
target (OECD, 2024a, Figure 4). The OECD used this tool to analyse a database of about 100  “net-zero
missions” and 20 in-depth case studies.
Like a mission theory of change, a theory of action consists of several stages, from the mission design
principles to the achievement of the mission’s objectives:
• The starting point of the theory of action is the description of its main features along the three
dimensions of orientation, co-ordination and implementation.  These features describe the
processes and structures (incl uding governance bodies) that set objectives and targets, co -
ordinate vertically and horizontally  actions, build synergies between funding streams, combine
instruments to support a consistent portfolio of activities, etc.

Missions are living entities. Not only their objectives but also their forms, shapes and content evolve as
they learn and gradually come closer to what they are expected to be and perform. Against this backdrop,
mission M&E approaches are also changing and improving. However, this co -evolution only functions
through real case-based studies and evaluations. These provide essential feedback on mission processes
and effects, and are also key to refining the M&E approaches and methods.
Relying upon desk research, previous OECD work on missions, inputs from mission M&E experts (see
Annex A), a dedicated benchmarking study and a Mission Action Lab experiment in Austria, this paper
proposes a M&E framework combining different types of evaluations at different stages o f a mission’s life
cycle, with tailor-made criteria and tools to capture the systemic effects it is expected to generate.
The results of this work, which has already been applied to several experiments, will require more thinking
and interactions and will be “stress-tested” in other contexts, at different levels. The next steps will be as
follows: i) apply the developmental evaluation framework and guidelines proposed in this paper to real
cases to define practical evaluation terms of reference , including concrete indications on the scope, the
steps, the criteria to be used, the related evaluation questions, the methods and the overall governance of
the evaluation; ii) improve and apply the tools to other mission M&E cases in the near future  (the OECD
Mission Action Lab is currently in discussions with several missions for that purpose ); and iii) define a
dynamic approach to mission M&E, with clear indications regarding the types of M&E activities to be carried
out at different moments of the mission life cycle.
Engaging in real cases of mission M&E is the condition  for delivering effective and practical tools and
methods that are able to assess what a French mission policy maker called the “supplément d’âme” of
missions.

Annex A. The Mission M&E expert meeting
An expert meeting, held on 3 and 4 October 2023, aimed to develop collectively some of the critical
components of an M&E practical framework through prior document -based consultations and structured
discussions. It gathered some key mission M&E experts (see the list below) for two half days . Several
OECD officials from across the organisation (STI, Public Governance, Development Co-operation, Internal
Evaluation) also attended the meeting. All of them have confronted the challenge of the mission M&E from
different angles and in various contexts  (theoretical and/or practical ) related to regional, national or EU
missions.
In stark contrast with most traditional workshops, where each expert presents their own work on a specific
issue, this meeting proceeded thr ough successive sessions that contributed to the final aim established
above. The experts were asked to work in the broad context of the analytical framework developed by the
OECD. An early part of the meeting focused on a critical review and revision of t his framework to ensure
it is relevant and does not miss important points. Once this was accomplished, experts worked within this
framework to derive the practical resources (including the evaluation’s scope, questions, criteria,
methodological approaches and steps) needed to guide policy makers and evaluators as they attempt to
monitor and evaluate missions. The discussions were expected to lead towards clear and practical
guidance – i.e. away from participants' theoretical frameworks, while building on these.
Discussions were informed by the inputs provided by experts before the meeting via a dedicated template.
The experts’ inputs were compared, synthetised and used as the main background material for the
meeting.
Both the template and meeting agenda were  structured along missions’ groups of (expected) effects.
Having listed these groups of effects, experts were asked to focus on each of them successively to
determine their content and definition ; the relevant criteria, markers and indicators ; and the methodology
for assessing them in concrete -use cases. The template and meeting , therefore, used a logic of inquiry
inspired by theory -based evaluation approaches, whereby the identification of the expected causal
linkages that underpin missions’ effects is a prerequisite for evaluation fieldwork.
The main questions discussed at the expert meeting were:
1. Does the OECD mission M&E framework seem adequate for underpinning M&E efforts for
mission-oriented policies? What is missing or possibly misleading? How could its content and formulation
be improved?
2. What are the two or three main groups of  effects expected from typical mission -oriented policy
initiatives?
3. For each group of effects:
• What are the main mechanisms/causal linkages underpinning this type of effect?
• At what level(s) (MOIP initiative, specific mission, specific instrument within  the mission) should
evaluation capture this type of effect?
• What are the corresponding evaluation questions and evaluation criteria/markers/indicators?
• How can one assess this type of effect? What are suitable tools/methods?
