Executive Summary

The goals of the K-Innovation ODA Program are to build each partner country ’s
capacity to formulate STI policy for implementing the project , with the s pecific aim of
promoting the project’s purposes . The K -Innovation ODA Program’s profe ssional and
academic experience in designing strategies have enabled the Kingdom of Cambodia
(Cambodia) project to produce both the national strategies and technology roadmaps.
This is the third year of phase 2 of the K -Innovation project with Cambodia. Phase 1 of
the project was carried out from 2018 to 2019 under the title of ‘Policy Consultation
on the Development of a National STI Policy and Management of the STI System for
Cambodia's Policy makers’. During the second year of phase 2 in 2021, it was
recognized that the project had produce d some meaningful outcomes despite the
restrictions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic. Based on the experience s and lessons
learned from the first year, the project’s third year was effectively conducted in similar
ways to the second year, utilizing online meetings and drawing on support from local
institutions in Cambodia. In addition to the way the project is being operated, STEPI
and the Ministry of Industry, Science, Technology & Innovation (MISTI) of the Royal
Government of Cambodia have concluded a service contract to develop the first
national technology roadmap of Cambodia.
The consultation process for the project comprised the following activities as explained below:
As in the first and second years, the whole process of the 2 nd phase of the project was
implemented online due to the ongoing COVID-19 pandemic. Since direct face -to-face
activities were discouraged in both countries, the kick -off meeting, co ntract signing
process and ceremony, and a training workshop were held online or via e-mail.

The contracts were signed in early August 2022. MISTI selected three local institutions from
among government organizations and academia based in the STI field. MISTI’s main
activities consisted in organizing and operating national TRM committees for three selected
technologies. The main roles of each committee were as follows: 1) to elaborate and identify
the scope of the selected technologies; 2) to develop TRM for each selected technology
based on the guidelines established by STEPI; 3) to collect opinions from interested groups
and build a consensus on the developed TRM; and 4) to finalize the TRM and submit the
report. MISTI also designated the principal institutes and a project manager (PM) for each
technology roadmap (TRM) for the contract with STEPI.
The training workshop was convened on August 3, 2022, and Korean experts reviewed
the draft version of the roadmap shared by three local organizations and provide d
relevant feedback. The roadmap focuses on three categories of technologies: digital,
energy, and tourism. The review workshop was held virtually (i.e. online) on November
24, 2022 to produce a final version of the roadmaps. The three reports were finalized
and submitted in late December after a series of rev iews of the first drafts with Korean
experts. The main output of the reports was the establishment of a concrete
technology development strategy that can be viewed as a natio nal policy plan for
fostering the relevant experts and government officials.
Despite the numerous difficulties caused by the Omicron variant of COVID-19
pandemic in 2022, STEPI was able to accomplish the ob jectives of the project. First , it
succeeded in developing three additional national technology roadmaps of Cambodia,
i.e. roadmaps for digital, energy, and tourism technologies, in addition to three TRMs,
i.e. agricultural, educational, and health technologies, in 2021. Furthermore, Korea and
Cambodia have successfully accumulate d sufficient experience to explore a ‘more
effective and safer’ ODA project during the COVID-19 pandemic era.
Finally, the overall efficiency and effectiveness of the project were carefully managed
throughout the project process. MSITI and two other local organizations managed the
budget for each activity in a thorough and professional manner, and also develop ed
technology roadmaps which could serve as stepping stones to designing, implementing,
and monitoring the Science, Technology and Innovation field in Cambodia.

1. Introduction

This program is a follow -up to the second phase of the “2018-2019 Policy Consulting
Program for the Establishment of STI Policy in Cambodia” carried out as a part of the K-
Innovation project. In the first phase of the project, the National STI Policy 2020-2030,
the Cambodian government's first national science and technology master plan, was
established and adopted as a key outcome of policy consulting.
Based on the trust developed between STEPI and the Cambodian governme nt
following the successful implementation of the first phase of the program, the
Cambodian government requested and promoted the “Consulting Program for Building
a National Technology Roadma p” for the implementation of the pla n as a second
phase project.
However, due to the outbreak and spread of the COVID -19 pandemic at the beginning
of 2020, which has made visits to Cambodia and in vitational training in Korea equally
difficult to implement , the second phase of the program has been carried out largely
through online-centered cooperation and consulting activities.
In 2021, the second year, the local partner organization was changed from GS -NSTC to
the Ministry of Industry, Science, Technology, and Innovation (MISTI) following a

1. Summary of Cambodia’s 2020 STI Policies

1.1 Cambodia’s Sustainable Development Goals (2016-2030)
A project to establish Cambodia’s Sustainable Development Goals (CSDG s) was
launched in late 2015 by researching and mapping the global visions and targets while
taking into consideration national priorities. The Government of Cambodia chose a
total of seventeen SDGs, and included an extra goal r elated to the clearance of
landmines and “Explosive Remnants of War ” (reflecting the national priority of de -
mining of the national territory) (STEPI, 2020). The final version of the CSDGs consists
of the followin g: 18 Cambodian Sustainable Development Goals, 88 nationally
important targets, and 148 (global and locally defined) indicators (STEPI, 2020).2
The CSDG Framework has four clear goals:
• Presentation of the national goals, targets, and indicators based on Cambodia’s
main priorities;
• Identification of the organizations’ important activities to accomplish the targets;
• Identification of data sources for each indicator and the data cycle, with provisions
on the working definitions and the methods for computing the indicators;
• Presentation of pathways toward achievement of the targets, setting (2015) of the
national baselines and the annual (or cycle -based) target values, and their
implementation at the sub-national and regional levels (CSDGs, 2016) (STEPI, 2020).

1.2 Cambodia’s Industrial Development Plan (2015-2025)
Cambodia’s industry remains weak due to the family -focused nature of many
production activities , the absence of entrepreneurship , and insufficient use of
technology, all of which restrains the country’s ability to compete in international
markets to a significant extent . The crucial characteristics of the country ’s industrial
weakness can be summarized as follows: a weak industrial base; a misplaced middle
and informal industrial structure; weak, urban -centered entrepreneurship; low value
addition; and a low level of technology application (STEPI, 2020).
However, coordination among additional regulators remains problematic, and
sometimes it varies relevant to their own goals. For instance, the tax syst em puts
immoderate weight on tax collection, whereas the trade system pays attention to
market openness. Such absence of coordination triggers unnecessary barriers to the
implementation of industrial development objectives.

2. STI Policy Roadmap and Survey

2.1 Introduction
In order to achieve the strategies and ultimate goals of the Rectangular Strategy (RS)
Phase IV and the National Strategic Development Plan 2019 -2023, the Royal
Government of Cambodia ( RGC) needs to determine the priorities of the national
strategy for technology development, w hich are crucial to keeping Cambodia’s
economy alive amid the effects of the global Covid-19 pandemic.
This survey includes text and illustrations about the results of a small survey conducted
by the MoP research team investigating the current uses of technology from public
and pri vate institutions that were randomly selected for interview and respondents
who filled out the forms. The survey aimed to review strategic technology candidates
and their current activities in Cambodia and their suggestions; highlight the main
technology areas from both the short-term and long -term perspectives, and analyze
the economic impact s, technical feasibility, national safety, and security concerns
according to the responses of the interviewees.

2.2 Method of Investigation
The team develop ed the questionnaires and data sets and organized a list of the top
twelve technology areas and some technology candidates to make it easy and
convenient for the interviewers and respondents to select or fill in the 10 technology
areas and 10 strategic technology candidates for each question. All experts were
cordially asked to answer the questions based on the given guidelines and conditions.
The GS -NSTC staff were assigned to interview experts from the selected sample size
because their ideas would be key to identifying the current status of technology
development and challenges in Cambodia . After carefully considering only those
candidates with a high educational background and abundant experience in the field of
S&T, a total of fifty respondents were selected and interviewed face-to-face.

2.1 Introduction
2.1.1 Background
The United Nations Agenda 2030 highlights access to energy in its Sustainable
Development Goal (SDG) No. 7, which aims to provide “affordable, reliable, sustainable
and modern energy for everyone by the year 2030” (UN, 2015). SDG 7 is considered a
medium-term goal that will contribute to the achievement of other goals. As stated in
SDG 7, renewable energy includes solar energy, hydropower, wind turbine s, bioenergy
(waste-energy), geothermal energy, hydrogen and fuel cell energy, nuclear power and
ocean energy. The availability of energy has transformed the course of humanity over
the last few centuries (Mika et al., 2021).
Based on the report of the International Energy Agency (IEA), some 770 million people
around the world did not have access to electricity as of 2019 (IEA, 2019). The
accelerating progression of this trend is largely the result of the expansion of electricity
grids and the use of fossil fuels (IEA, 2015). Electricity generation is still heavily reliant
on fossil fuels, nuclear energy or large -scale hydropower , making the sustainability
aspects o f energy sector development somewhat problematic (Mika, et al., 2021).
Currently, various sources of energy are being used around the world, including fossil
fuels, nuclear power, hydropower and other renewable technologies. Indeed, t he
energy system has transformed dramatically since the days of the Industrial Revolution.
Demand for energy is growing massively across many countries around the world, as
societies become increasingly affluent and populations increase (Hannah Ritchie).
Based on the World Count (TWC) , a global energy consumption observatory , global
energy consumption will reach 740 million terajoules (additional growth of 30%) by
2040. The Association of Southeast Asia n Nations (ASEAN) was considered the
seventh-largest economy and the fifth -largest investment destination in 2016. As one
of the largest economic coalitions, ASEAN has a cumulati ve GDP of USD2.5+ trillion.
This vigorous economic growth has increased energy demand by 70% compared to
2000, and the region currently accounts for 5% of total global energy demand.
Therefore, given that the region is experiencing ever increasing energy demand, each
country will need to make an effort to meet the pr ojected target ( Yang Liu, 2020). To

there can be more coal in un-surveyed areas, preliminary estimates of the deposits are
over 150 million tons in northern Cambodia. These small deposits could supply enough
coal for a 400-megawatt (MW) coal power plant for thirty years (JDI, 2009). Th e coal is
of the lignite type (ERIA, 2022) , and is similar in quality to Thai coal, which has a
calorific value of 3,000 kilocalories per kilogram and is suitable as boiler or kiln fuel (JDI,
2009). However, no commercial-scale coal mining has ever taken place in the country
to date (ADB, 2018).

Oil and gas: Seismic data show that the country has more oil and gas reserve potential
in onshore areas than offshore areas. There are nineteen onshore petroleum blocks (I-

untapped, leaving aside the fact tha t it has no oil refineries. In May 2017, a US$ 2.3

straddling the provinces of Kampot and Sihanoukville. This will be the country’s first-
ever refinery, with a projected annual production capacity of 5 million tons of oil upon
its completion (ADB, 2018).

2.2.1.2 Renewable Energy Resources
Biomass: The country has plenty of biomass energy resources deriving from rubber
plantation forests, tropical trees such as Gliricidia and Acacia , and agricultural residues
such as rice husks, rice straw, corn cobs, waste from palm oil extraction, cassava stalks,
sugarcane bagasse, and cashew nut shells, as well as animal waste. The estimated total
theoretical potential of agricultural residues alone is around 15,000 g igawatt hours
(GWh) per year, 80 percent of which could be converted from rice residues (See Annex
1). By 2017, seven biomass generation power plants were in operation in Cambodia,
representing roughly 39 MW of the installed capacity. Th e production of biofuels for
transport also uses biomass. A joint -venture with a Korean company, MH Bio-Energy
Group (ADB, 2018), produces nearly 36 million liters of bioethanol per year from

much as 2.4 million cubic meters of biogas per day (See Annex 2 ). The fact that most
agricultural holdings nationwide are small -scale, however, means that the t echnical

Hydropower: There are sixty-three possible locations across the country for the
development of small and l arge hydropower projects (MME, 2019), fifty percent of
which are located in the Mekong River basin, forty percent on its tributaries, and ten
percent in the southwestern coastal highlands. Hydropower generation varies
according to seasonal changes such that it is higher in the rainy season than in the dry
season. The country’s techn ical hydropower potential is estimated to be about 10,000
MW. Currently, only 980 MW of this capacity has been developed, 400 MW is under
construction, and 90 MW is in the feasibility-study stage (ADB, 2018).
Solar energy: The country has a considerably high level of solar irradiation, rendering it
rich in solar energy resources. The daily average solar irradiation is around 5 kilowatt
hours (kWh), with daily av erage sunshine of 6 -9 hours (MME, 2019). The Global
Horizontal Irradiation (GHI) is valued at 1,644-2,009 kWh per square meter per year,
while the Direct Normal Irradiation (DNI) ranges from 1,168 to 1,607 kWh per square
meter per year. Most parts of the country have a GHI level of 1,826 kWh per square
meter per year or more , and a DNI level of 1,314 kWh per square meter per year or
more ( See Annex es 3 and 4). The total suitable land area for solar photovoltaics
development is 134,500 square kilometers, which corresponds to a maximum technical
solar energy potential of around 8.1 megawatt -peak (MWp) or 12 ter awatt hours
(TWh) per year and a maximum economic solar energy potential of around 7.2 MWp
or 10.8 TWh per year (see Annex 5) (ADB, 2015).
Wind energy: The country has lim ited wind energy potential as only 3 -5 percent of its
total land area has wind resources of medium intensity viable for utility -scale wind
turbines. The annual average wind speed is between 6 -9 meters per second in the

2.2.2 Primary Energy Supply
The country’s domestic energy only includes hydropower and biomass as its other energy
resources remain unutilized. To meet the local energy needs, it imports sub -bituminous
coal primarily from Indon esia (GGGI, 2018); petroleum from Singapore, Thailand, and
Vietnam; and electricity from Laos, Thailand, and Vietnam. Its import dependency ratio13
was 68 percent in 2019, rising from 35 percent in 2000. Greater reliance on energy imports
could pose a greater threat to its energy supply security (ERIA, 2022).
The total primary energy supply (TPES) 14 increased from 1,956 kilotons of oil
equivalent (ktoe) in 2000 t o 6,885 ktoe in 2019. In the same period, the supply of
petroleum increased from 670 ktoe to 3,10 4 ktoe, that of hydropower from 3 ktoe to
346 ktoe, that of biomass from 1,279 ktoe to 1,902 ktoe, and that of imported
electricity from 4 ktoe to 263 ktoe. Coal began to be imported in 2008, rising from 14
ktoe in that year to 1,270 ktoe in 2019. Petroleum now makes up around 45 percent of
TPES, replacing biomass as the most dominant energy source. The steeper slope of the
TPES curve over time indicates that su pply is increasing faster than before. Its growth
rate was almost two times higher in 2010-2019 than in 2000-2009 (See Figure 4-2).
This trend toward growth of the primary energy supply can be explained by the
growing need for energy to support the rapid economic developmen t of Cambodia ,
which is seeing a greater influx of foreign direct investments in the construction of
commercial buildings, infrastructures, and economic zones (ERIA, 2022).

2.4 Development of the National Energy Technology Roadmap
This section describes the pro cess of developing t he Energy Technology Roadmap
(Energy Tech Road map), a document designed to serve as a guideline for the
development of key technologies to be implemented by 2030, in order to achieve the
co-development consensus-building vision. Additionally, this roadmap gives
government, private sector, and academic institution s investment priority and sets the
guiding principles for the technologies listed in this roadmap. According to background
knowledge, the primary objectives of the Energy Tech Roadmap are as follows:
• Establish a technology roadmap to support th e implementation strategy for the
National STI Policy 2020-2030.
• Provide a strategic direction for resolving the difficulties facing energy technology.

The following steps make up the process of development of the Energy Tech Roadmap:
1- The first step consists in defining the vision and goals for Cambodia’s energy sector
over the next eight years, as well as the essential technologies required to develop
the sector globally, and more particularly aims to contribute to and help
accelerate national economic growth.
2- The second step consists of an environmental analysis that takes into
consideration social, technol ogical, economic, environmental and political
concerns, and then , depending on the drivers, an evaluation of the opportunities
and threats in the energy sector.
3- The third step aims to determine potential function s, strategic products and
services. Then, the expert committee score s the priority products and services to
determine the final selection.
4- The fourth stage consists in identifying key technologies. The expert committees
then rank the most important technologies and choose the priority key
technologies.
5- Finally, a technolo gy roadmap is created with timeframes ranging from the short
to the long term.

2.8 Conclusion
Energy is a mandatory resource that is required for human survival and societal
advancement. Energy is not only used for economic development, but it is also crucial
from a political and strategic perspective. No country, without internal energy security,
can develop fully if it relies on energy sources from other countries. Th us, the
development of energy production has a direct impact on the development and
security of a nation. A nation's economic and social development require increasingly
huge a mounts of energy each year as its civilization develops. The lack of energ y
hampers urban and economic growth as well as population expansion.
Due to the possibility of a severe energy crisis and concerns about the depletion of
fossil fuels, the energy structure has been modified over time: Until relatively recently
it was dominated by fossil fuels such as petroleum, natural gas and coal, but it has now
switched to renewable energy sources including solar, wi nd, ocean, biomass, and
geothermal energy, and potential new energy sources such as nuclear and hydrogen.
This pattern could lead to a significant evolution of energy technology. As such, science
and technology are key factors in the advancement of energy technology that will in
turn bring about radical changes in socioeconomic growth. Throughout the history of
humanity economic development has been associated with energy transition s ranging
from human labor to steam engine s to electricity to what we see around us today.
Human civilization has gradually transformed from an agrarian society into an
industrial civilization thank s to an energy evolution based on scientific and
technological progress.
Cambodia must have a clear strategic plan such as this roadmap in order to utilize
science, technology and innovation to ensure energy security for its prosperous
economic development in the future as well as sustainable and inclusive growth. This
energy technology roadmap has identified the technologies that Cambo dia need s to
adopt for its future development in order to realize the vision of becoming an energy
self-sufficient nation by deploying technology and innov ation and leveraging
renewable energy sou rces for sustainable development by 2030 , based on an

• Government p olicies and incentivized scheme s are mandatory to meet the need

• Progress in Science, Technology and Innovation (STI) is crucial for national development

• Talents play a central rol e in this energy roadmap as well as in other energy

3.3.2 Technology Application in Tourism
Over the last few decades, it has been observed that tourism development has
significantly benefited from the development and introduction of innovative
technologies to the market (Gössling, 2021). The transformation of technology in the
tourism industry can be seen throughout different periods of time from the 1970s to
the present, and the industry has been transformed accordingly. The first stage, from
the 1970s to 1980 s, saw the development of Computer Reservation Systems (CRSs)
and Global Distribution Systems (GDSs) which changed the modes of operation of
different sectors of the tourism industry, most particularly the hospitality industry, i.e.
the reservation of accommodation and food and beverages, and the air transportation
industry, i.e. the booking of flight tickets (Buhalis & Law, Progress in Information
Technology and Tourism Management: 20 years on and ten years after the Internet —
the state of e -Tourism researc h, 2008). Technology has significantly improved the
efficiency and effectiveness of business operations, particularly through the sharing of
information between different departments in the hotel industry (Koo, Gretzel, Hunter,
& Chung, 2015).
During the second stage, i.e. the late 1990s to 2000, the beginning of Internet
technology and the development of the World Wide Web transformed every aspect of
the tourism business and visitor experiences (Buhalis & Inversini, 2014) . These
technology developments mo dernized and revolutionized communication in the
tourism industry, changing the marketing and management strategies and the
approaches of tourism business operators a nd destinations (Buhalis, 2000; Drosos,
Chalikias, Skodoulis, Kalantonis, & Papagrigoriou, 2017).
During the latest stage, from 2000 to the present, smart technologies have substantially
and rapidly transformed tourism business transactions, marketing and promotion

3.3.2.1 Internet of Things
The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to the refers to the network of physical objects —
“things”—that are embedded with sensors, software, and other technolo gies for the
purpose of connecting and exchanging data with other devices and systems over the
internet.” (Also see Wise & Heidari for a definition , 2019). It can allow new types of
real-time data, traffic -aware car navigation, smart homes, smart cities, and smar t
wearable devices and clothes. The IoT is one of the essential forces behind the
expansion of big data (Mauro, Greco, & Grimaldi, 2015). In the tourism sector, it
provides opportunities for greater access to tourism information, social media
interaction, content marketing, big data and wearables (Wise & Heidari, 2019). The IoT
allows tourists to access a massive variety of information on tourism destination
products and services, including accommodation, food and beverages, communication,
and transporation. Thus, the IoT vitial for bouth current and future tourism, along with
the rapid growth of advanced technologies and the Internet.

3.4 Scope and Approach
3.4.1 Scope
The roadmap is limited to the application of technologies to tourism from 2022 to
2030 in Cambodia, and does not cover the use of technologies in other sectors beyond
2030. However, because tourism encompasses a wide number of sectors, including
accommodation, transportation, tourist attractions, tour operators and travel agencies,
and entertainment, the technologies used in these sectors can be included in the
roadmap.

3.4.2 Approach
The Tourism Technology Roadmap has been developed with a qualitative approach.
First, the literature relevant to tourism, technology trends, especially in Cambodia, and
digital tourism was collected. Then, the stakeholders relevant to tourism were

3.5 Roadmap Development Process
The development of the roadmap consisted of five steps. The first step consists of
building the vision of the Tourism Technology Roadmap 2030, and initiating the goals
in accordance with the vision elaborated in this step. The second step consists in
analzying the overall environment, which includes the social, tec hnological,
environmental, economic, and political environments. The third step consists in
selecting the strategic products and services required to fulfil the vision. Product
priority will also be determined in this step. The fourth step is to determine the
technologies for product/service development. The fifth and final step co nsists in
creating a chart to link the strategic products and services and technologies to the
target market.

3.6 Vision and Goal
3.6.1 Vision
Cambodia’s Tourism Technology Roadmap 2030 has the vision of making Cambodia
“one of the top tourism destinations in the region by adopting innovative and
transformative technologies in order to develop a competitive, sustainable and
inclusive tourism industry by 2030”.

3.6.2 Goals
To achieve this vision, the following goals are proposed:
(1) Improve the technology literacy and skills of tourism operators and users
The stakeholders in the tourism industry, including the private and public sectors and
non-governmental organizations, need to improve their digital/technological literacy
and skills in order to better understand how to use advanced technologies and skills.

They can improve such literacy and skills through both formal and non -formal
education. Therefore, more courses on advanced technology and digital literacy and
skills should be developed and made widely available to them.
(2) Strengthen technology infrastructure support
The supporting infrastructure should be more developed and monitored to adopt and
transform technology for the tourism industry in 2030. The infrastructure could include
hardware, software, and networks. For instance, phone networks, coverage, and the
internet should be more developed. Infrastructure development will provide both the
supply and demand sides with greater convenience in using technology.
(3) Improve cyber security
Deeper knowledge of technology and the availability of infrastructural support can
lead tourism operators to adopt technology, provide that security related to
technology and technology usage is guaranteed. Improving the security for technology
users, for instance, data security and safety, could build trust among technology users,
inducing them to adopt new technologies free from worry.
(4) Enforce the utilization of advanced technologies to improve tourism business
operations in Cambodia
As advanced technologies are essential for busines s operations, business operators
tend to adopt new technologies in order to gain a competitive advantage for their
business. However, enforcing and encouraging the use of advanced technologies
among tourism business operators, especially by the government, could help speed up
the adoption and transformation of technologies for tourism -related businesses by
2030.
(5) Enable technology adoption and transformation in the tourism industry
As mentioned in the foregoing no. 4, business operators usually adopt new
technologies to benefit their business. Therefore, enabling the adoption and
transformation of technologies will help to promote and foster their use.

3.7 STEEP Analysis
STEEP analysis is conducted to identify the drivers that could push the adoption of new
technologies by 2030. It covers the social, technological, economic, environmental, and
political environments.

3.7.1 Social Environment
Social factors have been reported to affect the adoption of new technologies. Such
factors include population growth, the youn g generation, the Covid -19 pandemic,
better and higher education and literacy skills, globalization, religion, the durability of
life, changes in lifestyles, public participation, safety and security, good hospitality,
access to Internet learning online, life convenience, technology demand, and
nationalization.
Globalization and regionalization bring both opportunities and threats. Regarding
opportunities, globalization and regionalization allow people from all over the world to
exchange ideas and values, w hich facilitates the circulation of massive amounts of
knowledge and information among them. People now understand each other better
than before, regardless of language, religion or cultural differences, which has
significantly boosted the tourism industry. Globalization has increased global mobility
and enabled the free flow of information, making it easier for travel enthusiasts to
travel around the world and allowing them to discover hidden travel destinations, as
well as learning all about different cul tures before making travel decisions. In contrast,
the rise of globalization and regionalization have also produced some negative effects,
such as terrorism and the erosion of cultures. Globalization allows everything,
including weapons and criminals, to move freely between different countries, and
terror groups can now send their terror messages much more easily and faster. The
richness of a given culture determines whether visitors will be attracted to it. However,
with globalization and regionalization s weeping across countries and continents, many
people are losing their cultural identity and adapting to foreign traditions.

3.7.2 Technological Environment
Technology itself can influence the adoption of new technologies in Cambodia. The
reasons include new technologies, research and development, patent, intellectual
properties, copyrights, innovation and creativity, media, branding, more convenience,
advanced theology, and promotion. Research and development, for instance, requires
the use of technology.

3.7.3 Economic Environment
The economy is another area that requires the adoption of new technology. Influential
factors include global and regional integration, high ROI in technology businesses, high
labour costs, public -private partnerships, privatization, competition, stable growth,
green companies, purchasing power, indigenous reliance on tourism, and safe foods.
The economic factor also plays an important role in improving technology
transformation and adoption within the tourism industry. It is undeniable th at both
global and local economic circumstances can significantly affect the opportunity to
improve technology adoption. The integration of Cambodia's economy with other
countries, whether globally or regionally, in terms of economic cooperation could
provide opportunities for tourism businesses and organizations to adopt new
technologies that offer them a high return on investment. Other factors that could also
increase technology adoption include the rise of public -private partnerships and
privatization.

3.7.4 Environment
Environmental changes could lead to the adoption of new technologies to deal with
such change. Environmental factors include climate change, disaster, natural resource
depletion/abundance, geological benefits, eco -tourism/community-based tourism,
good temperatures, biodiversity, pollution, forecasting, irrigation systems, and
environmental protection. Advanced technology can help us to predict and manage
more effectively climate change, environmental change, natural resources, and

Politics ha ve been reported to influence the adoption of new technologies. Such

“right” political stand.
A number of different external factors are known to influence digital transformation
and adoption in tourism development. Among such factors, political factors might b e
the most important, even though different countries have different development
contexts. Based on the results of a discussion among experts in Cambodia, the major
political drivers supporting and constraining technological transformation and
adoption were found to include the government’s support policy and the legal
framework. This means that the transformation/adoption of technology requires the
government to develop policies that will provide a guide and create an environment
that is conducive to the a doption of the tourism business ecosystem. One of the key
drivers is political stability, as this would lead to the stable development of the country
as well as the tourism industry. Additionally, it is necessary to provide tourism tax

tourism industry. However, certain political factors might obstruct the adoption of

3.15 Conclusion and Recommendation
Technologies play essential roles in society, economy, politics, environment, and
technologies. Furthermore, certain technologies became even more important during
the Covid -19 pandemic. Tourism is regarded as a sector that is quick to adopt new
technology, and certain technologies have contributed to the management and
recovery of the tourism industry during and after t he Covid-19 crisis. Meanwhile, the
Royal Government of Cambodia (RGC) is paying a lot of attention to (the role of?)
technology in the development of the country. The RGC has initiated various
technology policies and roadmaps for 2030 in different sectors. As a crucial sector in
Cambodia’s economic development, the RGC has paid particular attention to tourism,
and encouraged the development of the “Tourism Technology Roadmap 2030”.
The visio n of the Tourism Technology Roadmap 2030 is to transform Cambodia i nto
one of the top tourism destinations in the region through the adoption of innovative
technologies and to develop a competitive, sustainable, and i nclusive tourism industry
by 2030. As such, it has the five following five goals: to improve the technology literacy
and skills of tourism operators and users; to strengthen support for the country’s
technology infrastructure; to improve cyber security; to enforce the utilization of
advanced technologies to improve the operation of the tourism business in Camb odia;
and to promote the adoption of technologies that will transform the tourism industry.
A series of drivers ha s been identified and examined in te rms of society, economy,
politics, environment, and technology, and on the basis of these drivers, prioritized
tourism products and services and key technologies have been identified.
The roadmap prioritizes the following twelve tourism products and services: virtual
tour services, integrated transportation services, green tourism products, digital
transaction services, digital communication, tourism security and safety services,
automated and contactless services, digital marketing services, research and
innovation, and destination management. However, virtual tour services are regarded
as having the lowest priority, while digital transaction services and digital marketing
services are the most prioritized ones. Thus, digital transaction and marketing services

are highly prioritized and strongly recommended as the focus of tourism development.
Thirty key techn ologies have been identified to support the development of the
prioritized tourism products and services. The key technologies are rated based on
their importance and the feasibility of each product and service, and are then
identified as ‘critical’, ‘less important’, and ‘strategic decision needed’. These
technologies are classified into such areas as platforms, apps, devices, networks,
biometric security and image recognition, digital map, artificial intelligence, sensing,
machine learning, audio and visu al, and renewable energy. Since the roadmap is
divided into three periods: short -term, medium -term, and long -term, the
products/services and technologies are prioritized according to those periods.
Supporting infrastructure should be provided to support the implementation of this
roadmap. The infrastructures include human resources, digital payment infrastructure,
security infrastructure, collaboration among stakeholders, research and innova tion
infrastructure, and institutional management and monitoring.

service
A combination of different transportation modes designed to
maximize travel effectiveness and efficiency by improving
comfort and convenience, reducing cost and time, and
improving access and safety for travelers (Rodrigue, 2020).

Tourism development that fully takes into consideration present
and future economic, social, and environmental impacts while
simultaneously accommodating the needs of the tourism
industry, visitors, and local communities (UNWTO, 2022).
Green tourism product
Various tourism related activities that are environmentally-
friendly or any environmentally-friendly tourism service or
product offered to visitors (Furqan, Som, & Hussin, 2010).
Smart experience Use of technology to facilitate the (active) experiences of
tourists (Buhalis & Amaranggana , 2015).
Digital transaction service An action or series of actions aimed at creating, issuing, and
circulating digital financial assets (Popkova & Sergi, 2019).
Digital communication
Interchangeable use of “Digital communication” services,
including the use of any type of digital communication or
electronic communication, such as email, SMS text messages or
other types of electronic or digital communication (Cox &
Perkins, 2008; Anil, 2018).

service
