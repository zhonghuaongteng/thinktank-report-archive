Abstract
This report provides an ecosystem analysis of cultural and creative industry (CCI) sector with
regard to the adoption of new technologies and as case studies additive manufacturing and
advanced materials as these two technologies are becoming consolidated in prototyping and
manufacturing in many industrial sectors, but are still in their infancy in the CCI. The
introduction of these two technologies, however, is the base of emerging new business
models as showcased by a number of promising startups. We identified 430 CCI-startups in
Europe that operate innovative products or services and that were further analysed via an
online survey to which 83 companies participated. In addition, we discuss the role innovation
ecosystems for the CCI development in Europe taking four ecosystems as example, namely
the ‘Finnish Additive Manufacturing Ecosystem’ in Helsinki, the ‘Design Farm’ in Berlin, the
‘Sustainable and Tech Fashion’ hub in Amsterdam and “Portuguese footwear cluster” in the
northern region of Portugal. Our study shows that green and digital transformations make
additive manufacturing and advanced materials technologies quite worth looking into for CCI
firms. The survey demonstrates a high degree of business dynamism of those companies
that have fully embraced additive manufacturing and advanced materials as their
operational basis. Furthermore we show that an effective ecosystem are key for deploying
novel technologies to the sector, mostly composed by small and very small enterprises.

Executive summary
Policy context. Cultural and Creative Industries (CCI) are an essential part for the continued
development of societies and important part of Europe’s economy. CCIs are knowledge-
intensive and largely based on individual creativity and talent, generating growth and create
jobs. But CCIs do not escape from the techno-societal or circular economy megatrend: they
have to master the green and digital transition, too. In this respect, a number of EU cities and
regions did already start the transition and have been very successful at tapping into the
potential of CCIs to boost their socio-economic development. However, there are still too
many places in Europe, which have not yet been making most of their potential.
Many industrial sectors and value chains will have to go through a very rapid green and
digital transformation in the next years towards a sustainable model of inclusive growth,
embracing an improved environmental footprint along all steps of the value chain. Within
this industrial context, investments in strategic value chains and new forms of collaboration
with industry will be essential for ensuring Europe’s strategic autonomy and technological
leadership. Likewise, in order to support the implementation of European value chains across
EU territories, it is necessary to understand the innovation capacity in particular places, the
dynamics of their actors and whether & how effective working relationships can be
established across Europe.
Technological Considerations. Additive manufacturing (AddManu) is increasing finding its
place in the creative industries as it allows them to move from custom production towards
mass-customisation, to design products with previously unattainable geometry, to reduce the
manufacturing complexity, to improve material efficiency and to simplify the supply-chain.
We present two dozens of startups that show how AddManu is finding its way for interiors,
particularly furniture and lightning and for fashion, the application areas are clothing,
footware, and jewellery. In a survey we carried out we find that AddManu is mostly employed
in design stage, and that conceptual design is the process step expected to benefit most in
the mid-term.
In this study we analyse also CCI startups that employ advanced materials (AdvMat) in their
manufacturing process, such as advanced composites, textiles & fibres, coatings, gels &
foams, high-performance polymers, light alloys or nanomaterials. We exemplify our results
using a dozen of startups for which advanced material constitute the backbone of their
businesses. For many years the introduction of advanced materials was on grounds of
technical performance and costs. More recently, social and environmental motivations
prevail.
Trends. Responsible consumption patterns and sustainability considerations come
increasingly into the fore. In fashion, clients are increasingly demanding sustainable "cradle
to grave" solutions, assuring the recovery and recycling dynamics set from the start of the
design process. Such concepts can go beyond mere recycling, such as using fabrics that can
be easily be dissolved, transformed or re-polymerised into new semi-finished products.
Another aspect influencing the choice of materials is the enhanced sensitivity towards animal
protection and more people practising veganism, calling for new natural fibres and materials.
Examples include (i) vegan textiles made of mushroom skin and mushroom roots, (ii) naturally
dyed sports shoes grown with microbes, which lose more than 70% of their mass one week
of after composting or (iii) wool fibre created through biodegradable fibres.

This trend may become of interest for rural areas. As ‘providers of novel materials’ for CCIs
industries may appear to collect, select, shred and mix significant quantities of post-
consumer materials or agro-industry waste, developing compounds suitable for specific
applications. CCIs in rural areas may then move up the value chain becoming ‘digital artisans’
managing the whole design service, i.e. analysing client’s needs to process fashion
accessories or goods for home living.
Emerging Business Models. Introducing additive manufacturing tools can change the
company’s former design and manufacturing process. Likewise, materials with additional
functionalities or sustainable materials serve to offer new kind of products. From a business
point of view a number of conclusions can be drawn. First, new services and products can
lead to new business opportunities for the CCIs.
Second, AddManu and AdvMat in combination with the CCI’s digitalization will change the
interaction between the stakeholders. For instance, online customer platform can change the
speed and the degree of interaction between the former CCI’s providers and final consumers
(prosumer). While the overall process remains as before, digitalization changes the roles and
activities of the stakeholders, which become more interlinked. In a traditional business a
client would entering a shop to buy a product; let say a designer table. The CCI would design
the table, manufacture it and sell it. In the new setting the client can take active part in the
design of the product (e.g. detailing some characteristics of the table) and adapt is to the
customer’s specific needs (e.g. the dimension of the table). The client becomes a ‘prosumer’.
From a business model point of view, the novelty lies in the separation of design, delivery,
adaptation and production as independent building blocks, which allow for potentially

Ecosystems. Innovative CCI startups seldom appear in isolation, rather they emerge as a
spin-off of previously existing industries or in environments with a high density of creative
talents. Several countries have realized the importance of nurturing the CCI ecosystem.
Examples of geographical hubs that pursuit contribute to strengthening an innovation driven
ecosystem include the DesignFarmBerlin in Berlin (focusing on smart textile wearable
technologies), the Finnish Additive Manufacturing Ecosystem in Vaasa (3D printed furniture
and biocomposites for 3D printing), FAMEST in Madeira (sustainability and circular economy
applied to biomechanics and sensed foodware), Porto Global Hub (a design factory for applied
research and industrial collaboration), ClickNL in Eindhoven (diverse activities for local
creative industries) or ‘REG X’ in Vejle in Southern Denmark, (providing IT services and design
support for user experience and user interaction).
Policy Considerations. CCI ecosystems contribute to economic, societal and environmental
policy challenges. It is claimed that they may contribute fighting against the depopulation of
rural areas, avoiding migration to urban areas. We observe, however, that most innovative
CCI startups are located in traditional innovation hotspots like Berlin or Amsterdam. It is in
these metropolitan areas where an ecosystem exist where talented people and technology
wise can meet and find the infrastructure, capacities and funds to advance promising
projects. In rural areas where the density of talented people is lower, the importance of
organizations promoting measures to tighten networks and support innovation are more
critical. Examples of organization whose aim is to integrate specific local knowledge with the
opportunities deriving from new technologies include FAMEST in Madeira, FAME in Vaasa or
REG X in Vejle. Whether in metropolitan or rural areas, there seems to be need to support
startups with hands-on practical training on technological, and particular digital, support for
design, modelling and manufacturing.
Key conclusion. As whole, CCI industries still have a long way to go to master the green and
digital transition. This study, however, shows that there are hundreds of innovative firms and
startups in Europe that have already mastered this transition. They make use of state-of-
the art technology (digital tools, additive manufacturing, use of advanced materials, etc.) in
their respective business process steps that allow them to master societal and environmental
demands (sustainable production, animal-free materials, recycled materials, etc.), while
staying economically robust. The support of a functioning local, placed-based innovation
ecosystem is key to promote and help companies which are still in the transition phase. The
case studies presented in this study show that such ecosystems can emerge in different
geographical environments taking into account the specificities and needs of the regions.

1 Introduction
Many industrial sectors and value chains will have to undergo a very rapid green and digital
transformation in the coming years towards a sustainable model of inclusive growth,
embracing an improved environmental footprint along all steps of the value chains. Within
this industrial context, investments in strategic value chains and new forms of collaboration
with industry will be essential for ensuring Europe’s strategic autonomy and technological
leadership. Likewise, in order to support the implementation of European value chains across
EU territories, it is necessary to understand the innovation capacity in particular places, the
dynamics of their actors and whether & how variable geometry coalitions across Europe can
be established.  Cultural and Creative Industries (CCI) are an essential part for the continued
development of societies and important part of Europe’s economy. CCIs are knowledge-
intensive and largely based on individual creativity and talent, generating growth and create
jobs. But neither CCIs do escape from the techno-societal or circular economy megatrend:
they too have to master the green and digital transition. In this respect, a number of EU cities
and regions did already start the transition and have been very successful at tapping into the
potential of CCIs to boost their socio-economic development. However, there are still too
many places in Europe, which have not yet been making most of their potential.
The aim of this report is to analyse a number of place-based innovation ecosystems in
promising segments of the Creative Industry. The report reviews specifically the impact of
additive manufacturing (AddManu) and advanced materials (AdvMat) on the design sector,
including trends, novel business models, and interactions between stakeholders. We start by
mapping innovative CCI startups and analyse the industry dynamics. Thanking the industrial
landscape into account we select meaningful case studies to shed light into the dynamics
from an innovation ecosystem point of view. Our study aims at providing new insights in the
functioning of existing ecosystems in CCI and presents good practices in terms of technology
integration, network dynamics, governance, or internationalization.

2 Novel Technologies for Design
This section introduces additive manufacturing (AddManu) and on advanced materials
(AdvMat) in the creative industries and specific aspects such as the classification of
technologies / materials and a scouting of recent specific applications.
It describes the productive dynamics and the impact of technologies on manufacturing
industries, identifying possible interactions with services with the objective to understand, to
anticipate and to address the impact of new technologies on design and production processes
and hereby to contribute to smooth transformation the role of designers and creatives in
general. The three components of the study are:
1. The level of knowledge and maturity and scope of applicability of AddManu
technologies and advanced materials, in terms of specific subsectors in Europe;
2. The (potential) qualitative impact on the production process, including the impact on
value chains, business models, productivity and products / outputs;
3. the (potential) creative impact on projects, in terms of complexity, personalization,
sustainability, types and levels of skills as well as physical places of production.
The first part introduces definitions, concepts, trajectories and evolution of the role of
AddManu in the creative industries, motivating how the increasing automation is gradually
shifting the focus from the process to the product, with an important empowerment of the
creative professions (both in terms of planning and management of extended and
multidisciplinary teams). The current trends in the Furniture, Lighting and Interiors sectors
(i.e. the introduction of AddManu in the products that populate homes and improve daily life)
and Textile-Clothing-Footwear, Jewellery, Fashion Accessories (or the introduction of
AddManu in wearable products, generically attributable to the fashion sector).
The second part introduces scientific and functional definitions and trajectories of some
classes of materials, classifiable precisely as advanced, with references to present and
prospective applications in the sectors presided over by the creative industries concepts,
trajectories and evolution of the role of AddManu in the creative industries. The time horizon
is five years, that is, it collects publications and productions presented starting from 2017
and with potential previews until 2022.

2.1 Additive Manufacturing
Additive manufacturing (AddManu) processes are considered in many applications as a new
industrial revolution. Technologies and processes currently used in CCI are manifold and
diverse; and they are used in different combinations rendering a complex set of new tools
for CCI. Over recent years, different terminologies have risen to encompass these
technologies, such as “rapid prototyping”, “rapid tooling”, “3D printing”, and “freeform
fabrication”. While all of these are commonly accepted, “additive manufacturing” is probably
the term that captures best its essence and is the term that we will use in this study.

Box: Additive Manufacturing

9
In additive manufacturing (3D-printing), a three-dimensional object is constructed from a
CAD model or a digital 3D model. A variety of processes can be used to deposite, to join or
solidify material under computer control to create a three-dimensional object. The materials
are typically added layer-by-layer and different types of materials can be used, including
plastics, liquids or powder grains being, which are fused together
Additive manufacturing changes the way products of services are made and delivered and
what this means for creative sectors. The introduction of AddManu in creative industries will
impact value chains, business models and specific activities such as product design and
prototyping. These changes require people ready to work with these technologies and that
are open to changes in production processes. AddManu in the creative industries, although
not new at all, is still considered an emerging technology because it is still not widely diffused
and has not yet reached a maturity level across the sector.
While not yet consolidated, we observe a fast adoption of AddManu technologies is due to a
miniaturization of machines, to an ever greater computing capacity, to an important
expansion of the range of materials used and to the reduction of cost. In some cases this
acceleration is propelled by the expiration of the first registered patents. In essence, AddManu
is a new way of turning data into physical things, which enables creative industries to.
 To move from custom production towards mass-customisation;
 To design products with previously unattainable geometry;
 To reduce the manufacturing complexity;
 To gain material efficiency; and
 To simplify the supply-chain.
The main advantages of these technologies are the high precision, the possibility of using
different materials (available for metals, ceramics, plastics and biomaterials), and the ability
to obtain impossible prototypes using conventional means. AddManu is applied mostly in
creative industries where the following parameters influence decisions about technologies
and production processes:
 High added value of customisation;
 Small batch size;
 Design complexity;
 Importance of low weight;
 Drastic assembly reduction.

Therefore, AddManu is increasingly being applied in the creative industries. In addition, there
are broader, ‘horizontal’ applications of AddManu to produce components for prototypes and
spare parts. Especially for these applications, the technological developments and the cost
effectiveness and price of AddManu (mostly for metal) are important drivers. Other drivers
(or enablers) are the extent to which AddManu can deliver on parameters such as reducing
weight and simplifying assembly lines. Current barriers include technological immaturity
(depending on the material used), standardisation and interoperability issues, a lack of

10
certification,4 and to some extent IP concerns. Other limitations in design processes include
the high cost of the processes, the time required to obtain the prototypes, and potentially a
lower degree of resistance. Active work is under way to improve these limitations in order for
additive manufacturing to be competitive with regard to other more conventional means.
According to a recent an online survey of product and industrial design practitioners,5 the
typical size of components produced in series in AddManu varies from 10 x 10 x 7 mm to
400 x 500 x 280 mm. The average size of 138.7 x 111.8 x 61.2 mm is suitable for fashion,
but too small home living applications. For end-user components, the typical volume to
produce is in the order of 11 to 1.000 pieces. Generally speaking, small scale production
suffer from high fixed costs and weak economies of scale.  Cost reduction is particular
essential in such cases and AddManu contributes to his objective. The extent to which
AddManu reduces cost with traditional manufacturing depends of the product and the sector.
In all cases, however, AddManu offers an unprecented alternative that suits both niche
production as well as high-end markets with low volume.
Polyamide and ABS seems to be the materials frequently most used. The main reasons for
using AddManu were low volume, complex shape, speed, cost, shape manufacturability and
customisation. The main limitations of using AddManu for series production were cost, post
processing, productivity, materials, build platform and accuracy. Cost is the main perceived
reason for not employing AddManu in production. Interestingly, many designers have never
considered AddManu-based design nor AddManu production.
The more the AddManu process becomes further user-friendly and require less expert
knowledge for its handling, the more AddManu enables to shift towards design and creativity.

To which extent creative industries exploit DfAM methods (Design for Additive Manufacturing)
to generate creative concepts, solely, or whether is also employed in developing new projects
is an interesting aspect, which can be assessed by its impact on the product definition at
different levels:6
Level 1: Formal newness – Here, DFAM methods are oriented to the redesign of existing
products. Designers start the process knowing most of the product’s data: they know their
inner and outer functions, 7 how are its inner and outer forms and the product behaviour. The
purpose is to redesign in order to render the product suitable and optimized for AddManu.
Level 2: Functional reconfiguration - Methods of this category are dedicated to redesign
existing products that embody assemblies, for instance producing several components in one
go,8 or redesign multiple components into one. The purpose is to reduce the number of
components of existing assemblies or of existing whole products.

flexible tools to to creatively deposit specific materials, it empowers people with printer
control skills to develop creative businesses or even general purpose 'creative factories'
beyond professional designers
These changes in the production process imply that, increasingly, the tasks and occupations
of designers, material suppliers, IT specialists will be intertwined and that an efficient and
dynamic collaboration promoted by multidisciplinary teams will therefore be necessary
(examples in chapter 2 demonstrate that this enables new business models such as ‘bridge
manufacturing’). The more the "production" process gradually becomes safe and controllable,
the more there will be a transition from process responsibility to product responsibility. This
is an indicator of how the creative process is crucial and how the creatives themselves:
people skilled in traditional abilities (sketch, drawaing, project culture, etc.) -but ‘IT illiterate-
are called upon to digitize their skills to make the most of the potential of AddManu.
In terms of the process, further considerations need to be made. Assuming that production
activities do not require direct operator intervention and that AddManu tech allows for
contributes to manufacture more complex projects in a single run (see the potential offered
by multi-material printing), the challenge remains that part of the success of creative
products comes from the surface finishing of consumer products, which cannot yet be
automatised. In this sense, it is worth monitoring how additive manufacturing will evolve and
how the work teams will handle the manual post-processing of the components, using
traditional processes or contributing to the introduction of new ones.
In the following chapters, we present aspects connected to the methods and places of supply
of services for the creative industries. At the moment, the relative complexity of domestic
printing and the big dimensions of some products, such as furnishings, do not suggest an
immediate future for the “click to materialize”. Local production centres and producers'
spaces, where local specialists and inventors meet, are a more realistic conception of the
‘democratization’ of production and of the transformation of creative industries. And this
could be a revolution within the revolution.

2.1.1 Furniture, Lightning and Interiors
By now, AddManu technologies are widespread and tested in the creative industries. However,
whether its use is consolidated in the design and prototyping activities, is unexplored.
Similarly, whether the transformation of some professional firms or SMEs occur rather in
digital factories or in small centres of production of consumer goods is equally of great
interest. Designers and manufacturers use 3D printing technologies to increase complexity,
map out unique or unexplored visual identities, and offer customizable furniture. Within the
following paragraph some experiences have been collected, providing information relating to
furniture, lighting and interior design products, technologies and 3D printers, with some
references to the hybridization between design / sustainability / approaches to market.

or interiors
Drawn Furniture - Drawn is a French company that has developed its XXL 3D printer called
Galatéa to create custom furniture and decoration. After a successful Kickstarter campaign

2.2 Advanced Materials for Creative Industries
Generally speaking, advanced materials refer to all those materials that represent
advancements over ‘traditional’ materials. They may comprise new materials and
modifications to existing materials that achieve superior performance in one or more
characteristics critical for the application of interest in the creative industries. Superior
performance does not only refer to technical or economic characteristics, but can capture

16
also other broader concepts such as sustainability. In fact, a deeper and more focused
approach to advanced materials is to consider materials that are early in their product and /
or technology life cycle. In other words, there is ample room for growth in terms of improving
the performance characteristics (technology life cycle) and their sales volume (product life
cycle). This selection is based on the latter definition and on the innovative capacity of the
families of materials identified for the creative industries.
2.2.1 Advanced materials and its use in the creative
industries
2.2.1.1 Active Materials
Active materials, also called ‘smart’, multi-functional or adaptive materials, are capable of
modifying in a reversible and controllable manner any one of their particular properties
whenever external physical or chemical stimuli operate on them. These materials have the
capacity to change their colour, shape or viscosity, generate electricity, etc. in response to
changes or alterations in the medium (light, sound, temperature, voltage). A first order
classification of these types of materials include:
- Materials with shape memory;
- Electro active and magneto active materials;
- Phase-change materials;
- Photoactive materials;
- Chromo active materials.

Active materials have inherent or embedded "actuators" that respond to certain stimuli. They
have response control and selection mechanisms, to control the response in a predetermined
way. Their response time is short and the system returns to its original state as soon as the
stimulus ceases. These materials can be used in the creative industries, with reference to the
design of multifunctional products and can also configure intelligent structures and systems
that, with a combination of materials, are able to self-diagnose and modify themselves to
adapt to the conditions that have been set as optimal or correct for them.
CCI Focus – Active Materials are undoubtedly of great interest for the creative industries,
just think of conductive fabrics for fashion, photo-luminescent or shape memory materials
for furniture, sound-absorbing partitions. The design processes will be increasingly linked to
how these materials can meet the possible needs of the population, the designer will
therefore be committed to investigating the "training" process that will contribute to the
exercise of the business.
2.2.1.2 Advanced composites
Composites are a broad family of materials. From a design point of view, it is worth regarding
them by its structure, manufacture and behaviour, which render them different from
monolithic or conventional materials. On the basis of these concepts, we can regard
composites by one of the following features:

17
- It is manufactured artificially (thus excluding any natural materials such as wood), mixing
the components in such a way that the dispersion of one material into another may be
undertaken in a controlled manner to attain an optimal set of properties;
- It has two or more physically and/or chemically different phases or constituent parts,
which are non-inter soluble and appropriately arranged and separated by a defined inter-
phase.
- its properties are uniquely superior in one specific aspect and cannot be attained by its
constituent components separately.
The development of composite materials is conditioned by several challenges such as:
- Reduction of manufacturing costs;
- Adaptation of automated manufacturing technologies to other industries;
- High standards of inspection and testing;
- The need for complete recyclability.
CCI Focus – Natural fibres composites (as lineo, jute, hemp) mixed with conventional
material such as carbon or glass fibre improves significantly the damping properties of the
material while ensuring good mechanical properties. Lots of seats and furniture projects are
going to be realized through increasing knowledge of composites potential.

2.2.1.3 Advanced manufacturing;
Manufacturing processing of parts, although assisted by the most advanced controls, is still
basically conventional: chipping, cold- or hot-forming, casting or injection. All of them face
limitations such as the impossibility of curved drilling, collisions of tools with a part of
complex geometry, restrictions in mould release angles to give just some examples. It is a
barrier in the development of high value products with new functionalities.
The emerging of Additive Manufacturing with the advantage of all knowledge developed in
the digital era is allowing to overcome such limitations. Is possible now manufacture through
controlled deposition of material, layer by layer, putting down exclusively where it is needed
to achieve the final sought-after geometry instead of stripping the material (mechanization,
die-cutting, etc.); or shaping with the help of tools and moulds (casting, injection, folding, etc.).
The manufacturing processes of parts can be classified in the following way:
- Shaping technologies, which use pre-shapes to obtain the required geometry such as
plastic and metal injection, PIM, sintering, vacuum casting, RIM, electroforming, etc.
- Subtractive technologies, which obtain the required geometry by subtracting material
from a larger geometry such as mechanizing, electro-erosion, waterjet cutting, laser
cutting, etc.
- Additive technologies (AddManu) which obtain the geometry by adding material through
virtual geometry, without the use of pre-shapes and without subtracting material
A feature that distinguishes AddManu from other small-scale industrial manufacturing
process is the easiness to produce complex geometric objects. This favours targeted product
customization, as cost for the adapted process increases only slightly while results in unique

18
products that sell at higher prices.  In addition to the cost advantages, the benefits lies in the
design of new or differential products.

CCI Focus – Is possible to think advanced manufacturing in every field of creative industries
as described in the previous paragraphs. Multi-material printing, printing on fabrics and
reinforced/recycled/bio filaments are the most promising evolutions for home living and
fashion.
2.2.1.4 Advanced textiles and fibres
In recent years, technical fabrics have undergone major development and offer many
possibilities for innovation to create high-value products by offering new applications.
Until recently ‘sandwich’ structures were achieved by bonding polyurethane foam (PUR) to a
variety of fabrics for the external layers. The development of 3D fabrics means that obtaining
these sandwich fabrics is considerably simplified.
Currently, non-woven fabrics made by a mechanical punching technique or by means of
needles are the most widely manufactured ones, as they have found many possibilities in
the replacement of conventional fabrics. However, producing nano-fibres through
electrospinning is gaining importance.
Electrospinning is a technique that allows to obtain fibres from molten or solution polymer
with an average diameter in the range of 50 nm to 5 µm. There are studies in which
electrospinning is used that have achieved composite nanoparticle materials in nanofibres or
coaxial nanofibres. Recently a new method has been developed to facilitate the production
of nanofibres called electrospinning based on polymer solutions or molten polymer.

- they save water by dispensing with excess cleaning;

2.2.1.6 Gels and foams
Aerogels are the lightest solid materials known to man, as most of their structure is hollow.
Their extraordinary porosity gives them a large surface area, which provides them with
unique characteristics among solid materials. Their density ranges between 0.4 g/cm3 and
0.004 g/cm3 (only three times the density of air). This is because of their high porosity: more
than 95% of their volume is occupied by air, giving rise to a high surface area. These
characteristics give them unique properties in a solid material, like extremely low thermal
conductivity and sound velocity and high optical transparency. They are considered to be the
best thermal insulator, capable of withstanding temperatures of -50°C and melting at a
temperature in excess of 1648°C. Recognized as the lightest solid in the world, it has a touch
like foam.
CCI Focus - There are currently three crucial lines in the furniture sector and interior design
solutions: the development of foams from recycled material and/or the recycling of polymer
foams, such as polyurethane and polystyrene. The development of metallic foams made from
aluminium, steel, lead and other metals with remarkable characteristics such as high
stiffness, high resistance to compression and much lower density than non-foamed metal.
The development of ceramic foams with density control. Some of the commonly seen
applications are thermal and acoustic insulation, energy absorption systems, filling of
metallic structures or sandwich panels in lightweight structures and development of ceramic
foams with density control.
2.2.1.7 High-performance polymers
Modification and reinforcement of compostable, degradable and/or conventional polymers
(or a mixture of them) with bio-fibres and/or nano-charges can result in materials with very
advanced properties for innovative applications.
Traditionally, the use of charges with polymers has had the purpose of reducing the product’s
cost and of improving its physical-chemical properties. Charges are normally small particles,
short fibres, organic or inorganic materials. The main advantages of using organic fibres to
reinforce plastics are: they have low densities, they are non-abrasive, easily recyclable,
biodegradable with a low energy consumption and low cost.
CCI Focus – High performance polymers are used to produce ergonomics and flexible chairs,
advanced shelf solutions, technical parts of sports shoes.

The creative industry is constantly searching for light, strong materials that can be used in a

2.2.1.9 Nanotechnology
Nanotechnology refers to a comprehensive field of applied science and technology whose
unifying theme is the control of matter at molecular level, which is smaller than a micrometer,
normally on scales of 1 to 100 nanometers. It includes the manufacture of devices on a
nano-scale. It is a highly multidisciplinary field seen across applied physics, material science,
colloidal science, supra-molecular chemistry and electromechanical engineering.
Nanotechnology can be considered as an extension of sciences on a nano-scale. Two main
approaches are used in nanotechnology: top-down and bottom-up. One of them is bottom-
up, where materials and devices are built from molecular components, mostly in a self-
constructing matter. In the top-down approach nano-structures are built by using techniques
like photolithography or nano-imprinting.
Carbon nanotube was the pioneering material in this technology and today graphene is the
most researched for applications. Carbon nanotubes are based on cylindrical nanostructures
made from carbon atoms. It is renowned for its unusual resistance and capacity to conduct
heat and electricity. Graphene, in turn, is transparent, flexible, extraordinarily resistant,
impermeable, abundant and conducts electricity better than any other known metal. This
material permits manufacturing of electronic devices with flexible and transparent screens
and ultra-rapid batteries, powerful solar panels, applications in aeronautics, medicine etc.
Nanotechnology is an excellent base for creating new materials, according to specific needs.
It is also a source of inspiration for other two-dimensional materials such as fluorographene,
a two-dimensional analogue of TeflonTM with extraordinary lubricating and insulating
properties, hexagonal boron nitride, a very hard crystalline and transparent insulator that
combined with graphene improves its electromechanical properties, molybdenum disulfide,
another two-dimensional crystal with promising properties for the construction of a new class
of transistors or silicene, a version of graphene made from silicon that can be easily
integrated with current silicon-based electronics.

CCI Focus – Nanotechnologies are going to be used more and more in textile industry,
fashion products and advanced surfaces or covering solutions for home living.

3 Emerging Trends affecting CCI
3.1 Sustainability
Consumption patterns have evolved over the past decade in Europe and so has also the
European creative industries sector. In particular fashion is experiencing a period of changes,
one of the trends being a demand by clients for more sustainable production and
consumption models. At the same time, the spread of technological startups strongly focused
on the radical transformation of the supply chain and international logistics, as well as on
the use of materials with low environmental impact, is accelerating the introduction and sale
on the market of compatible solutions from the environmental point of view.
In the fashion industry, the biggest problem lies in the volume of unwanted clothing that
ends up in landfills. According to the Ellen MacArthur Foundation [AA.VV, 2017]9, which works
to promote sustainability, global apparel production nearly doubled from 2000 to 2015. Over
the same period, the number of times a garment was worn fell by 36%. All in all, "the
equivalent of a garbage truck full of clothes is burned or dumped in a landfill every second,"
their report noted. Around the same time, according to the World Economic Forum10, 60 %
more garments were bought, but consumers only kept them half the time.
Sustainability can be analysed within the five broad phases of the life cycle of a product (pre-
production, production, distribution, use and disposal), leaving individual responsibilities to
producers or consumers, or evaluating, as always more often it happens, collaborative
approaches in the management of the end of life.

End of Life
Some companies are trying to increase their sustainability by encouraging consumers to
activate virtuous recovery circuits. There are various strategies to raise awareness on the
value of the garment or furniture. In some stores, for example, customers may pay a nominal
amount to transform unwanted clothing into new ones through a process that breaks down
old fibres and combines them with new ones. Other companies seeking to provide "cradle to
grave" solutions, an expression used for processes that intend to keep materials within a
circular economy by having set the recovery and recycling dynamics right from the start of
the design process. Therefore, digital and non-digitized services are being tested to actively
collect products, also collaborating with important players in the shipping sector. The main
objective is to structure sustainable recovery and recycling models, often ‘bypassing’
providers of environmental services or recyclers of urban and industrial waste.

3.2 Product and Service Differentiation
The CCIs, considering their nature and their main vocation, that is to elaborate and direct
creativity towards recognizable and attractive products and services, are the ones most
involved in differentiation processes, a fairly extensive concept that can be analysed from
different points of view. Product differentiation, at least in general terms, is what
distinguishes the product or service from a market characterized by a comparable offer. For
this reason it can also be understood as the perception that a potential recipient has of the
product, aimed at undertaking cognitive, exploratory, purchase and loyalty actions.
Digitalization and new technologies have allowed creative industries to explode some
possibilities and to inaugurate differentiation strategies based on specific factors, such as
the level of personalization and the interaction experience for the achievement of goods and

24
services with unique characteristics or performances or compatible with purely subjective
qualitative elements.
We therefore moved from a focus on the aspects of style, production, quality and sales, to a
focus based on how the potential customer would imagine a good with respect to specific
needs of natural anthropometric, aesthetic and functional, therefore, with respect to needs
related to the target person or environment. The simplification and optimization of the
personalization experience are now considered the key to understanding and the main
objective to ensure that the technical potential is translated into an object with the desired
characteristics. And if by simplification we mean the speed with which a customization
process can be concluded with satisfaction, by optimization we can multiply the field by
including tools that allow an evaluation of the asset beyond its intrinsic characteristics. In the
fashion sector, for example, I can customize a colour based on personal taste. But if
technology allows me to simulate the colour of my skin and evaluate a garment worn also
in relation to the performance on the skin, the experience can be considered extended or
increased. The same goes for a table that can be customized in terms of wood surfaces and
grain, to which I associate a possibility of contextualization in a real, virtual or mixed
environment (real + virtual). This type of approach tends to lead the customer to recognize a
real approach of the CCI towards his needs, with a consequent greater willingness to purchase
products that offer a unique and useful experience.
It is important to differentiate products in any industry, but especially if you are in a crowded
market with many competitors as creative sector. The goal is to show potential customers
what company can offer that other businesses can't and why it's valuable to them.

3.2.1 Types of Classification and their Competitive
Advantage
Product or service differentiation is an asset as a competitive advantage. The creative
industries, today, are measured mainly with three types of competitive advantage: benefits,
approach to the recipient and competition.
Benefits are the values a customer receives when purchasing a product or service. They are
different from the aesthetic characteristics or functionality, which are the characteristics
attributable to the primary function of the asset. Creative companies are focusing more and
more on the benefits related to the purchase, expanding the concept of value from the quality
/ price ratio to the ability to establish benefits extended beyond the purchase or in some
cases in the medium and long term.
The recipients of the product, once classified almost exclusively as customers, today
represent a pool of subjects through which to sell, but also to develop or improve the product
itself. The creative industries therefore fuel a gradual transformation of the passive to active
approach, where the differentiated advantages of the product are aligned with the interests,
needs and values of a defined target audience. Differentiation has widened the field of desire
from "why he wants it and how much he will pay" to "how he wants it to appear and where
he wants to buy it".
Finally, competition helps to understand what the margins of differentiation are and which
strategies have already been probed and applied. The value of the creative product lies more

25
and more in the perception of how the advantages and specific needs of the potential buyer
are distinguishable or alternative to the solutions populated by the competition.
In this study we distinguish five types of differentiation, three of which are traditional and
two that we can call “advanced”.
- Vertical differentiation: occurs when customers choose a product by ranking their
options from best to worst using an objective measurement, such as price or quality.
- Horizontal differentiation: occurs when customers choose a product based on
subjective preferences, because they do not have an objective measurement to
distinguish between the best or the worst;
- Mixed Differentiation: Customers who make more complex purchases tend to use a
mix of vertical and horizontal differentiation when making purchasing decisions. Many
creative products, especially with the advent of the IoT, select vertically for functionality
and horizontally for aesthetics;
- Extra-combined differentiation: it is a type of differentiation of a mixed type, but
guided by extra criteria concerning the customer's ability to marry the specific purposes
of the producer / service manager, such as sustainability, ethical production, support of
disadvantaged categories, solutions to problems of a global and non-particular nature;
- Selective differentiation: customers are grouped numerically, the differentiation field
is strongly pushed and therefore focused on lead users or experts. It is based on how the
product is sold and managed over time, on the quality of the service, on the duration, on
the adaptation or upgrade of functionality.

3.3 CCIs as a revived opportunity for the development of rural
areas
The emerging trends analysed in the previous chapters, especially as regards additive
manufacturing and the production of advanced materials in geographic areas characterized
by the high presence of secondary raw materials and waste from the agro-industry or the
extractive industry, open up scenarios of great interest in the creation and strengthening of
creative industries in the peripheries of Europe, or in contexts circumscribed at regional level
characterized by a low population, few companies and large distance to large urban centres
or industrial areas.
A necessary premise concerns the direct and indirect needs or benefits that could arise from
this scenario. Creative workers have the flexibility to work from anywhere as long as facilities
are available, especially a quality broadband connection. Preserving the quality of life,
landscape, built environment and natural heritage of a rural region appears to be key to
maintaining its attractiveness to people and creative businesses. Opportunities for social,
cultural and commercial interaction between creative professionals can also play an
important role in rural regions where isolation can be a problem and where there is a risk
that creative talent may be lost in favour of larger centres. Another aspect concerns
connectivity, both physical and virtual, which is fundamental for the functioning of creative
businesses in rural areas. Currently, many of the creative sectors at the regional level mainly
serve local or national markets; it is likely that the future growth of the sector will require an
expansion of the export business, with greater connectivity key for this. There is a possibility,
however, for tensions to arise if greater connectivity in rural regions impacts their 'creative
place' attributes. Finally, there is an ever greater reduction in public resources to support the
sector, with particular reference to the artistic departments of local authorities, investment
funds for cultural infrastructures and other sources of financing for the creative sector.
The importance of environmental factors and quality of life is therefore fundamental to
retain and attract creative talent in a rural region, as well as overcoming some constraints
of a rural location to the functioning of creative enterprises, particularly in the infrastructure
sectors, accessibility and networking. Digital creative factories could be the appropriate and
evolved answer, both in the form of small digital workshops and hubs of experts in the use
of ICT technologies for the creative industries. Digital creative factories are, to all intents and
purposes, small-scale factories equipped with laboratory or industrial-grade equipment,
electronic tools and software used for the production of prototypes or small series. The digital
creative factories arouse great interest at the local level, just imagine for example the islands
or the extreme provinces of the continent, since they can concentrate resources and experts
towards creative purposes, or towards the development of new manufacturing and tourism
products, towards repair and maintenance of goods produced with industrial production

technologies, towards solutions that in some cases help small towns to express their own
production culture that is increasingly distinguishable.
Despite technological and infrastructural advances, several regions of Europe suffer from
the low concentration of companies, the distance from supply chains, the increasing
comparison with labor costs in non-EU countries. Creative businesses operating locally,
therefore, express strong values in terms of craftsmanship, but do not have tools to intercept
interests from richer areas or to aggregate skills in order to integrate specific know-how with
the use of local materials in a digital key, or, above all, on increasingly pushed and customized
design elaborations.
In the business models that we will analyse later, two main profiles emerge that could
contribute in an important way to the development of rural areas:

- Providers of novel materials for CCIs: manufacturers of filaments, spools, profiles,

and CNC machines. These micro-productions would be of interest if associated with the
ability to collect, select, shred and mix interesting quantities of post-consumer materials
or agro-industry waste, developing compounds suitable for specific applications;
- Digital Artisans able to manage the whole design service based on analyse, process
and transform product files into fashion accessories or goods for home living.

These profiles would contribute to more ample societal challenges, such as:

- Integrating specific local knowledge with the opportunities deriving from new

- Recovering of waste and scraps as new resources, activation of circular economy

- Creating of training centres (design, modelling, manufacturing) and assistance for on-

4.3.1 New business models
From the analysis of the CCIs surveyed and their degree of digitalization it can be seen that
traditional business models and advanced models still exist, the latter strongly influenced by
the Digital Economy and the centrality of the customer in the products and services offered.
The development of new business ideas that use digital technologies are mainly customer-
oriented and the proposal of new and disruptive business models tends to favour the User-
Intensive development approach, which orients the functionalities of the proposed product or
service based on the analysis of needs and interaction with users, rather than an exclusively
Technology-Push approach, which instead puts the proposed technology at the centre of
development. It should be emphasized that, as a consequence of the Digital Transformation
of society, it becomes increasingly important for creative companies to interact with their

34
reference Community, or in general with the target market, even during the product
development phase and before the marketing phase, anticipating the phase of
communication (marketing) with the market.
The spread of digital infrastructures and the emergence of increasingly complex but simple
to use digital technologies have together determined the evolution of the business models
proposed by creative companies. To rank some of the most interesting models, we considered
the elementary framework of Baden-Fuller (2016) as the preferred approach for this study.
The four models can be used to identify, classify and analyse business models in this
research. The four elementary models are:
- Product model;
- Solutions model;
- Matchmaking model;
- Multi-sided model.

The product model involves the sale or licensing of goods and services; as describer before
in this paper, it is the most common model found in the CCIs: “The company develops a
product or standardised service and sells it to customers. The value proposition is
transactional: to provide a product or standardised service that customers will buy;
Solutions models are more tailored, and are driven by the firm’s response to consumer needs,
such as a consulting firm: “The company engages with a customer about a problem the
customer faces, and provides an integrated solution. The value proposition is relational: to
tailor solutions to each customer;
Matchmaking involves the firm matching buyers and sellers within its marketplace; the
creative company joins customers and sellers in its online or physical marketplace. The value
proposition is transactional: to facilitate exchange.
Multi-sided involves the creative offering products or services to different consumers, and
these consumers derive additional value from each other. The company provides different
products or services to different customer, designer or manufacturer groups. The value
proposition is multi-sided: one customer group gets additional benefits from the other group’s
transactions.”
We can classify product and solutions models as dyadic, indicating that they are dominated
by relationships between two parties (company-customer.) However, the matchmaking and
multi-sided model are instead triadic or more, incorporating both the firm’s relationship with
consumers, and the relationship between those customers. In triadic models we can observe
competition and cooperation phenomena that generate further external relationship among
the actors.

4.3.2 Consumer as producers, as integrator of resources
and as remixer

Consumers are increasingly theorised as producers on their own or through participation with
firms and/or other consumers especially in online communities. From a theoretical
perspective, consumer productivity is evident in many strategies and visionary trajectories of
creative industries. On this basis is important to develop a better understanding of
‘prosumption and the emerging role of the prosumer.
Consumers actively involved in the practice of consumption become prosumers for a variety
of reasons, not least the creative attitude or the need to modify / improve products that are
part of their daily life. This means that, by becoming a prosumer, one is asked to be more
involved in consumer experiences rather than relying on commercial suppliers or becoming a
producer who establishes co-creation dynamics. New technologies, therefore, in addition to
bringing machines into homes, are exercising practices that concern, more than mere
collaboration, consumer empowerment as prosumers acquire more self-dependence (or self-
sufficiency) for products or services that otherwise they would acquire as such on the market.
A not secondary aspect is also the reduction of susceptibility with respect to the exploitation
imposed by the supply chain, since the prosumers relate to the dependence on secondary
intermediation (for example agents or retailers) and commercial offers. What certainly

37
appears interesting in the survey is that Prosumers intervene in the strategies of the creative
industries at different levels and which, gradually, as designers and producers, are
increasingly establishing themselves as integrators of resources. This is with the vision of
manufacturing products as the results of a broad integration of resources such as people,
technologies, raw materials, knowledge and information, which a company bears entirely and
for which often excessive, superfluous and recurring costs are incurred.
If on the one hand, therefore, we imagine the prosumer as engaged in tangible aspects, from
co-design to the self-production of goods and components, it is interesting to imagine the
consumer as a key player in a collaborative path based on the following categories:

- human resources, supplier of knowledge to the creative enterprise;
- organizational resources, provider of data on use, wear, management and reliability;
- information resources, provider of knowledge in terms of market segments, competitors
and technologies;
- relational resources, supplier of relationships with local consumer groups, small distribution.

Finally, it must be considered that new technologies and their use within increasingly
advanced platforms offer unprecedented flexibility in adapting existing product designs to
changing needs. Prosumers, to benefit from this potential, are increasingly equipping
themselves with structured design procedures and tools, while online platforms for creating
collaborative projects are implementing more and more features for remixing, a concept that
describes the creation of new models based on existing design elements. The value of
remixing lies in improving product design processes, whether they are oriented towards
creativity (inspiration, learning) or productivity (speed, improvement). The remix helps to
increase the quantity and diversity of projects, it is a fact that projects created by remixers
are printed and produced much more often by community members, suggesting that the
remix helps ensure compatibility of production similar to improvement continuous process.

4.4.2.1 Sustainable and Tech Fashion in Amsterdam
Over 100 fashion houses are actually situated in Amsterdam and the city is also home to
various major fashion labels’ European headquarters. The fashion industry has a long way to
go before becoming sustainable, but the awareness of sustainability issues is widespread in
the Netherlands. Dutch consumers are generally very conscious. In the last years Amsterdam
is building a growing reputation of being at the forefront of technology and sustainability,
with startups concentrating on 3D digital design, new manufacturing techniques, and
biodesigned textiles, there is a lot of innovation and startups working towards a circular
approach. The main elements that corroborate these considerations derive from the number
of companies and sustainable trajectories, with particular reference to:
- Textile recycling. Some clothing collection companies take responsibility for fabrics donated
to clothing banks. The goal is that each new item of clothing produced contains a percentage
of used fibers.

aesthetics and have their own knitting and innovation lab, atelier, specialists and textile
experts to achieve this. Part of the solution for a sustainable fashion industry is finding ways
to create exciting fabrics in new ways, and using the circular economy to create a design
system that uses waste products rather than virgin ones;
- Marketplace for end-of-seasons fashion. Company that work with the fashion industry to
ensure that every item of clothing is worn, helping to find a buyer for every piece already
produced, and providing the tools brands need to develop lines that they can have confidence

4.4.2.4 The Finnish Additive Manufacturing Ecosystem
The Finnish Additive Manufacturing Ecosystem (FAME) is an innovative industrial ecosystem
that aims to increase the role of 3D printing and bring Finnish players together in the field of
additive manufacturing. Finland has positioned itself as a highly educated, high-tech country
with stable infrastructure, competing in traditional labor-intensive manufacturing industry.
These qualities have supported initiatives to position the country as a forerunner in the field
of AM. By its nature, AM is a high value added production method, which is based on a highly
skilled workforce and a high level of automation. In the future, artificial intelligence will also
play an important role in both design and production, complementing the existing Finnish
experience. Artificial intelligence applications already exist that support quality assurance in
the AM process and Finnish research facilities are contributing to this development. All these
attributes support the idea that Finland is one of the main AM hubs in Europe. The seemingly
remote geographic location is not an obstacle due to the high value per kg ratio in the AM
parts, unlike traditional low value added manufacturing where the cost of logistics is
significant.
The goal of FAME is to increase the use of additive manufacturing technologies in the Finnish
machine manufacturing industry and, consequently, to fuel innovation scenarios in highly
strategic sectors such as product design, starting with furniture. The actors of the FAME
ecosystem could, in the coming years, contribute significantly to the European market of
products designed and manufactured in AM and of production systems, also thanks to
elements such as low barriers to collaboration. This ecosystem will be based on the
development and acquisition of new skills, aimed at creating a portfolio of systemic offers
from which the world of creative companies could draw significantly, increase their AM use
rate and share experiences, information, data, physical equipment and facilities.
Innovative firms in this ecosystem include Kalevala; Hernesaari; UPM; Prenta; Materflow.

4.4.2.6 Portugal Footwear Cluster
The footwear industry is particularly relevant in the north of the Portugal, where the majority
of the companies are based. The industry has two major production centers: one in Felgueiras
and Guimarães; and the other one in Santa Maria da Feira, São João da Madeira and Oliveira
de Azeméis. Both hubs are approximately at 50/60 kilometers from Porto. A third, smaller,
center is located at Benedita, at 90 kilometers from Lisbon. Some facts give us an idea of
the size and importance of the two larger footware clusters for Portugal in general, and the
northern region, in particular: the footwear sector accounts for 6,7% of the jobs and 2,8% of
the business volume in Portugal. The figures increase to 12% and 6.8%, respectively for the
northern region. The footwear industry is, therefore, one of the main economic foundations
of the northern Portugal.
The geographical concentration of companies facilitates the relations between firms
specializing in the manufacture of footwear, components and leather goods, creating good
conditions for formal and informal business networks, and it enables firms to benefit from
economies of agglomeration. In addition to the business fabric, some of the sector's main
infrastructures, such as the Portuguese Footwear Technology Center14 and the Design and
Footwear Academy (CFPIC),15 are present in both centers mentioned above.
According to the 2019 Statistical Report by the Portuguese Footwear, Components, Leather
Goods Manufacturers’ Association APICCAPS,16 the Portuguese footwear cluster is formed by
1877 companies, of which 48.3% of which are micro-enterprises and 39.5% are small. Only
11.7% of firms employ between 50 and 249 workers (medium-sized businesses) and 0.6%
more than 250 (nine footwear manufacturing companies, one in components and one in
leather goods). The foregoing data leads to the conclusion that the average size of firms is
very different in the three industries that make up the cluster. A footwear manufacturing
company does in fact employ an average of 26.4 workers, a number that decreases to 20.5
in the components industry and 15.6 in the leather goods industry.
The sector seems to be characterized by a high capacity of flexibility and quick response,
where even small enterprises demonstrate being able to respond to a very selective demand
with a high quality of production. This achievement is possible thanks to the adoption of
advanced technological solutions for their production processes. In terms of innovation
