CHAPTER 1
Introduction
Over the past five years, machine learning–driven protein design has progressed from academic demonstra ‑
tions to point‑and‑click commercial services available to students and hobbyists. AlphaFold’s public release
in 2021 triggered a wave of derivative prediction and generative models (Baek et al., 2021; Dauparas et al.,
2022; Jumper et al., 2021a; Lu et al., 2026). Diffusion‑based backbones, language‑model sequence generators,
and automated in silico screening are now standard components of cloud biofoundries.
While these innovations accelerate legitimate research by shortening the path from concept to therapeutic
lead, they also reduce the barriers to engineering potentially harmful proteins. The same concepts that could
accelerate novel drug discovery also accelerate equally novel toxins, immune‑evasive viral capsids, or cata ‑
lytic domains that can amplify existing threats. In 2022, the World Health Organization and the National
Science Advisory Board for Biosecurity both flagged artificial intelligence (AI)–enabled biological design
(AI
‑bi
o) as an emerging dual‑use concern (National Science Advisory Board for Biosecurity, 2023; World
Health Organization, 2022).
A central biosecurity challenge is verifiable provenance prior to synthesis and shipping of genetic material
(Crawford et al., 2024). Absent an immutable record, investigators can falsely claim that a design is benign,
and reviewers cannot independently reproduce or audit the workflow. Even when required to present logs,
such as notebooks, adversaries can retrospectively doctor records to discard incriminating steps. Laboratory
documentation insufficiently satisfies the evidentiary standards for export‑control determinations and the
Biological Weapons Convention confidence
‑bu
ilding measures. In addition, it can be challenging to verify
customer authenticity if addresses and identities are able to be spoofed.
Therefore, in this paper, we propose and examine the feasibility of a cross‑platform audit‑trail architec ‑
ture that treats every design run as a cryptographically sealed “flight recorder.” By binding each event to a
hash chain, signing the root hash with a hardware‑attested key outside the user’s control, and anchoring the
signature in a public transparency log, regulators gain a tamper‑evident ledger. This ledger can then be veri ‑
fied with commodity open‑source tools, independent of the designer’s honesty or the specific software used.
The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows: Chapter 2 characterizes the verification gap in greater
detail, examining current logging practices, the information that is and is not captured by existing tools, and
the threat model that the protocol is designed to address. Chapters 3 through 5 then describe the protocol
itself—covering its logical design and security properties, trust architecture, deployment configurations, and
cross
‑to
ol generalizability. Chapter 6 turns to the policy implications and the governance, legal, and orga ‑
nizational challenges to be addressed alongside the technical work for the protocol to achieve its intended
purpose.

CHAPTER 2
The Verification Gap in Protein Design
Regulators, scientific journals, and security practitioners require provenance to assess dual‑use risk and
reproducibility (Chhetri et al., 2025). Our conversations with users of AI‑bi
o tools and analysis of commonly
used tools highlighted three pain points: inconsistent logging, poor cross‑to
ol semantics, and user‑co
ntrolled
signing. Current tools produce logs intended for user benefit to iterate on experiments rather than to provide
a comprehensive receipt for third parties. As a result, some tools may produce either proprietary log files or
none at all. Relatedly, the diversity of tools leads to an equal diversity of data that are not easily comparable
across tools. While the input(s) and final output(s) will typically be gene sequence files and protein struc ‑
tures, other metadata, such as scoring or model parameters, may be useful but can vary in their data type or
inclusion. This variety can make it burdensome to parse through to understand the experimental process.
Another overarching challenge is the extent of user control of files. A bad actor can edit any produced file to
conceal evidence of malicious intent.
Information Life Cycle and Trust Assumptions
The information under consideration flows through three stages, each with distinct vulnerability surfaces.
In the design stage , a researcher uses one or more AI
‑bi
o tools on a local workstation or cloud instance to
generate a candidate sequence. The primary risk here is that log entries are suppressed, modified, or gener ‑
ated retroactively by a designer with full control of their own machine. In the submission stage , the result ‑
ing sequence and an accompanying audit bundle are transmitted to a synthesis provider for screening. The
risk here is that the bundle is substituted or stripped in transit. In the verification stage , a synthesis provider
or regulator independently checks the bundle’s integrity and authenticity. The risk here is that a forged or
backdated signature is accepted as valid. The architecture addresses each stage in turn: Hardware
‑bo
und
signing inside a trusted execution environment (TEE) removes the designer’s ability to forge or suppress log
entries at the design stage, because the private key is never exposed to the host operating system; the crypto ‑
graphic hash chain and in

log provides an independent, append‑on

key trust assumption underlying the entire schema is that the TEE manufacturer and the transparency log
operator are not colluding with the attacker, an assumption that is consistent with the threat model above,
which focuses on individual designers rather than nation state–level adversaries capable of compromising
hardware supply chains.

CHAPTER 3
Requirements and Design of an Audit System
This chapter describes the verifiable audit trail (V AT) protocol at the level of its logical steps and security
properties, independent of any specific implementation. The goal is to specify what each party must do,
what artifacts are produced, and what security guarantees result, in a way that allows implementers to select
appropriate technologies while ensuring that the essential properties of the schema are preserved.
The protocol described in this chapter draws on concepts from cryptography and computer hardware
security that may be unfamiliar to readers without a technical background. Four concepts are foundational
to understanding how the system works:
•
 A ha
sh function is a mathematical operation that takes any piece of data, such as a file, a log entry, or
a sequence, and produces a short, fixed ‑le
ngth fingerprint. The fingerprint is effectively unique: Any
change to the original data, even a single character, produces a completely different fingerprint. This
property is what makes tampering detectable.
•
 A ha
sh chain is a sequence of records in which each record includes the fingerprint of the record before
it; altering any record breaks all the fingerprints that follow, making the alteration immediately appar ‑
ent.
• At
testation is a cryptographically verifiable claim that a specific, known piece of software is running in
a specific environment.
• A TEE i
s an isolated region within a computer processor that is protected by hardware, such that even
the owner of the device cannot read or modify what runs inside it.
Together, these four building blocks—hash functions, hash chains, attestation, and TEEs—allow the audit
trail to be tamper evident, independently verifiable, and resistant to falsification by the designer. Readers
seeking definitions of additional technical terms used in this chapter and in Chapters 4 and 5 should consult
the glossary.
Parties and Roles
The protocol involves four parties. The designer is the individual or organization that uses AI ‑bi o tools to
generate a biological sequence. The recorder is a software process, operating under a hardware‑en
forced trust
boundary on the designer’s behalf, that observes and logs each step of a design run. The notary is an indepen‑
dent service that receives the completed log, validates it against defined policies, and issues a signed attesta ‑
tion. The verifier is any downstream party, such as a synthesis provider or regulator, that receives a design
submission and wishes to confirm its provenance. These roles are not necessarily filled by different organiza‑
tions; a synthesis provider could operate both a notary and a verifier. What matters is that each role carries
distinct trust assumptions, described in the “Protocol Phases” section later in this chapter.

Security Properties and Their Justifications
Properties of security—completeness, immutability, usability, scalability, extensibility, and neutral trust
anchor—can be mapped to the protocol steps. Completeness is a logging requirement: It specifies what must
be captured in phase 1 and is what makes phase 4 check (4) meaningful. Immutability is enforced jointly by
the hash chain in phase 1 and the transparency log in phase 3. Usability and scalability are constraints on
implementation rather than cryptographic properties, but they are security relevant because a schema that
imposes prohibitive overhead will be circumvented. Extensibility is a schema property that ensures that the
logging format can accommodate new tool types and new risk indicators without breaking existing verifica ‑
tions. The neutral trust anchor property is what justifies hardware
‑ro
oted trust in phase 1 and independent
notarization in phase 3: Without it, the schema reduces to a self‑si
gned claim by the designer, which a deter‑
mined attacker could fabricate.
Achieving tool independence requires that the protocol remain agnostic to which specific software is used
at each step of a design workflow. This is accomplished through two design choices. First, the structured
provenance envelope uses open, domain
‑neu
tral standards that can represent any computational activity
regardless of which tool performed it: A Rosetta scoring run and an AlphaFold structure prediction are each
recorded as a provenance activity node with typed input and output (I/O) entities, and the schema does not
change between tools. Second, recording is implemented as a process that intercepts file I/O and environ ‑
ment variables at the operating
‑sy
stem level rather than requiring integration within each tool, meaning that
even legacy tools with no application programming interface (API) or plug ‑in a
rchitecture can be logged
without modification to their source code.
Protocol Phases
The following sections describe how the protocol phases are realized using specific open standards and
technologies.
Phase 1: Logging
During a design run, the recorder observes each computational event (tool invocation, input file, parameter
set, output artifact) and appends a structured record to an append
‑on
ly local log. Each record includes a
cryptographic hash of the previous record, forming a hash chain that makes retroactive insertion or dele ‑
tion of entries detectable. The recorder operates within a hardware ‑en
forced trust boundary, such as a TEE,
such that the private signing key is never accessible to the designer or the host operating system. This prop ‑
erty of hardware‑ro
oted trust is what distinguishes the recorder’s attestation from a self ‑si
gned claim by the
designer: It provides strong evidence that the log was produced by a known, unmodified software process
rather than by the designer acting unilaterally. Note that software
‑ro
oted trust, in which the signing key is
protected only by operating‑sy
stem access controls, may be acceptable in lower‑ri
sk deployment contexts but
provides weaker guarantees because a sufficiently privileged attacker on the host machine could extract the
key.

Once the log is written, it needs to be locked so that entries cannot be removed or rewritten. Think of a wax
seal on an envelope: If the seal is broken, it is apparent that someone opened the letter. In the digital world,
that seal is created with hashes—mathematical fingerprints that change if even a single character changes.
Each diary entry includes the fingerprint (SHA

6 hash) of the previous entry, forming a chain. If someone
edits line 3, the hash on line 4 no longer matches, and the chain breaks. The final hash is then signed with a
private key stored in a secure chip (HSM or TPM) or a trusted notary. The signature is wrapped in an attesta‑

actor cannot delete the line that says “filtered out 78 sequences because they were too toxic” without the hash
chain revealing the cut. The signature proves to a synthesis provider that the log they received is identical to
what existed right after the design run finished (Mohanty et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2025).

Policy Considerations and Conclusion
The preceding chapters have established the verification gap and proposed a technical solution to it via secure
audit trails. This chapter details some implications of a verifiable audit chain and the challenges to adoption
within the research enterprise.
To bridge the verification gap in protein design, we recommend advancing the conceptual framework pre‑
sented here toward a scalable, fully developed audit

Policy Implications of Verifiable Audit Trails
The technical protocol described in this paper has implications that extend beyond implementation choices
and into the structure of biosecurity governance. This chapter articulates those implications, as follows,

•
 Ve

synthesis providers focuses primarily on the output: The sequence submitted for synthesis is compared

a known dangerous sequence?” but “Was this sequence generated by a process that included optimiza ‑
tion objectives consistent with legitimate research?” This is a meaningful expansion of the information
available at the point of screening, and policy frameworks governing synthesis providers should be
updated to reflect it, by both specifying what audit information providers may request and establishing
what inferences they are permitted to draw from it.

Traditional and AI‑Dri ven Design Tools
Traditional protein-design tools focus on using physics ‑bas ed modeling and search algorithms to identify
optimal amino acid sequences that would fold into desired structures. As the name suggests, AI-driven
protein-design tools use AI or other machine learning models. In some instances, this entails inverting a
model, whereby software designed to predict protein structures from sequences then solves the inverse prob‑

past ten years and are the primary driver of the verification gap described in Chapter 2.
Rosetta is one of the most established and widely used computational platforms for protein design. It
can perform de novo protein design, enzyme design, ligand docking modeling, and structure prediction of
biological macromolecules and macromolecular complexes. Rosetta’s approach incorporates physics

Glossary
amino acid sequence: The ordered string of chemical building blocks called amino acids that make up a
protein. Just as letters in a specific order form words with specific meanings, amino acids in a specific order
cause a protein to fold into a specific three‑di
mensional shape and perform a specific biological function. In
protein design, generating or modifying an amino acid sequence is the primary computational task (Jumper
et al., 2021a).
attestation: A cryptographically verifiable claim that a specific, unmodified piece of software is running in
a specific hardware environment. Attestation allows a remote party, such as a notary or verifier, to confirm
that the recorder process has not been tampered with, without needing physical access to the machine on
which it runs (Ménétrey et al., 2024). See also remote attestation and trusted execution environment .
BioCompute Object (BCO): A standardized format for describing and communicating the steps of a com ‑
putational biology workflow, developed to support reproducibility and regulatory review. In the context of
this protocol, the BCO’s provenance and execution domain fields provide a schema for recording design run
metadata in a structured, machine
‑re
adable way (Alterovitz et al., 2018).
de novo protein design: The computational design of a protein with a structure or function that does not
exist in nature, as opposed to modifying an existing natural protein. De novo design is one of the higher ‑
ri
sk applications of AI‑bi
o tools from a biosecurity perspective because it can produce sequences that do not
resemble any known natural sequence and therefore may evade screening methods based on sequence simi ‑
larity to known pathogens (Watson et al., 2023).
dual-use research of concern (DURC): Research that, while conducted for legitimate scientific purposes,
could be misused to pose a threat to public health, safety, or national security. NIH maintains a formal DURC
policy that focuses on laboratory manipulation of specific high
‑co
nsequence pathogens; we recommend con‑
sideration of whether in silico protein design using AI tools should be brought within the scope of that frame‑
work (Imperiale and Casadevall, 2015).
FAST-All (FASTA): Standard DNA file type containing the nucleic acid bases (Pearson and Lipman, 1988).
GeoIP: An address indicating the geographical location of the IP address used (Wang and Simmons, 2006).
hardware security module (HSM): A dedicated physical device or a protected region within a processor
designed to generate, store, and use cryptographic keys without exposing the key material to the host system.
HSMs are used in applications in which the integrity of a signing key must be guaranteed even if the sur ‑
rounding software environment is compromised. In this protocol, notary services use HSM ‑back
ed keys to
sign attestations (Aref, Alfughi, and Ouda, 2025).
hash chain: A sequence of records in which each record contains the hash of the record immediately pre ‑
ceding it. Because each record’s hash depends on all previous records, inserting, deleting, or modifying any
record breaks the chain at that point and makes the alteration immediately detectable. Hash chains are the
primary mechanism for tamper evidence in the logging phase of this protocol (Lefevre and Mennink, 2024).

into a short, fixed‑le

input always produces the same output, but any change to the input, however small, produces a completely
different output. This makes hashes useful as tamper

in a transparency log. An inclusion proof allows a verifier to confirm that a particular attestation was sub ‑
mitted to the log at a particular time, without needing to download or inspect the entire log (Laurie, 2014).
in-toto: A framework for supply chain security that allows each step in a multistep process to produce a
signed attestation describing what it did, what inputs it received, and what outputs it produced. The resulting
chain of attestations allows a verifier to confirm that a final artifact was produced by the expected sequence
of steps. In this protocol, in

Merkle log: An append‑only ledger whose entries are leaves of a Merkle tree, enabling compact inclusion
proofs and making any modification or deletion cryptographically detectable (Laurie, 2014).
protein folding: The process by which a chain of amino acids spontaneously adopts a specific three
‑
di
mensional structure. A protein’s structure determines its function; predicting how a given sequence will
fold is therefore a central challenge in both biological research and biosecurity risk assessment. AI tools, such
as AlphaFold, have dramatically improved the accuracy and speed of structure prediction (Jumper et al.,
2021a).
remote attestation: Attestation performed across a network connection, allowing a remote party to verify
the identity and integrity of a software process running on a machine that it does not physically control.
Remote attestation is the mechanism by which a notary can confirm that a submitted log was produced by
a genuine, unmodified recorder process running inside a TEE rather than by a designer
‑co

bundle: the package of log, attestation, and inclusion proof that a designer submits to a synthesis provider or
regulator (Leo et al., 2024).
root hash: A single hash value that summarizes an entire hash chain or Merkle tree. Computing the root
hash of a log allows a verifier to confirm the integrity of the entire log by checking a single value rather than
rehashing every individual record (Laurie, 2014).
sequence similarity: A measure of how closely two biological sequences resemble each other. High sequence
similarity to a known pathogen or toxin is used as a risk indicator in synthesis screening. The protocol’s
