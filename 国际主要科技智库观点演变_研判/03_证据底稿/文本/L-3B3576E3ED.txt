About This Report
As global artificial intelligence (AI) competition intensifies, the United States faces critical
decisions about how to structure AI partnerships with allies and partners, particularly with the
European Union (EU) and EU member states that have developed valuable yet often overlooked AI
capabilities. In this report, I provide U.S. policymakers and industry leaders with a strategic
framework for assessing the conditional value of AI partnerships across different technological futures
and identify options for transatlantic cooperation that advance U.S. strategic interests. This analysis is
intended for policymakers and industry stakeholders involved in AI strategy, international technology
cooperation, and alliance management.

Summary
Issue
This study examines scenario-based approaches to structuring U.S.–European Union (EU) and
member state artificial intelligence (AI) cooperation amid intensifying U.S.-China rivalry, at a
moment when AI leadership is becoming central to economic competitiveness, global standard-setting,
and the maintenance of democratic governance (as articulated in Winning the Race: America’s AI
Action Plan and the 2025 National Security Strategy of the United States of America).
1
The case for recalibration does not rest on the absence of transatlantic institutional architecture.
Government mechanisms, research, and private-sector partnerships are already substantial, although
some have gone dormant. It rests on a scope mismatch between what that architecture delivers and
what the competitive moment requires. The existing toolkit enables bilateral dialogue, research
collaboration, venture capital for the most visible EU firms, and market access with reasonable
competence. What it has not produced is a strategic partnership for AI. For the purposes of this
analysis, I define a strategic AI partnership as a formal, durable collaboration that aligns resources,
rules, and incentives across the AI development and deployment life cycle, enabling each partner to
gain capabilities or leverage that it could not efficiently secure on its own while providing a degree of
insulation from short-term political or commercial volatility.
The existing architecture has not accounted for the possibility that the strategic value of EU and
member state offerings may differ as AI develops. A strategic partnership designed without that
contingency thinking risks being well calibrated for one future and poorly positioned for several
others, leaving allied capabilities underdeveloped in domains that may prove consequential to the
democratic bloc’s collective position. To inform this assessment, I analyze four archetypal scenarios—
Concentrated Dominance, Commoditized Frontier, Dependent Specialization, and Collaborative
Specialization—representing the structural pathways along which AI development could unfold,
defined by two variables: (1) the ultimate locus of AI value creation and (2) the degree of advanced
model openness.
By mapping EU and member state capabilities against these potential futures, I propose a
conditional framework for partnership, one that moves away from asking how deeply the United
States should engage with such allies toward asking under what conditions different forms of
engagement generate the greatest returns for U.S. strategic interests. The framework encompasses five
cooperation efforts. Two foundational efforts—an evaluation, testing, and standards interoperability
stack and lithography resilience efforts—generate returns across all trajectories. The remaining three
scenario-contingent efforts are those whose priorities are calibrated against the signposts identified
during the course of this analysis.

Study Approach
To address the uncertainties inherent in assessing Europe’s role in future AI development, I
combined the mapping of current EU and member state AI capabilities, as of June 2026, with an
evaluation of how those capabilities might perform across different technological development
pathways. Data collection involved collating and cross-referencing materials from multiple source
categories: government policy documents, including the EU AI Act; industry reports from technology
companies and consulting firms; academic publications identified through targeted searches, using
such terms as “transatlantic AI cooperation” and “EU AI governance”; business intelligence from
financial news outlets and market research firms; and corporate communications, including press
releases. I prioritized primary sources over secondary aggregations.
Stakeholder consultations with private-sector stakeholders, AI researchers, and U.S. and EU
current and former government officials provided contextual information that complemented the
document-based research. These consultations served two purposes: validating preliminary
assessments of EU and member state capabilities against real-world experience and identifying
cooperation challenges and opportunities not evident in public sources. The discussions focused on
areas in which EU governance expertise, member state sectoral specialization, and institutional
deployment capabilities could complement U.S. technological leadership, as well as on structural
constraints that might limit the effectiveness of partnerships.
I used AI tools selectively in this analysis to support specific methodological tasks. I used Google
NotebookLM as a navigation tool to identify relevant sections within large-scale reports, such as the
annual AI Index Report published by Stanford University, which I then verified and extracted
independently. OpenAI’s Deep Research mode helped identify potentially authoritative sources, all of
which I independently verified before including in this analysis. In both cases, AI tools served as
research aids rather than as analytical decisionmakers. I retain full responsibility for the interpretation,
synthesis, and conclusions drawn from the material underlying the analysis presented in this report.
Building on the empirical foundation from data collection and stakeholder input, I constructed
four archetypal scenarios to assess how EU and member state capabilities might perform under
different AI development futures. These scenarios derive from two variables that I assessed to be the
most uncertain drivers of where AI value may accrue. Finally, I synthesized insights to develop a
cooperation framework based on varying assumptions about technological development trajectories,
capability evolution, and available resources.
A note on indicator selection is warranted. National and regional AI capacity can be assessed
across a variety of indicators, including AI model use, adoption rates, import data, research outputs,
and patent activity, among others, and a comprehensive assessment of each was outside the scope of
this study. This study examines the EU AI ecosystem across governance frameworks, infrastructure
efforts, and model development strengths, which stakeholder consultations consistently identified as
the dimensions that most directly bear on the strategic partnership questions that this study examines.
Several other indicators appear where relevant throughout the report to paint a broader picture of EU
AI capacity, but I did not analyze them in depth as I did the primary inputs to the partnership
assessment. Similarly, an in-depth assessment of U.S. and EU export control regimes and AI
development for dual-use or military purposes falls outside the scope of this study.

Key Findings
• According to the scenario logic, two cooperation efforts are likely to generate returns across all four
AI trajectories analyzed and should be pursued now. These are establishing a permanent joint
infrastructure for evaluating AI systems and maintaining interoperable safety standards and a
coordinated framework to sustain the research and development (R&D), supply chain
integrity, and operational security of the allied lithography base.
• Three additional efforts should be calibrated against observable signposts indicating which of two
variables driving AI futures may be materializing: the benchmark gap between proprietary and
open-source models and the margin differentials between general-purpose and specialized AI
providers. These efforts involve structuring U.S. private investment to extend compute access
to EU startups, small- to medium-sized enterprises (SMEs), and research institutions;
deepening cooperation on the privacy-preserving technologies that enable trustworthy AI
deployment in regulated sectors; and building financing and distribution networks that help
specialized EU firms commercialize. Such a split gives policymakers a defensible sequencing
logic: Act on the two foundational efforts now; build institutional readiness for the three
scenario-contingent efforts.
• The EU AI Act’s effects run counter to the prevailing narrative. The often-cited compliance
burden is driven by the cumulative effects of the General Data Protection Regulation, sectoral
rules, and national policies rather than by the Act itself. To the extent that global AI
regulation continues converging toward EU-style risk-classification frameworks, the firms
bearing the highest compliance costs are also those best positioned to export compliant
solutions, because the conformity infrastructure they have built can be redeployed into new
markets at a fraction of the cost that competitors must incur to build one from scratch. The
Act should therefore be understood as a conditional first-mover dynamic for trustworthy
deployment, with implications for how U.S. firms should think about EU partners as
compliance bridges into regulated markets adopting equivalent frameworks.
• U.S. private capital is reshaping EU member states’ AI infrastructure, but its allocation patterns
may foreclose broader access outcomes that the alliance should seek to secure. Hyperscaler-anchored
capacity is being absorbed before it reaches the market, often concentrating compute among
large enterprise customers and underserving SMEs, startups, and research institutions, where
specialized innovation also occurs.
• Export controls on ASML’s sales to China are necessary, but they impose revenue pressure on the
R&D pipeline that the U.S. AI hardware base benefits from. Coordinated long-horizon R&D
funding commitments to sustain high numerical aperture extreme ultraviolet lithography
innovation across project cycles are largely absent; no public evidence of a pre-agreed U.S.
emergency allocation arrangement commits ASML output during a supply crisis; and the
publicly available record does not document an ASML-specific allied framework for joint
threat intelligence and incident response, although visible coordination has a deterrence and
alliance cohesion value that arrangements beyond public view cannot fully substitute for.
Together, these vulnerabilities weaken the resilience of one of the most consequential global
hardware chokepoints.

• EU and member state skepticism of U.S. dependency is neither irrational nor hostile; it reflects a
legitimate response to accumulated asymmetry. The same imbalances that provide the United
States with leverage today are generating political pressure that may erode it tomorrow, with
EU entities increasingly inclined to build alternatives wherever feasible. Maximizing short-
term leverage from EU dependence is therefore self-defeating; structuring the relationship so
that allied capability grows through cooperation is the most durable insurance against
ecosystem fragmentation.
• EU and member state AI asset value in part depends on whether economic value concentrates in
general-purpose or specialized models and on how accessible advanced models become. For instance,
in a future where specialized models capture value comparable to or greater than that of
general-purpose systems and open-source models remain competitive enough to serve as
accessible foundations for that specialization, EU member states’ sectoral expertise becomes
one of the principal inputs to AI value creation. By contrast, in a future where a small number
of firms control proprietary general-purpose models capable of performing most economically
valuable tasks and open-source alternatives fall significantly behind, the EU and member states
lose some of their differentiating value.

Chapter 1
Introduction
Global competition over artificial intelligence (AI) supremacy has emerged as a defining contest of
the 21st century. The United States commands the high ground in frontier models and hosts the
world’s leading compute infrastructure (Kariuki, 2025). China is staking its AI leadership claim
through state-directed investments, particularly in compute efficiency (Heim, 2025b). The leading
nation may shape the rules governing emerging technology, capture disproportionate economic
returns, and influence how AI systems reshape the global order (Mazarr, 2026, p. v). Failure to
maintain that leadership position risks ceding influence to adversaries (Executive Office of the
President, 2025).
The contours of that competition, and the imperatives it generates for U.S. strategy, are by now
broadly accepted across the U.S. policy community. Less well-resolved is a question on which U.S.
strategy depends: How should the United States partner with its European allies—primarily the
European Union (EU) and its member states—to maintain and extend its competitive position?

governments have built scaffolding for technology cooperation across government, university, and
private-sector channels, although some of it has gone dormant in recent years.

The diagnosis, then, is a mismatch of both scope and durability. Existing mechanisms have not
produced, and are structurally unlikely to produce, the level of coordination required to permanently
protect irreplaceable allied assets, build shared infrastructure for AI governance, or enable the
commercialization of allied capabilities at the scale their strategic potential warrants. Those gaps carry
a cost. The collective position the democratic bloc must maintain against state-directed competitors
erodes wherever allied assets go unprotected, shared infrastructure frays, or complementary
capabilities remain subscale. This analysis proceeds from that diagnosis. Its purpose is to offer a
framework for determining, across a range of plausible technological futures, which forms of AI
cooperation advance U.S. strategic interests—and, in doing so, strengthen the AI alliance that the
United States shares an interest in fortifying (Executive Office of the President, 2025, p. 22).
One aspect of that last point requires emphasis: a range of plausible technological futures. This is
where the standard policy treatment of transatlantic AI cooperation falls short. The prevailing
approach asks how deeply the United States should engage with allies, as though the answer were
fixed. It is not. Along certain development trajectories, EU and member state capabilities—
particularly in such sectors as health care, manufacturing and robotics, financial services, and
government services—could considerably strengthen the U.S. position.

European markets dominate digitally enabled service exports and host dense networks of AI talent

engagement weakens considerably. The value of EU and member state AI assets depends on how AI
itself evolves—on whether economic value concentrates in general-purpose or specialized models and
on whether advanced models remain proprietary or become openly available.
The more defensible question, therefore, is not how much to cooperate but under what conditions
different forms of cooperation generate the greatest strategic returns. Answering that question
requires a framework built for uncertainty rather than optimized for a single predicted future. A
partnership designed without contingency thinking risks being well calibrated for one trajectory and
poorly positioned for several others, leaving allied capabilities underdeveloped in domains that may
prove consequential.

Chapter 2
The Strategic Inventory
Bold ambitions, coupled with systemic impediments, characterize the EU and member state AI
ecosystem. The EU and its member states hold AI assets of genuine strategic value, including a
governance architecture with first-mover potential in regulated markets, a monopoly position in
advanced lithography, deep sectoral expertise, and a research base of global standing. Those assets
coexist with structural constraints—capital scarcity during growth and late-stage development phases,
insufficient sovereign compute, fragmented data access, and talent outflow—that have consistently
prevented the broader base of AI capabilities from reaching commercial scale. This chapter documents
both sides of that picture.
That picture is visible in aggregate metrics. AI deployment is becoming embedded in EU member
state economies—20 percent of all EU enterprises used AI in 2025—but the pipeline from research to
commercial scale continues to leak (Eurostat, 2026a, pp. 13, 16).
5 The EU produced 18 percent of
global AI scientific publications between 2020 and 2024 yet holds less than 3 percent of global AI
patents—a gap the EU, the European Investment Bank (EIB), and the renowned Draghi report have
each attributed to fragmented regulation, shallow late-stage financing, and slower technology diffusion
(Niestadt, 2025, pp. 3, 10; Schörner, 2026; StepUp StartUps Consortium, 2025, pp. 13, 45;
European Investment Bank and European Patent Office, 2024, p. 2; Draghi, 2024, p. 6).
In the sections that follow, I examine the EU’s AI ecosystem across three dimensions: (1) the
governance frameworks that shape the operating environment for native and foreign AI firms alike;
(2) the infrastructure and resourcing conditions that currently define the scale and accessibility of
indigenous AI development, as well as the investments that are beginning to alter that picture; and
(3) illustrative sectoral applications in which EU member states have built meaningful, strategically
relevant capabilities that complement U.S. strengths. Together, these three dimensions provide the
empirical foundation for the scenarios that follow, and their interactions, which I examine at the close
of the technology stack assessment, determine which cooperation efforts generate compounding
returns.

sectoral regulations, and national implementation strategies—not solely the EU AI Act (Fairless and

Yet those requirements generate conformity infrastructure—audit workflows, documentation
architecture, tested risk-management systems, and trained personnel—whose marginal cost of
redeployment into new markets adopting comparable frameworks would be a fraction of the initial
development cost. To the extent that global regulation continues converging toward EU-style risk-
classification models, the Act creates a conditional first-mover dynamic for trustworthy deployment,
with direct implications for how U.S. firms should assess EU partners as compliance bridges into
regulated markets.
That condition is partially met and advancing. Brazil’s AI bill, Colorado’s AI legislation, and
emerging elements in Asian jurisdictions each show convergence toward EU-style provisions, although
none yet replicates the full framework (Almada, 2025). The EU has moved to accelerate the timeline
on its own through the AI Pact, which invites companies to implement the Act requirements ahead of
legal deadlines, compressing the interval between regulatory adoption and conformity infrastructure
maturity (EU, 2026). The trajectory is therefore plausible and partially underway but not yet certain
enough to be treated as a planning assumption. It is a conditional asset whose value scales with the
number of jurisdictions that adopt equivalent provisions and whose absence would substantially
narrow the Act’s strategic significance to the EU’s internal market alone.
The Act’s limited reach is not randomly distributed across the economy. Many AI applications in
industrial manufacturing and robotics, a sector where EU member states hold documented
comparative advantages, are subject to limited regulation (Laurer, Renda, and Yeung, 2021). For EU
member state companies adopting AI, particularly SMEs that use off-the-shelf models in non-high-
risk contexts, the Act’s direct obligations remain limited compared with existing GDPR and sector-
specific compliance work. Where high-risk classification does apply—notably in health care, financial
services, and government operations—it is those very obligations that generate the conformity
infrastructure whose first-mover value is described above (European Parliament, 2025; Vardas,
Marketou, and Vardas, 2025; European Insurance and Occupational Pensions Authority, 2024). In
these sectors, the compliance cost and the strategic asset are the same investment viewed from
different angles.
The compliance friction most reported by EU firms reflects the cumulative interaction of multiple
regulatory layers rather than the EU AI Act in isolation. Businesses allocate 40 percent of their
information technology (IT) budgets to regulatory compliance, and two-thirds remain unclear about
the Act’s specific obligations, a confusion that itself suggests the difficulty of disentangling one
instrument from the broader stack (Fairless and Luhnow, 2025). The EIB finds that 59 percent of EU
firms view compliance with new regulations as an obstacle to business activities, with bureaucratic
costs consuming an estimated 1.1 percent of turnover for firms broadly and 1.8 percent for smaller
companies (EIB, 2025, pp. 3, 12). Divergent national implementation strategies compound the
picture; despite unified EU legislation, varying member state approaches generate additional friction
for firms operating across borders (EU Artificial Intelligence Act, 2026). The cumulative weight of
these layers—not any single instrument—is the primary friction point.

The EU has built direct countermeasures to the compliance uncertainty documented above. For
instance, regulatory sandboxes, mandated across all member states, allow firms to test AI systems in
controlled environments with direct regulatory guidance before bearing full conformity costs (EU
Artificial Intelligence Act, 2025). For SMEs and startups, in particular, sandboxes and other such
simplification mechanisms reduce the gap between regulatory intent and operational clarity that the
“two-thirds unclear” finding reflects, offering a structured pathway through the overlapping demands
of the EU AI Act, GDPR, and sectoral rules rather than requiring firms to navigate them unaided
(European Commission, undated-g).
Because the EU AI Act’s enforcement timeline is only beginning, definitively assessing its
operational impact would be premature. What can be said is that the conformity infrastructure the
Act mandates also generates auditable deployment records that function as a standing security
screening layer for AI systems entering critical allied sectors, a benefit the United States captures
without building or funding these mechanisms independently. For the partnership framework
discussed later in this analysis, the EU’s governance infrastructure constitutes a conditional asset that
cooperation should be designed to leverage, and the cumulative compliance friction constitutes a
design constraint that cooperation mechanisms should help manage.
That friction is unlikely to resolve quickly. The European Commission’s proposed Digital
Omnibus simplification package targets smoother implementation of the EU AI Act, and such figures
as French President Emmanuel Macron and former Italian Prime Minister Mario Draghi explicitly
advocate regulatory efficiency (Scott, 2025; Merli, 2025). Yet securing agreement among the 27
member states remains challenging; reform tends to succeed only when national priorities align with
EU objectives, leaving most simplification efforts stalled in lengthy negotiation (Schumann, Minano,
and Maggiore, 2025; European Council, 2026).

The Infrastructure Beneath: From Lithography to Talent Retention
To identify where deeper engagement can most effectively support capability development, we
must first understand the EU and member states’ position across relevant components of the
technological stack—the layered infrastructure of hardware, software, and services that enables AI
systems to function. Central to this effort is the EU’s EuroStack initiative, an ambitious digital
sovereignty project aiming to reduce dependence on external technology providers by building
indigenous infrastructure across semiconductors, cloud services, AI platforms, and data governance
frameworks (EuroStack, undated). However, EuroStack faces formidable obstacles, including a
projected cost exceeding €5 trillion for a fully independent stack, market fragmentation across EU
member states, and balancing sovereignty goals with geopolitical and market dynamics across
components (Cheslow and Pollet, 2025).
Rather than surveying that full ambition, the subsections that follow provide an overview of the
dimensions of the stack that most directly bear on cooperation design: ASML’s lithography machines,
EU compute and data access conditions, the capital environment that finances development across all
layers, and talent.

ASML’s Next-Generation Lithography Monopoly
The most unambiguous global leverage within the EU resides in advanced semiconductor
manufacturing equipment, where the Dutch-based ASML holds a monopoly on high numerical
aperture extreme ultraviolet (High-NA EUV) lithography—the only technology capable of producing
the most-advanced chips powering frontier AI systems (ASML, undated-a; van der Lugt, 2024). The
magnitude of this position warrants emphasis. ASML is expanding its annual output by 36 percent in
2026, a ramp-up whose pace is set not by demand but by the production capacity the company can
physically bring online (Mackrael, 2026).
ASML’s research and development (R&D) spending is also growing by roughly 16 percent
annually and will likely need to accelerate further (Mugisha and Jenkins, 2025, p. 3). That acceleration
is largely driven by AI customers who are seeking a 16-fold increase in the density of transistors every
two years, up from a historical twofold increase over the same time frame, a trajectory whose
achievability, according to ASML’s chief executive officer, depends on the company’s ability to fund
the extraordinarily high cost such a pace demands (Norges Bank Investment Management, 2025,
minutes 7–9). Because ASML’s R&D is primarily financed through commercial revenue, sustained
high demand can partially close that gap (ASML, 2025a, pp. 33, 52). Nonetheless, the gap between
what AI customers require and what revenue alone can support is not trivial. ASML has, in the past,
asked customers to co-invest directly in its R&D to sustain the pace of innovation (Norges Bank
Investment Management, 2025, minute 13; ASML, 2012a).
ASML’s R&D model functions well when its customer base is broad and expanding. It comes
under pressure when that base contracts. Since 2018, successive rounds of U.S. export restrictions

Abundant Data, Restricted Flow
Even if the EU and its member states solved their computing challenges, accessible, high-quality
data would remain a binding constraint. The raw material is plentiful. EU member states generate
strategically distinctive datasets at scale, including longitudinal clinical records within national health
systems and granular data on securities, loans, and entities across the EU’s banking sector, that are
hard to replicate elsewhere (European Commission, undated-d; Mayerlen, 2023). The problem is not
volume or quality; it is accessibility.

distinctive data assets in the ecosystem fall under GDPR and sectoral rules. None of these barriers
yields to capital deployment or compute expansion. Resolution requires regulatory simplification,
harmonized national frameworks, and the maturation of privacy-preserving technologies that would
allow sensitive data to be used in AI development without legal exposure that currently deters such
use.

generative AI, although European firms secured just $20.92 billion compared with $285.88 billion in
the United States (Sajadieh et al., 2026, pp. 173, 179, 183).

A Talent Problem That Is Not a Talent Shortage
Table 2.1 includes talent retention as a factor constraining investment attractiveness; a full account
of EU member state talent dynamics lies beyond the scope of this analysis, but one distinction matters
directly for cooperation design. One mapping of global AI professionals found that Europe has
roughly 30 percent more AI talent per capita than the United States and nearly three times as much as
China (Kiorri, 2026). The problem, then, is not production but talent attraction and retention. Net
tech talent inflow to Europe fell from around 52,000 individuals to just 26,000 individuals between
2022 and 2024; only 10 percent of the top native AI researchers remain on the continent, and the
outflow is disproportionately concentrated among senior-level professionals (Kiorri, 2026; Mendel,
2025).
The retention problem operates through multiple channels, whose relative weights are difficult to
isolate. Firms may relocate to access deeper venture capital ecosystems and lighter regulatory

Distributed Excellence in AI Applications
Alongside the metrics of model scale and compute intensity on which U.S. and Chinese
competition is most visibly fought, other indicators, including specialized model development,
domain-specific research output, and sectoral deployment, tell a meaningfully different and more
favorable story about the EU’s AI trajectory. The pathway to an economically and strategically
consequential AI future does not hinge solely on indigenous frontier-model leadership; it requires the
capacity to develop, deploy, and scale AI systems that generate value in the domains where a given
economy has the deepest expertise and strongest institutional integration. It is along that pathway that
EU member states have built their most durable and strategically significant AI capabilities.
While firms in the EU market cannot match the sheer scale of resources driving U.S. frontier
model development, they are quietly generating pools of excellence in select AI applications. This is
not a second-best position relative to frontier model development; it is a potentially decisive
competitive posture, particularly under technological trajectories where domain-specific models prove
as valuable as general-purpose models. France’s €10 billion commitment exemplifies this, combining
investments in foundation models with targeted funding for AI in health care and industrial
automation (Bpifrance, 2025; AI Action Summit, 2025). Germany similarly allocates portions of its
€5 billion strategy to sector-specific applications, particularly industrial domains, where German firms
already lead technologically (European Commission, 2021).
EU member states possess meaningful competitive advantages in at least four sectors—
manufacturing and robotics, health care, financial services, and government operations—although the
nature of those advantages varies across them. In manufacturing, the advantage is most clearly
documented relative to other geographies. European manufacturers already lead, with 48 percent
using AI technologies, compared with 28 percent of U.S. manufacturers as of 2026 (“Europe Can Still
Win the Other AI Race,” 2026). In health care, financial services, and government operations, the
advantage is less a matter of scale than of depth. EU member state firms have accumulated domain

demands those qualities, positioning them as natural development environments for applications that
require high trust, auditability, and compliance.
Europe’s strong industrial base, combined with deliberate national strategies to position AI as a
driver of economic competitiveness, makes these sectors particularly promising environments for AI
adoption and application development. Manufacturing exports, with Germany as the largest

frameworks.
The capacity for world-changing specialized AI development within the broader European
ecosystem is not hypothetical, although the most prominent example to date falls outside the EU’s

interorganizational entities hold comparative strength (Google DeepMind and European Molecular

domain expertise and institutional integration (Bosch Rexroth, undated). These examples are not
outliers. They represent an AI development model that is increasingly prevalent in EU member states
that may prove as valuable as frontier model capacity.

Chapter 3
The Fragmentation Risk
Before assessing which cooperation efforts generate returns under which AI futures, we must
address a prior question: What is the cost of failing to cooperate at all? The United States has a self-
interested reason to actively support EU and member state AI capabilities rather than to maximize its
short-term leverage from EU dependence. The same imbalances that provide leverage today are
simultaneously generating political pressure that will erode that advantage tomorrow, with EU entities
increasingly inclined to build alternatives wherever feasible. A more capable partner is a more willing
partner; structuring the relationship so that allied capability grows through cooperation is the most
durable insurance against ecosystem fragmentation. That fragmentation—driven by eroding trust or
an allied government’s conclusion that dependency has grown beyond what is domestically
sustainable—could overlay any of the four scenarios described in the next chapter.
The trajectory toward this outcome is already visible. The U.S.-EU relationship is under
considerable strain. Trade disputes, divergent threat perceptions about the Russia-Ukraine war, and a
broader erosion of institutional trust have created an environment in which EU member state
governments are becoming increasingly skeptical of arrangements that deepen their reliance on U.S.
partners. That skepticism is neither irrational nor hostile; it reflects a legitimate desire to build
indigenous capacity and exercise sovereign control over the infrastructure shaping EU member state
economies. Importantly, a component of that pressure is political; it responds to perceptions of
democratic legitimacy and strategic identity that favorable cooperation terms can moderate but not
eliminate. The risk is a partnership reduced to its irreducible minimum: EU markets would still
purchase chips and would still run workloads on U.S. cloud infrastructure because they must, but they
would treat that dependency as a vulnerability to be managed rather than as a relationship to be
deepened. In every domain where alternatives could feasibly be built, investment would flow toward
building them.
A reasonable objection is that the structural barriers documented earlier in this analysis make it
unlikely that the EU will achieve full technological autonomy within a policy-relevant time horizon,
thereby weakening the urgency of the fragmentation threat. The objection is partially correct. A fully
independent EU AI stack remains structurally out of reach under current conditions. But
fragmentation does not require full autonomy to impose costs. Procurement preferences that
disadvantage U.S. cloud providers in government contracts, data localization mandates, preferential
regulatory treatment for EU-origin AI systems, and deliberate diversification toward non-U.S.
partners in domains where alternatives exist are each achievable within EU institutional authority and
are already visible in nascent form.
Since early 2025, dozens of European governments, municipalities, and public institutions have
begun substituting U.S. providers for domestic or open-source alternatives. Most notably, the
European Parliament switched its default search engine to France’s Qwant, the Dutch government

===== PDF_PAGE 29 =====
19
began migrating its code off of the Microsoft-owned GitHub, Finland declined to host its election
data on Amazon’s cloud, and cities across the Netherlands, France, and Germany are moving to
replace Microsoft Office and Google Docs (Burgess, 2026).
These pressures are now migrating from practice into law. In June 2026, the European
Commission proposed a Cloud AI Development Act designed to ensure that no provider of critical
public workloads retains a “kill switch” over European systems, subjecting cloud vendors to
sovereignty risk assessments on a four-tier scale and requiring that the hardware and software behind
the most-sensitive public contracts be made in the EU (Nicol-Schwarz, 2026b; Chee, 2026).
None of these actions individually constitute a rupture, but their cumulative effect is a progressive
narrowing of EU market access, data flows, and institutional integration on which U.S. AI firms’
European revenue depends. The relevant threshold is not whether the EU can build a competing
ecosystem in the next five years. It is whether the EU can impose sufficient friction on U.S.
participation to erode the value of the transatlantic AI relationship incrementally over time. On that
question, the structural barriers offer considerably less protection than they might appear to.
The cost of fragmentation extends beyond economics and diplomacy to the security infrastructure
on which both blocs depend. The democratic bloc’s capacity to monitor and respond to AI-enabled
adversary activity depends on the same cooperation infrastructure whose erosion this section
describes. Joint threat intelligence on adversary AI capabilities, coordinated red-teaming of models
with national security implications, and shared methodologies for assessing AI-enabled influence
operations all require the kind of trusted institutional channels that fragmentation progressively
closes. A partnership that operates solely on transactional logic is also one in which the allied security
architecture for AI threats operates with incomplete situational awareness.
The risks of a frayed relationship, moreover, are not confined to what the alliance forgoes; they
extend to what a strategic competitor stands to gain. Given the high sovereign debt carried by many
European states, a United States that lets the transatlantic relationship deteriorate risks creating the
very conditions in which Chinese credit lines spread across the continent, purchasing goodwill and
working to pry Europe loose from Washington (Juijn et al., undated).

Chapter 4
Four AI Futures
The fragmentation dynamics described in the preceding chapter operate regardless of which
trajectory materializes. The scenarios that follow address a different question: Under which
technological futures do EU and member state capabilities generate the greatest strategic returns for
the United States? The wide range of plausible outcomes can be meaningfully simplified into four
archetypal scenarios—Concentrated Dominance, Commoditized Frontier, Dependent Specialization,
and Collaborative Specialization. Looking toward 2035, the scenarios capture the range of outcomes
that current technological trajectories and investment patterns could plausibly produce.
Two variables drive the analysis—the ultimate locus of value creation (general-purpose AI
supremacy versus specialized models capture comparable or greater value) and model openness
(closed-source dominance versus open-source remains competitive). These were selected over other
candidate variables on two grounds. First, both remain contested among technical experts and
industry analysts; scaling law evidence cuts in different directions on whether general-purpose models
will continue to absorb specialized use cases, and the post-DeepSeek competitive landscape has left the
closed-versus-open debate unresolved (Briski, 2025; Wolfe, 2025; Schmelzer, 2026; Edmond, 2025;
Eastwood, 2026). Second, while other plausible drivers (including data abundance, computing power
and infrastructure, investment levels, and algorithmic and model-architecture advances) independently
shape AI development, their effects are largely legible through the two chosen variables, because they
influence either where value concentrates or how accessible advanced models become. The reduction is
not perfect.

Four assumptions underpin the scenario matrix. First, the analysis assumes that AI capabilities
advance substantially over the next decade, whether through continued scaling, architectural
breakthroughs, or a combination of both, such that AI systems become capable of performing a
meaningfully broader range of economically and strategically valuable tasks than they do today.
Second, the United States and China are assumed to continue leading in AI, and the scenarios do not
adjudicate which country will prevail in the AI race. Third, ASML’s lithography monopoly is assumed
to persist. Fourth, the scenarios assume that the EU and member states continue along the
development path established earlier in the analysis, potentially narrowing, but likely not closing, the
compute gap. EU member states have produced frontier-capable models, such as those by Mistral AI
and Aleph Alpha, none of which yet match leading U.S. and Chinese models in performance, scale, or
capitalization. That assumption is the starting point for assessment, not a verdict on European AI
development potential.
Concentrated Dominance
Of the four scenarios, Concentrated Dominance typifies the most constrained operating
environment for EU actors. In a scenario where a small number of firms control proprietary models
capable of performing virtually every economically important task and open-source alternatives have
fallen so far behind the frontier as to forfeit their utility as economic instruments, EU actors could face
a dual squeeze.
First, foundation model providers could restrict access, impose punitive licensing terms, or
deprioritize allied markets, leaving U.S. allies at the mercy of decisions made in boardrooms beyond
the reach of democratic accountability. The resulting market structure—chokepoint control

concentrated in a handful of firms over a single critical access layer—would mirror the platform
dynamics the EU’s Digital Markets Act was designed to constrain, yet no equivalent systematic
remedy exists at the foundation model tier (European Commission, undated-a). Second, because
general-purpose models could handle tasks that previously demanded deep sectoral expertise, the
differentiation value of that expertise would erode, without disappearing entirely. Even the most-
capable general-purpose models would likely still require adaptation for deployment into high-risk,
regulated environments. EU and member state leverage would then narrow to two residual positions:
regulated market access for firms seeking to scale proprietary systems across diverse EU environments
and ASML’s lithography monopoly—the one asset in the EU’s strategic inventory whose value this
scenario cannot diminish.
Whether the small number of firms controlling such models includes Chinese providers would
materially affect the EU’s negotiating position, even absent realistic prospects for large-scale adoption.
The EU’s security posture, GDPR constraints, and political resistance to Chinese infrastructure
dependency make wholesale switching practically implausible. Yet credible alternatives need not be
adopted to reshape bargaining dynamics. The existence of competitive Chinese models at or near
parity may discipline U.S. providers’ pricing, licensing terms, and willingness to accommodate
sovereignty requirements, because the prospect of selective adoption in lower-sensitivity commercial
applications would become a latent but credible instrument for EU negotiators. That discipline would
not rest on EU leverage alone. The commercial value of the European market gives U.S. firms their
own stake in preserving access. Just as NVIDIA has lobbied Washington to keep selling chips to
China rather than forfeit that market, model and AI infrastructure providers that depend on
European revenue would have a commercial stake in offering guarantees that preserve it (Garicano and
Grimm, 2026). It would be in the United States’ clear interest to ensure that U.S. model providers
remain preferred not merely by dint of security constraints but also on the merits of the terms they
offer.
The asymmetry inherent in this scenario, particularly if dominant providers chose to gatekeep
access, impose extractive licensing arrangements, or redirect capacity toward other markets, could
fundamentally destabilize U.S.-EU technological cooperation. Confronted with prospective
technological marginalization, EU member states might pursue partnerships to build an independent
AI ecosystem through coalitions with other middle powers. While such a coalition of democratic
middle powers would present itself as nonthreatening, it would still attenuate U.S.-EU technological
integration, generate duplicative development investment, and risk fracturing the standards coherence
and interoperability essential for the democratic bloc to compete effectively against adversaries,
weakening the broader alliance architecture and eroding the collective leverage that a unified
democratic bloc commands against authoritarian competitors. The political dynamics that would
animate such realignment, and the realistic forms it would assume, are developed in Chapter 3.
U.S. stakeholders would therefore benefit from structuring access to U.S. models on terms that
preserve alliance cohesion. EU markets constitute a formidable venue for scaling proprietary systems
across a heterogeneous landscape of regulatory environments, making private-sector investments in
market entry and distribution partnerships essential for capturing the commercial and strategic value
of EU member states’ sophisticated, densely regulated consumer and institutional bases. At the
hardware layer, dedicated investment in ASML’s R&D would ensure that allied lithography capacity

Commoditized Frontier
Unlike Concentrated Dominance, this scenario dissolves the gatekeeper asymmetries that would
most constrain EU actors; however, the advantages it confers are conditional, and their realization
depends heavily on resolving the compute constraints discussed in the preceding chapters and on
activating the governance qualities that, under this scenario, would function as a competitive asset. In a
scenario where general-purpose models dominate value creation and their weights and technical
documentation are circulated openly, EU firms and institutions could access frontier-capable models
without the power asymmetries that define closed-source trajectories and build deployment solutions
on top of freely available systems. Nonetheless, the EU and its member states would likely face
compute constraints, and the value they could extract from this democratization of model access
would depend critically on narrowing them.
For most AI applications, long-distance cloud compute would be technically sufficient, carrying no
meaningful performance penalties. EU-controlled infrastructure, however, would remain preferable
or, in some contexts, legally mandated for fine-tuning on sensitive workloads. Actors with greater
access to affordable, abundant inference compute would therefore capture an outsize share of
economic value. However, because general-purpose models could capably perform most economically
important tasks, including in sectors where EU firms have historically held advantages, the returns to
domain specialization at the model layer would contract, although a residual tier of sectors (i.e., those
with sufficiently distinctive proprietary data inputs or demanding regulatory validation requirements)
would retain niches where specialized development would still remain competitive.

those who control the complementary assets required to deploy it profitably—what economists term
the complementary assets dynamic (Hughes and Morton, 2006). In this scenario, those assets would
include regulatory compliance infrastructure, sovereign compute, and sector-specific validation
capabilities, precisely the areas where EU member states have made their deepest investments. EU

Dependent Specialization
Of the four scenarios, this one positions the EU most ambiguously, because domain expertise
acquires genuine market value but the dependency conditions under which that value must be realized
blunt much of its strategic force. A scenario in which fine-tuned and domain-specific models capture a
large share of economic value would vindicate the sectoral depth EU member states have accumulated.
Still, the benefits would be unevenly realized if open-source alternatives have fallen significantly
behind the frontier, leaving the foundation models on which much fine-tuning depends proprietary
and controlled by a small number of firms. As in the Concentrated Dominance scenario, whether
Chinese providers were among those holding proprietary foundation models would affect EU
bargaining dynamics. Alternative non-U.S. providers, even imperfect ones, would expand the range of
options available to EU firms seeking platform access. The landscape would likely bifurcate.
For applications built on fine-tuned closed-source models, EU firms would possess the domain
knowledge, regulatory familiarity, and operational data that generate value. Nonetheless, they would
build on platforms whose access terms they do not set. The resulting dynamic is what transaction cost
economics terms a hold-up problem, whereby each round of fine-tuning investment binds an EU firm
or organization more tightly to the specific model architecture on which it builds, raising switching
costs and progressively weakening the firm’s negotiating position at each contract renewal (Hart,
2007, p. 2).
The EU AI Act would partially offset this asymmetry, because high-risk AI providers operating in
or deploying through EU markets would be obligated to furnish technical documentation, training
data summaries, copyright policies, and instructions for use, giving EU firms mandated visibility into
the models on which they build and an informational edge over developers in jurisdictions offering no
equivalent transparency floor (EU Artificial Intelligence Act, undated-b). For applications where
narrow models could be trained from scratch on proprietary operational data, EU firms would retain
full autonomy over development and deployment, although the competitive standing of such models
relative to those trained on leading frontier models would remain uncertain.

The resulting transatlantic dynamic would be mutually valuable but unevenly structured. Frontier
providers would need regulated market access to deploy models into high-risk domains and would face
constraints in sectors where models must be trained on datasets that reside exclusively within EU
institutional contexts, including clinical data governed by national health systems, proprietary
manufacturing sensor data, or financial compliance records subject to national regulatory regimes.
These data advantages would provide EU firms with durable niches that frontier providers could not
fill unilaterally, regardless of model capability. EU firms, however, would depend on proprietary
foundation models for applications where building independently on open-source alternatives would
yield significantly inferior performance. Hold-up logic suggests that the balance would tilt toward
model providers, given that a frontier lab could seek domain partners across multiple jurisdictions,
while EU entities’ room to maneuver vis-à-vis proprietary model providers would be circumscribed by
a far narrower set of alternatives.
That imbalance would not be static. If proprietary models continued to advance while open-source
alternatives stagnated further, the EU and member states’ negotiating position would likely erode
incrementally, and the political constituency for alternative partnerships would likely grow, extending
to non-U.S. providers or coalitions of middle powers. This dynamic reprises the fragmentation logic
developed earlier, where asymmetry that generates near-term advantage simultaneously generates the
political pressure that erodes it over time. How firms structure model access under these conditions is
therefore among the most consequential decisions on this path.
A compounding risk runs through the supply chain dimension. If French financial compliance
systems, German health care diagnostics, or Italian industrial automation come to depend on fine-
tuning U.S. proprietary foundation models, the operational continuity of EU critical infrastructure
would become contingent on decisions made by a small number of U.S. firms operating under U.S.
legal and commercial logic. Disruptions at the model provider layer, whether driven by commercial
restructuring, regulatory action, cyber incidents, or geopolitical calculation, would propagate into
sectors whose continuity is a core security interest of the EU and member states. Mitigating this risk
would require supply chain continuity provisions that effectively mandate private-sector behavior, an
approach that I have deliberately excluded from the cooperation framework.

Collaborative Specialization
If the Concentrated Dominance scenario presents the most constrained environment for EU
actors, Collaborative Specialization is the most generative, a scenario in which the conditions for
transatlantic complementarity align most naturally, and the EU’s accumulated sectoral depth
translates most directly into shared strategic value. A scenario in which domain-specific, fine-tuned
models capture value comparable to or greater than general-purpose AI models would most directly
reward EU member state positioning, and the benefits would be most fully realized if open-source
models remain competitive enough to serve as accessible foundations for that specialization. Under
these conditions, EU firms could leverage freely available models alongside their deep sectoral

The sectors where EU advantages would prove most consequential would be those requiring both
deep institutional knowledge and robust compliance infrastructure, spanning industrial automation,
health care and pharmaceutical research, financial compliance, and government services. EU firms
would have two pathways to specialized value creation, each preserving meaningful autonomy: They
could fine-tune openly available foundation models with domain expertise and proprietary data or
build narrow models from scratch in domains where proprietary data are sufficient to train high-
performing systems. Because open-source models could be fine-tuned freely, EU firms would not need

frameworks would further consolidate the EU’s position by ensuring the responsible development of
precisely the high-risk applications where specialized models would command global premiums.
Under these conditions, the transatlantic relationship would reach its point of greatest natural
complementarity. The underlying market logic is one of comparative advantage, with the United
States holding relative efficiency at foundation model advancement and EU member states holding it
at domain application and regulatory validation—both capturing more value by trading along those
lines than by pursuing autarky across the full stack. That division of labor is not merely theoretical; as
the sectoral applications surveyed earlier demonstrate, it already characterizes the most productive
transatlantic AI partnerships in the ecosystem. The access constraints that weigh on actors across the
other three scenarios would be largely absent, replaced by a self-enforcing division of labor whose
terms neither party needs to be coerced into accepting. The deciding factors on realizing that
complementarity would be EU sovereign compute, whether specialized EU firms can reach global
markets at scale, and the continued maturation of the open-source ecosystem on which their relative
autonomy depends.

Chapter 5
Structuring Cooperation Under
Uncertainty
Transatlantic AI cooperation cannot be optimized in advance; it benefits from being designed to
evolve. The AI future scenarios examined in Chapter 4 demonstrate why. The strategic value of EU
and member state capabilities is not fixed; it shifts materially depending on whether AI value
concentrates in proprietary foundation models or distributes across specialized applications, as well as
on whether open-source alternatives remain competitive or fall decisively behind the frontier. The
planning challenge this creates is not unique to AI: Colin Gray’s work on defense planning under
conditions of irreducible uncertainty counsels that when the future is “unknown and unknowable in
detail,” the goal is not to predict correctly but to build architectures tolerant of error, adaptable to
emerging conditions, and disciplined by political reality rather than analytical elegance (Gray, 2019,
p. 173). A cooperation framework calibrated to any single assessment of that value risks being well
positioned for one future while poorly structured for several others.
That does not imply that existing cooperation mechanisms have failed. Bilateral frameworks are
functioning, private capital is already reshaping EU and member state infrastructure at a scale that no
government program could match, and both public and private actors are engaged in forms of
coordination that approximate many of the priorities identified in this analysis. The argument
advanced here is that approximation is insufficient when the strategic value of cooperation is itself
scenario-dependent. What the existing architecture has not produced is a conditional logic that signals
to policymakers and industry leaders which priorities might rise and which might recede, as observable
signals indicate the technological trajectory materializing.
The initiatives that follow are not exhaustive; a comprehensive screening of current and potentially
worthwhile cooperation efforts is outside the scope of this analysis. The five cooperation efforts
offered below, identified through the study’s capability- and scenario-based analysis, instead serve as a
menu of engagement options across different technological futures. Many of the investments described
here have analogs in current practice. Where existing efforts are already underway, this analysis
identifies what additional structuring or scaling could help achieve stronger outcomes. Where the
initiatives are more novel, that distinction is noted explicitly. Where specific mechanisms, institutional
arrangements, and funding structures are identified, they are offered as illustrations of what
implementation could look like rather than as prescriptive recommendations. The determination of
specific instruments, mechanisms, and institutional arrangements through which these options are
pursued is appropriately left to the implementing actors best positioned to assess the political, legal,
and operational constraints of their specific contexts.
The preceding chapters establish that geopolitical fragmentation is a cross-cutting risk that
operates independently of which technological scenario materializes, driven by the erosion of trust, the

accumulation of dependency, and the political pressure those dynamics generate over time. Each
cooperation effort in this chapter has been selected, in part, because its pursuit, particularly when
responsive to the signposts identified in the preceding chapter, could help counter that risk.

Foundational Efforts
Both of the following cooperation efforts score high in Table 5.1 because they generate returns
regardless of the scenario. Policymakers and industry leaders who disagree on which scenario is most
likely may nonetheless find common ground here.
Evaluation, Testing, and Standards Interoperability
The ability of U.S. and EU actors to jointly evaluate AI systems, compare results across languages
and deployment contexts, and maintain common safety standards is the connective tissue of the
democratic AI ecosystem. Without it, the transatlantic relationship lacks the shared reference points
that make coordinated governance possible and that prevent a standards divergence from leading to
self-fulfilling fragmentation. This is why the effort scores high across all four scenarios in Table 5.1.
The institutional foundation for this effort exists but is somewhat dormant. The 2022 U.S.-EU
Trade and Technology Council (TTC) Joint Roadmap suggested activities to establish shared
terminologies and taxonomies, collaborate on international technical standards and tools, and monitor
existing and emerging AI risks to ensure interoperable risk management (European Commission,
2022b, p. 2). By April 2024, the TTC had also developed two lists of key AI terms with mutually
accepted definitions (EU-U.S. Trade and Technology Council Working Group 1 Subgroup on AI
Taxonomy and Terminology, 2024). In practice, the TTC’s working group architecture, paired with
its ministerial cadence, facilitated meaningful procedural and technical coordination. It convened the
relevant agencies on both sides, set shared work programs, produced jointly authored deliverables, and
provided a recurring forum for reviewing and advancing AI-specific outputs. Technical dialogue
between the European AI office and the U.S. AI Safety Institute has also explored watermarking,
government compute infrastructure, and AI-for-good use cases (European Commission, 2024a).

===== PDF_PAGE 41 =====
31
That architecture, however, has not convened at the ministerial level since 2024, and its future
remains uncertain. The current political conditions may not be favorable to reaffirming this type of
transatlantic relationship.
Three partial substitutes remain operational and matter for what follows: the International
Network of AI Safety Institutes, the bilateral technical relationship between the United States’
National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) and the European AI Office, and the
OECD’s AI principles (within the broader OECD Council on Artificial Intelligence
recommendation), to which both blocs adhere (Bengio et al., 2026; NIST, 2024; Executive Office of
the President, 2025, p. 22; OECD, undated-c; Dunietz et al., 2024, p. 3). Each provides coordination
capacity that is independent of the TTC’s ministerial format, although none replaces its convening
function.
What the current architecture has not yet produced, and what relative dormancy on the subject
has made more urgent, is the institutional permanence and operational depth that alliance-grade
coordination would call for. The existing stack is better described as a network of institutions whose
connective tissue has frayed than as a fully resourced, durable structure aligning U.S. and EU
governance practices.
Two gaps are particularly consequential. First, the function performed by the TTC’s working
groups and ministerial meetings has not been replaced by an equivalent mechanism, and the
alternatives currently in operation are valuable but episodic, depending on sustained political will from
institutional leadership rather than on a dedicated mandate that endures across administrations.
Second, there exists no reciprocal arrangement for models in high-risk domains comparable to the
bilateral agreements that the United States’ Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI) has
developed with specific U.S. firms, meaning that AI systems entering regulated sectors are not yet
streamlined through a joint transatlantic safety assurance process (Bordelon, 2026). The absence of
such an arrangement may grow particularly consequential as national security considerations rise on
the pre-deployment agenda. Catalyzed by concerns raised by Anthropic’s new Mythos model, the
United States has begun weighing whether AI models should be vetted ahead of release, a deliberation
whose outcome, in the absence of a transatlantic counterpart process, may leave allied regulators
evaluating the same systems against asymmetric criteria and on asymmetric timelines (Bordelon,
2026).
Closing those gaps could involve organizing existing institutions into a more durable form.
However, the political conditions for new permanent institutions do not currently exist. A productive
framing would distinguish between what is achievable within existing institutional mandates and what
could be designed now for implementation when political conditions permit.
In the near term (i.e., within the next year or two), what could be pursued would operate
through proven durable channels—the International Network of AI Safety Institutes, the NIST–
European AI Office bilateral technical relationship, and the OECD AI principles. Two categories of
deepening engagement are achievable without new political authorization. One possibility is more
frequent convenings with standing agenda items and coordinated red-teaming exercises that build the
operational habits that a more formal structure would eventually benefit from. A second is substantive
pilots, including shared test protocols for evaluating high-risk systems and mutual recognition
arrangements for conformity assessment in specific sectors, beginning where regulatory frameworks

===== PDF_PAGE 42 =====
32
are already most closely aligned. These are not substitutes for institutional permanence, but they are
the activities on which permanence could credibly be built without depending on available political
will.
On a longer horizon, what could usefully be designed now, for implementation when conditions
permit, is the institutional form that would make such coordination self-sustaining rather than
dependent on the continued initiative of individual agency leaders. A permanent joint secretariat
would address that requirement by providing a continuous brokerage function that rotating
institutional leadership and episodic ministerial engagement cannot reliably sustain. To be politically
viable when its moment arrives, such a secretariat would likely need to be lighter and more technical
than the TTC’s ministerial-led format, anchored in the agency-to-agency relationships described
above.
Equally important is how the secretariat is framed. The TTC’s governance-first framing proved
vulnerable to the shift in political conditions that followed its creation. A secretariat framed instead as
serving the geopolitical competitiveness and national security interests that both blocs’ leadership
prioritizes would carry less political baggage across future administrations. Once established and
functioning, such a secretariat could pursue more-ambitious coordination goals, including reciprocal
pre-deployment access arrangements for AI systems entering high-risk sectors comparable to the
bilateral agreements that CAISI has developed with specific U.S. firms—closing the second gap
identified above.
Lithography Resilience
As discussed in the preceding analysis, ASML’s High-NA EUV monopoly makes it one of the
most consequential hardware chokepoints in the allied AI ecosystem, and its continued capacity to
innovate under increasing geopolitical pressure constitutes a shared strategic interest under every AI
trajectory. Frontier model development continues to demand the most-advanced chips, regardless of
whether AI value is concentrated in general-purpose or specialized models and regardless of whether
open-source alternatives remain competitive, which is why this effort, like the evaluation, testing, and
standards interoperability effort, scores high across all four scenarios in Table 5.1. Any disruption to
R&D continuity, supply chain integrity, or operational security of the allied lithography base would
constitute a crisis.
Meaningful cooperation already exists across both governmental and research layers of the
lithography supply chain. On the governmental side, the most substantive efforts have developed
bilaterally between the United States and the Netherlands, driven primarily by export control
alignment (Bermingham, 2026). On the research side, U.S. entities maintain active R&D partnerships
with Imec in Leuven, Belgium, which serves as ASML’s de facto external R&D ecosystem (Bologa,
2025; Imec, undated-b).
Three gaps are particularly consequential. The first is an R&D funding gap. Coordinated long-
horizon R&D funding commitments to sustain High-NA EUV innovation remain largely absent,
leaving ASML’s development pipeline exposed to the revenue pressure that tightening export
restrictions has begun to impose, particularly given that ASML relies heavily on its own revenue for
R&D (Kharpal, 2026; ASML, 2025a, pp. 32, 55; Norges Bank Investment Management, 2025,

conditions. In its absence, the distribution of ASML’s output during a supply crisis would be governed
solely by commercial interests. The third gap concerns the publicly documented architecture for cyber
and operational security. Cyber activity against ASML, such as the 2015 breach of its IT systems, may
become more frequent in the current competitive environment (ASML, 2025b). Beyond the EU
Chips Act’s monitoring and alert provisions, the open record does not surface an ASML-specific allied

nonpublic coordination cannot fully replicate.

Scenario-Contingent Priorities
The cooperation efforts in this section reward attention to the trajectory. Each becomes
consequential depending on which scenario materializes, as described in the “Conditional Logic”
subsection for each effort below. The policymaker or industry leader who monitors the signposts
identified in the preceding chapter will find in them a set of priorities that sharpen as the AI
development landscape clarifies.
Compute Access for the Broader EU Innovation Base
As discussed in the preceding analysis, sufficient sovereign compute is a meaningful enabler of EU
AI development, and its accessibility shapes which actors can participate in that development and at
what scale. This matters most under scenarios in which open-source models remain competitive, because it is
under those conditions that the binding constraint shifts from securing access to capable models, which open
weights largely resolve, to securing the compute needed to fine-tune and serve those models independently.
Substantial U.S. private capital is already reshaping EU compute infrastructure. Amazon’s almost
€34 billion Spanish cloud expansion exemplifies a much broader pattern of hyperscaler investment
that is materially narrowing the EU’s compute gap (Latona and Pinedo, 2026). Public infrastructure is

===== PDF_PAGE 45 =====
35
also expanding. Multiple EuroHPC JU AI gigafactories have come online and explicitly prioritize
SMEs and startup access (EuroHPC JU, 2025; EuroHPC JU, 2026).
Two gaps nonetheless remain consequential. The first is an allocation gap. Hyperscaler-anchored
capacity is often absorbed before it reaches the broader market; Start Campus’s SIN01 facility, though
technically constituting EU sovereign cloud, was fully leased to Microsoft before operations began
(Dunn, 2025). The pattern concentrates compute among large enterprise customers and underserves
SMEs, startups, and research institutions where specialized innovation also occurs. This gap operates
directly on the Capital à Compute interaction identified in Figure 2.1. Capital scarcity among smaller
EU actors prevents them from securing the compute required for participation in the AI ecosystem. It
also bears on the Compute à Talent interaction. Where compute access is scarce, researchers who
depend on it must follow it elsewhere. The second is a coverage gap. EuroHPC JU’s gigafactories may
not meet aggregate demand from the smaller actor base on their own, particularly as that demand
grows with the maturation of open-source models.
Closing these gaps could involve structuring U.S. private investment such that a portion of
hyperscaler capacity reaches the smaller actors that market allocation patterns currently underserve.
On allocation, one option would be for the U.S. government to use foreign policy vehicles—
including the American AI Exports Program—to incentivize hyperscalers to reserve a portion of their
EU compute allocation for SMEs, startups, and research institutions with high potential to produce
novel breakthroughs (American AI Exports Program, undated). Available tools include streamlined
export licensing for providers that meet access standards and preferential treatment in U.S. and EU
government procurement frameworks. Hyperscalers do not normally need an export license to run
cloud infrastructure serving foreign clients, but certain AI- and compute-related exports, such as
advanced chips and high-capability AI models, are subject to such controls, and these controls are
increasingly shaping the terms on which that infrastructure is provided (Heim, 2025a). That
regulatory surface is what makes foreign policy instruments a viable lever. It gives the U.S. government
meaningful incentives to offer without mandating private-sector behavior.
On security vetting, any access program would benefit from incorporating calibration to the EU
member state in which the recipient is established. Adversary technology exposure is unevenly
distributed across European geography, resulting in varying vulnerability profiles with implications for
access structure. China’s manufacturing presence in Hungary, China’s equity stake in Portugal’s
primary electric utility, and Huawei’s operation of AI research centers in Germany, for instance, may
each bear on trusted-partner determinations in those specific national contexts (O’Carroll and
Garamvolgyi, 2026; Bugge, 2011; Huawei Research Center Germany, undated). Preferential compute
access extended to a recipient in a sector with documented adversary-linked equity or infrastructure
generates supply chain risks that the program design would do well to anticipate. Conditioning access
on effective member state screening regimes or on recipient-level vetting could ensure that the access
program accounts for such variability.

Conditional Logic
The returns for such incentivization would be highest under trajectories in which open-source
models remain competitive, because it is under those conditions that compute access would become
the primary determinant of which actors can participate meaningfully in the AI ecosystem. Under the
Commoditized Frontier and Collaborative Specialization scenarios, EU member state firms and
institutions with sufficient sovereignty-compatible compute could leverage freely available models to
develop the high-risk applications where EU member states hold their strongest competitive
advantages and where the combination of open-source model access and sovereign compute would
create the most-favorable conditions for specialized value creation.
Under the Dependent Specialization scenario, compute access would retain meaningful but
secondary value. For most AI applications, long-distance cloud computing is technically sufficient, and
in-region sovereign compute would become a binding requirement only when regulations mandate or
encourage domestically controlled infrastructure. For instance, a U.S. firm that has successfully
navigated the EU AI Act’s transparency and review obligations may still find that a model or
application cannot be deployed on U.S.-hosted cloud infrastructure if the data involved are subject to
sovereignty requirements. In-region compute, therefore, would become the final enabling layer for
U.S. firms seeking to further monetize their EU regulatory compliance investments.
How consequential that layer proves under the Concentrated Dominance scenario depends on
how commercially significant the high-risk application developer base becomes relative to the broader
enterprise market. If regulated sectors come to represent a meaningful share of U.S. providers’ revenue
ambitions (and those are precisely the use cases where closed-source frontier models fall short),
sovereign compute access for smaller EU developers would become salient.
PET Joint Collaboration
Deploying AI in regulated sectors depends on technical mechanisms that make trustworthy
deployment demonstrable to regulators, partners, and public actors whose data such systems process.
PETs address that function by enabling AI development and deployment of sensitive data without
exposing the underlying information (OECD, undated-b). Their strategic salience is greatest under
proprietary model trajectories, where closed-source architectures draw sensitive data outward to externally
controlled infrastructure.

===== PDF_PAGE 47 =====
37
The centrality of PETs is not solely a matter of best practice; it is a legal condition of AI
deployment in some of the EU’s most valuable sectors. GDPR’s data minimization and purpose
limitation principles, combined with sectoral rules governing medical records, financial data, and
government personal information, create conditions under which sensitive data cannot be exposed to a
model during training or inference, regardless of what contractual or procedural safeguards are in place
(Intersoft Consulting, undated). Only technologies that enable computation on data without exposing
their underlying information permit lawful AI development in these domains.
Two gaps are consequential. First, despite the centrality of this technical layer, no publicly
available information points to a dedicated transatlantic cooperation pillar on PETs currently in place,
even as both the United States and the EU have expressed considerable interest in maturing these
technologies (NIST, undated; European Commission, 2023a). That such a pillar is achievable is
demonstrated by the U.S.-UK PETs prize challenges, which established that bilaterally structured,
regulatory-linked competition mechanisms can produce deployable, privacy-preserving AI solutions
(NIST, 2025). Second, the demand for PETs across these layers is clear, but the supply is not yet
commensurate. Fully homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computation, and general zero-
knowledge systems have improved substantially but often remain impractical for large-scale or low-
latency workloads (Wiewiórowski, 2025; Zewe, 2025; Oguro, undated). These maturity gaps are
precisely what a dedicated cooperation pillar could target.
Cooperation in this domain could therefore operate on the Data à Capital edge. Investors pricing
AI deals in regulated sectors tend to account for the legal uncertainty surrounding sensitive data use—
uncertainty that mature, standardized PETs would materially reduce.
For research coordination, one immediately actionable path would be a dedicated PETs
cooperation dialogue stream between NIST and the European AI Office. The resulting outputs could
provide the governing framework for jointly funded research into privacy-preserving fine-tuning and
secure inference, building on the U.S.-UK PETs prize challenge model that has already demonstrated
bilateral viability. The EU’s testing and experimentation facilities could offer the sectoral
infrastructure for validating jointly developed solutions in real-world deployment contexts, bridging
the gap between research output and regulator-validated implementations that can be deployed at
scale.
Such a dialogue would need to address multiple points in the AI supply chain, because privacy
constraints bind at each stage where sensitive data are processed. In data pipelines and training
workflows, PETs enable model development using datasets that cannot be legally exposed in raw
form. In model-serving infrastructure and secure compute services, they permit inference on protected
inputs without requiring data to leave controlled environments. In high-risk applications, they enable
deployment that meets both the EU AI Act and sectoral requirements. In assurance tooling, they help
demonstrate compliance to regulators by providing auditable, privacy-preserving records of system
behavior. A cooperation pillar that addresses only one layer would leave the others as points of failure,
causing deployment to stall regardless of progress elsewhere.
Private co-investment would be valuable to cover all these layers. U.S. cloud providers and AI
developers operating in regulated EU markets have a commercial interest in the maturation of PETs,
because demonstrating trustworthy AI deployment is increasingly subject to market access; their

===== PDF_PAGE 48 =====
38
participation as co-funders and implementation partners could accelerate the transition from research
investment to a deployable, scalable component of the stack.
Conditional Logic
Returns to PET investment would be highest under scenarios in which proprietary models
dominate, because it is under those conditions that the exposure surface that PETs are designed to
protect is at its widest.
Under the Concentrated Dominance and Dependent Specialization scenarios, closed-source
architectures would require that training and inference workloads execute on infrastructure controlled
by a small number of external providers. The “Abundant Data, Restricted Flow” section in Chapter 2
establishes that, for instance, clinical records are governed by GDPR and sectoral rules and cannot be
lawfully transmitted to such providers in raw form. PETs would supply the technical custody layer
that would permit regulated-sector deployment on proprietary models without surrendering control
of the underlying data. The narrower the pool of providers and the more concentrated the potential
points of exposure, the higher the stakes for that custody layer functioning reliably.
Compounding that concern, the opacity of closed-source models means that EU actors cannot
independently verify how their data are processed once they cross into provider-controlled
infrastructure, making cryptographic guarantees a substitute for the inspection that proprietary
licensing forecloses.
Under the Commoditized Frontier and Collaborative Specialization scenarios, the exposure
surface would somewhat contract. Open-weight models could be deployed on-premises, keeping
sensitive data within the boundaries that generate and govern them. Fine-tuning on proprietary
operational data could proceed within the developer’s own compute environment, mitigating cross-
boundary data flows that drive PETs’ centrality in proprietary model scenarios. PETs would retain
value under these trajectories through federated learning across institutions and cross-border
collaborations, which require techniques that on-premises deployment cannot provide. Nonetheless,
the absolute returns would be lower than under trajectories where data must leave the institution to
reach the model in the first place.
Sectoral Commercialization Partnerships
Deep sectoral expertise, proprietary operational data, and established regulatory sophistication
together constitute genuine competitive advantages for EU member states, but the financing and
distribution infrastructure that would systematically convert those advantages into globally scaled
products remains underdeveloped relative to U.S. counterparts. This matters most under trajectories
where specialized applications capture meaningful economic value, because it is under those conditions that
the commercialization bottleneck becomes a binding constraint on whether EU expertise generates increased
returns at scale.
As the “Distributed Excellence in AI Applications” section in Chapter 2 documents, transatlantic
commercialization partnerships for specialized AI already exist and have worked extraordinarily well.
Of the EU member state firms shown in Table 2.2, 15 of 16 maintain active U.S. partnerships
spanning investment, distribution, cloud infrastructure, and research collaboration. Mistral AI’s reach

===== PDF_PAGE 49 =====
39
into global markets through U.S. cloud distribution channels and funding, while retaining sovereignty
over sensitive deployments, illustrates this model at its most developed.
Two gaps nonetheless persist. The first is the financing gap. EU specialized AI firms consistently
lack access to growth and late-stage capital at the scale that would bring their products to global
markets. The second is the go-to-market resourcing gap. Beyond capital, smaller specialized firms may
lack the distribution relationships, cloud and platform integrations, and commercial infrastructure
needed to reach U.S. and global markets. The Mistral AI model has worked, but it has worked for
firms that are already large, visible, and well-resourced enough to negotiate these arrangements
independently; the broader population of EU specialized AI firms absorbs transaction costs that make
equivalent partnerships less accessible at smaller scales. Both gaps operate on the Capital àß Talent
interaction identified in Figure 2.1: The absence of scaling pathways drives talent outflow, which in
turn compresses the funder pipeline that shapes future investor confidence.
Supporting the broader offerings of the EU specialized AI ecosystem could be approached
through a single bilateral mechanism that performs two functions: pooling and coordinating U.S.
capital toward specialized EU firms and brokering their access to the broader commercialization
infrastructure required to compete globally.
On financing, a U.S.-EU Strategic Technology Investors Council (STIC), modeled loosely after
the U.S.-UK precedent, could function as a coordinated co-investment platform anchored by
participating U.S. venture firms and institutional investors. This council could be co-led by the U.S.
International Development Finance Corporation, the U.S. Department of Commerce, and their EIB
and EU counterparts (The American Presidency Project, 2024). The U.S.-EU STIC’s core functions
could include scouting EU firms with high-value prospects; facilitating AI-relevant funding and deal
flow across health care, manufacturing, robotics, and other strategic sectors; pre-vetting EU firms
against security and regulatory criteria; and structuring co-investment terms that lower the diligence
costs that U.S. investors may bear when entering EU markets. The objective would not be to subsidize
firms but to reduce the friction that has kept U.S. capital concentrated among the most visible
companies.
On go-to-market resourcing, the STIC could connect vetted EU firms with U.S. sovereign cloud
providers and AI platform operators, helping absorb some of the networking costs that currently make
such partnerships less accessible to smaller actors. The aim would be to replicate, for the broader
specialized AI firm population, the commercialization advantages that the largest EU firms have
secured independently by providing them with STIC endorsement. Such a setup could allow a more
politically digestible framing on both sides. On the U.S. side, a Department of Commerce–led council
is more readily positioned to secure U.S. access to high-value EU sectoral expertise. On the EU side,
viability depends on the STIC facilitating capital flows and commercialization infrastructure that EU-
native sources are more risk-averse to provide at scale, rather than displacing the native capital already
flowing.
On security vetting, participation would benefit from being calibrated to the EU member state in
which the recipient is established. As discussed in previous efforts, adversary technology exposure is
unevenly distributed across European geography, and the same calibration logic applies here.
Conditioning STIC participation on effective member state screening regimes or on vetting at the
recipient level could ensure that the program accounts for such variability.

A reasonable objection to this effort would be that systematically channeling U.S. capital and
distribution access toward EU firms further entrenches the dependency that EU sovereign AI
ambitions seek to reduce. The objection has merit: A STIC that succeeds in its core function will, by
default, deepen the integration of EU firms into U.S. capital and distribution networks. The
countervailing consideration is that the alternative is materially worse. More technology startups are
founded in Europe than in the United States, but they routinely fail, relocate, or fail to scale

Conditional Logic
The returns on such an effort would be highest in scenarios where specialized applications capture
meaningful economic value and more limited where general-purpose models come to erode the market
for domain-specific expertise.
Under the Dependent Specialization and Collaborative Specialization scenarios, the
commercialization bottleneck would be a binding constraint for EU specialized AI firms. What would
prevent EU expertise, data, and governance advantages from generating returns at scale would be the
structural lack of financing and distribution connections required to bring specialized products to
global markets. Closing that gap would be among the most consequential priorities for cooperation
under either trajectory.
Under the Commoditized Frontier scenario, the returns would be meaningful but secondary.
General-purpose models would dominate value creation, shifting competition primarily to the
deployment layer. More-mature EU firms would be better positioned to capture deployment-layer
value than those without it, but strengthening EU firms’ sovereign compute access and PET
infrastructure would be the more urgent cooperation priorities under this trajectory.
Under the Concentrated Dominance scenario, the logic would shift materially. If general-purpose
models can effectively replace most domain-specific ones, the addressable market for EU specialized
AI applications shrinks. Apart from comparative niches that will likely appear, returns would be
limited enough under this trajectory that they would not warrant displacing the foundational efforts.

The Window and Its Closure
The question the United States faces is not whether Europe will develop robust AI capabilities. It
is whether the United States will contribute to that development to strengthen itself and the Western
bloc or stand aside while Europe advances more slowly in relative isolation—a trajectory that serves
U.S. adversaries. The answer, however, depends fundamentally on how AI technology itself evolves.
Technological dominance without trust creates political pressure for independence. The EU’s
investments in governance, frontier models, and sectoral applications signal not merely a drive for
technological sovereignty but also responses to perceived technological subordination. The same
imbalances that provide the United States with leverage today are simultaneously driving allies toward
options that will erode that advantage tomorrow. The relevant unit of analysis is no longer the nation-
state but the technological ecosystem, and the United States must decide whether to cultivate a robust
global AI alliance or defend national advantages until circumstances determine whether those
advantages remain strategically valuable or become hollow.
The asymmetric nature of current U.S. and EU member state AI capacity creates a window of
opportunity to structure cooperation on favorable terms. EU member states’ reliance on U.S.
technological infrastructure provides leverage that can be operationalized to benefit both parties while
ensuring continued democratic leadership in AI. The timeline for that window’s closure is uncertain.
EU structural constraints are deep and slow-moving and Chinese alternatives remain incomplete, but
the EU’s direction toward sovereign AI ecosystems is accelerating. As EU member state capacity
matures and alternative providers continue to emerge, the terms available to the United States for
structuring engagements will narrow incrementally. Most importantly, the stakes extend beyond
bilateral cooperation to the fundamental architecture of global AI development. In an era of
competition with authoritarian powers, democratic nations may not be able to afford fragmented AI
ecosystems that dissipate collective advantages.
The United States stands at an inflection point, where decisions about transatlantic AI
partnership will shape the AI development landscape for decades. Success requires abandoning the
comfortable fiction that transatlantic AI cooperation is a diplomatic courtesy extended to traditional
allies. Success equally requires avoiding the assumption that partnership is uniformly beneficial
regardless of technological developments. Both errors are, at root, failures of political ambition—the
first is too modest, the second is insufficiently disciplined—and Gray’s 2019 reminder that the choice
of ends is the most consequential decision in strategic planning applies with full force today to the
transatlantic AI relationship. The framework presented in this report is designed to navigate between
these errors: pursue foundational efforts whose returns are scenario-invariant now and build
institutional readiness for the scenario-contingent efforts whose value depends on which trajectory
materializes while monitoring the signposts that indicate when to scale engagement or hold it at its
current level.

===== PDF_PAGE 52 =====
42
Such is my argument for conditional preparedness—the deliberate construction of an architecture
that allows the alliance to capture value from cooperation along whichever trajectory arrives, without
overcommitting along those trajectories that do not. The price of that preparedness is manageable; the
cost of discovering belatedly that the architecture was never built is not.
