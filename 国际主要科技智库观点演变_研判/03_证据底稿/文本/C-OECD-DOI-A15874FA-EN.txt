Abstract
Policymakers aiming to foster and govern emerging technologies may wish
to develop anticipatory capacities and strategic intelligence to inform and
future-proof their efforts. This report delves into how these approaches are
being mobilised, using advanced materials as a policy-relevant case given
their regular inclusion in critical emerging technology lists and the rise of
national strategies to promote their research to meet strategic goals. A
series of case studies from across the globe are brought together to
illustrate practical applications in this field of anticipatory coordination
efforts, upstream governance and “by-design” approaches, and strategic
intelligence tools. Insights from this report aim to support policymakers and
technology assessment practitioners seeking to develop or update national
strategies for emerging technologies like advanced materials to accelerate
innovation and further develop national ecosystems.

Executive Summary
Advanced materi als (also referred to as “novel materials” , “value-added materials”  or “materials
innovation”) are regularly listed as a critical emerging technology , encompassing highly diverse
applications across multiple sectors – from healthcare to defence to chemical  manufacturing. Whilst this
varied nature means there is no global standard definition , the OECD Working Party for Manufactured
Nanomaterials (WPMN) has developed a working description of materials designed with new or enhanced
properties, or targeted stru ctural features to achieve specific or improved performance. These attributes
make them essential to foster innovation in many sectors, drive economic growth and help tackle
societies’ grand global challenges.
Recognising the current role and future poten tial of advanced materials to pursue strategic goals,
governments are  increasingly investing in policy tools to support their development and
implementation, most notably via dedicated national strategies . In constructing these, governments
gain a unique o pportunity to map out the sector’s state of play (e.g. national capacities), understand key
associated factors (e.g. socioeconomic impacts, global landscape ), and identify the ir country’s unique
challenges and opportunities. Strategies can then set a desir able vision for the future of the national
advanced materials ecosystem around which actors can coalesce in pursuit of strategic goals.
While different policy drivers will shape these efforts depending on the national context, this report
identifies a number of  common themes. For example, advanced materials are regularly identified as
essential elements to ensure resilience, particularly with a view to issues around  access to critical
resources to support e conomic and national security  – in the face of unreliable supply chains and new
trade barriers . Advanced materials are a key area driving innovation in the defence sector.  Advanced
materials are a powerful engine for economic growth  and employment across sectors : many countries
set out strategies with the explicit aim of securing or maintaining global leadership in the space to provide
a competitive advantage in global markets, foster technology security and allow them to shape the
technology in their desired direction. They will also be foundational in the digital transformation of advanced
manufacturing industries. They are also considered to be key enabler for the green transition , and
concurrently to adapt to new international trade rules setting sustainability standards.
The development of these national strategies mobilises a number of anticipatory processes, some
of which are illustrated in this report through a series of case studies from across the globe, including some
deeper dives. This analysis is divided into three sections.
The first focuses on anticipatory coordination efforts, which aim to use strategic intelligence producing
processes as a way to bring together the various stakeholders from across the value chain to coordinate
a path to a functioning new innovation ecosystem. It includes activities such as roadmapping, participatory
foresight and collective agenda building.
The second focuses on upstream governance and “by -design” approaches, which aim for proactive
identification of information gaps, concerns, and regulatory needs early on, and thus lay the groundwork
for research, guidelines, testing systems, and legislative action to mitigate or avert potential negative

The third relates the use of ‘strategic intelligence’: the analysis and knowledge of possible directions and

development. Future work could assess the quality and outcomes of both the anticipatory processes and

strategic intelligence that the national strategies can draw upon.

The unique properties and rapid innovation of advanced materials hold great promise to build safer, more
sustainable and higher performing products for more resilient soci eties and to tackle some of the critical
crises of our times. For example, new photovoltaic materials and batteries could help further harness the
potential of renewable energy, and bio -based products could diminish the need for fossil fuel feedstocks
and usher in a circular economy. Beyond this, advanced materials could usher in innovations in sectors as
diverse as aerospace, food packaging and soft robotics (ChemistryNL, 2021[1]).
Whilst advanced materials are increasingly seen as a critical area of emerging technologies, t here is no
global standard definition of advanced materials, and national strategies on the subject often use broader
terms such as “materials innovation”, “novel materials” or “value -added ma terials”. Agreeing with the
OECD Working Party for Manufactured Nanomaterials (WPMN) the term “advanced materials” is often
used broadly and in different contexts, the Steering Group mobilised the “working definition”1 of Advanced
Materials by the WPMN, to provide clarification in terms of scope of this project activity:
Advanced materials are understood as materials that are rationally designed to have (i) new or enhanced
properties, and/or (ii) targeted or enhanced structural features with the objective to achieve specific or improved
functional performance. This includes both new emerging manufactured materials (high tech materials), and
materials that are manufactured from traditional materials (low tech materials). This also includes materials
from innovative manufacturing processes that enable the creation of targeted structures from starting materials,
such as bottom-up approaches. It is acknowledged that what are currently considered as advanced materials
will change with time.
The report has been built on three “data rich” sections:
1. National advanced material strategies  – This section analyses advanced material strategy
documents (or advanced material related areas like advanced manufacturing) to articulate the
underlying policy drivers embedded in these strategies.
2. Anticipatory processes that inform, or enable, national strategies  – This section looks at
activities and processes that facilitate the building of new advanced material value chains  and
innovation ecosystems (through examples of roadma pping activities, coordination programmes
and anticipatory actions in research and innovation cluster activities) as well as lead to good
governance of early -stage emerging advanced materials (through upstream governance and “by
design” approaches like anticipatory Life Cycle Assessments and Safe and Sustainable by Design.
3. Strategic intelligence roles and capacities – This section makes explicit the strategic intelligence
tools and approaches mobilised in the anticipatory processes mentioned above to identify the role
and application of strategic intelligence approaches, and  articulate the capacities needed for
contemporary advanced material strategy development.
These are supplemented by three Annexes comprising the full list of advanced materials -related policies
identified, the guiding questions to support experts in case study development, and the case studies
provided by Steering Group participants.

Introduction
Demand for advanced materials is growing and is expected to continue doing so in the future, in particular
for technological solutions in renewable energy, semiconductors, medicines and defense applications
(European Commission, 202 4[2]). However, traditionally, advanced material innovation has a  relatively
long development timeframe – 10 to 20 years from inception to market launch – meaning that solutions
may be much delayed and may be unable to meet current urgent demand. This situation requires policy
investments and  renewed efforts  to accelerate the translation from research laboratories to market,
including the active support of emerging supply and value chains, as well as the accompanying framing
conditions (technical stan dards and other forms of governance)  (AMI2030, 2023 [3]). This issue is
increasing in importance for governments and is reflected in advanced materials being included in several
national critical technology lists and dedicated national strategies. Given differing national contexts and
priorities, each country has its own constellation of drivers for this push, but there ar e common themes in
both the goals they pursue and how they aim to achieve them.
Governments may establish a desirable vision of the future , around which actors can coalesce  in
pursuit of strategic societal goals (e.g. maintaining competitive global leadership, economic growth, drive
to net zero carbon emissions), addressing crises (e.g. loss of biodiversity), strengthening sectors (e.g.
defense and national security) or guiding  development and deployment of  critical technologies (e.g.
artificial intelligence, biotechnology).
The process of constructing a national vision and associated strategy allows nations to map out the state
of play for sectors, technologies or challenges. Understanding key factors like current national capacities,
socioeconomic impacts, and the global competitive landscape, can tease out the areas of highest potential
impact for a country as well as its key assets. At the same time, this mapping can be used to identify the
challenges in achieving the desired vision , which could includ e dependence on other countries for
resources, gaps in national infrastructure, barriers to scale-up and commercialization – to name a few.

countries – particularly those with vibrant advanced materials ecosystems like Japan and the United States
– governments have dedicated national strategies or roadmaps for promoting advanced materials,
identifying ambitious goals and initiatives to achieve them. Other countries still recognize the strategic and
scientific importance of the sector, but instead of developing specialized  plans they ha ve included

Policy Driver 1 – Ensuring resilient resource flows for national security. As geopolitical uncertainty
increases, unreliable international supply chains combined with new trade barriers are challenging access
to critical raw materials concentrated in limited sources. This access is not just important from an economic
competitiveness perspective, but can also be key for national security, as some assets are becoming
increasingly essential to  power and protect national socio-economic activities – for example, vital h ealth
resources like medicines or technological components like semiconductors. There is an increasing need
to secure vital resources  and explore which advanced materials could support strategic autonomy. For
example, advanced materials can improve durability and recyclability, help develop processes that utilize
more readily available alternatives, or even outright replace some of these critical raw resources (AMI2030,
2023[3]).
A sub-element of this policy driver is advanced materials’ key role for innovation in the defence sector,
due to dual -use applications.  Advanced materials can  be stronger, more durable and lighter than
conventional materials (which can improve resistance and fuel efficiency), w hilst new properties like
temperature resistance, self -repair or stealth capabilities can provide revolutionary applications (Burnett
et al., 2018 [4]). Advanced materials are also essential for increasingly critical sectors like space
technologies and satellite communications (MaterialsNL, 2021[5]).
Policy Driver 2 – Powering economic growth and valuable innovations . Scientific and technological
innovation serve as a key economic motor for countries, creating jobs in research and manufacturing and
generating revenue. For example, in the U nited Kingdom in 2023, the materials-related manufacture and
export generated £430 billion in revenue, employed over 2.6 million people and accounted for 49% of all
UK exports (Henry Royce Institute, 2024 [6]). Such innovations also represent a long -term investment into

society, as materials discoveries made last century are still providing revenue today. Furthermore, the
pervasive nature of materials means they can impact practically all sectors: beyond the aforementioned
sustainability and defence fields, m aterials innovation can support advancements in healthcare,
agriculture, construction and electronics, amongst others. For instance, smart materials could act as
biosensors for diagnostics or 3D print organs and body parts, membranes to purify water could increase
access to clean sources (MaterialsNL, 2021[5]).
Policy Driver 3 – Securing or maintaining competitive advantage  on global markets . As more
countries recognize the importance of advanced materials for sustainability, economic competitiveness
and national security , and invest accordingly, the global landscape of innovation is shifting. Emerging
countries in particular are driving this change, supported by factors like cheaper labour and resource costs
and the advent of digitalisation. Historical ly-leading nations  in advanced materials research  – such as
Japan, Germany, the United Kingdom and the United States – are facing growing competition, exacerbated
by the commodification of products (involving faster spread, shorte r lifecycles and more competitive
pricing) whilst concurrently requiring more complex products at a time of challenging access to resources
due to unstable supply chains (Council for Integrated Innovation Strategy, Cabinet Office, Go vernment of
Japan, 2021[7]). The desire to maintain or obtaining technological hegemony is a strong driver, as not
only does it provide countries a competitive economic edge in global markets, but it also fosters technology
security by avoiding dependence on others. In addition, being at the tech frontier allows them to shape
technology development according to their values and wield influence in international discussions to shape
the future of the field.
Policy Diver 4 – Enabling advanced manufacturing and convergence with AI and data. Manufacturing
is undergoing a revolution, underpinned by  new opportunities provided by data analytics and artificial
intelligence. This is in response to increasingly complex supply chains calling for multidisciplinary
integration and customization of products and processes , as well as to lower labour costs and  meet high
demand rates. These developments are reshaping the global manufacturing landscape and enabling novel
countries to participate in new ways. Scientific and technological advancements are identified as key to
competitiveness in this changing space,  and although many enablers will help foster this transition
(including but not limited to sensors, robotics, 3D printing and augmented reality), developments in
advanced materials are identified as a key pillar. At the moment, their unique properties are used to help
tackle product-specific issues, such as weight or durability, but in the future they could be incorporated as
early as the design stage to speed up the development process and provide more foundational solutions
(CSIRO, 2016[8]).
Policy Driver 5 – Supporting the sustainability transition to tackle the climate  crisis. Developing
more circular and sustainable societies – to tackle the climate crisis, increased pollution and loss of
biodiversity – is an urgent task for cing practically all sectors to evolve. Advanced materials hold promise
in supporting this transition by providing novel technological solutions. For example, they can increase a
product’s durability or their recyclability for a more circular economy; be used to produce renewable energy
to replace fossil fuels and reduce carbon dioxide emissions; and catalyse processes for increased
efficiency or reduction of waste. Recognising this potential, the German  Federal Ministry of Research,
Technology and Space  has established “Mat2Twin”  - the largest German funding program for material
sciences at national level  - to foster  research on materials for the green and digital transformations,
including the creation of more closed-loop systems and recycling of high-value resources (Federal Ministry
for Economic Cooperation and Development, 2025 [9]). Ho wever, advanced materials can also lead to
environmental harm and thus their own safety and sustainability should be ensured throughout their life
cycle to maximise the opportunities provided by this technology. The German Environment Agency, for
example, advocates for following the precautionary principle and identifying concerns for sustainability
(e.g. toxicity, increased ecological footprint) at an early stage to close knowledge gaps early and re-design
when necessary (Schwirn and Völker, 2023[10]).

A sub-driver relates to new trade sustainability rules. As countries around the world implement policies
to drive sustainability, it may be the case that other nations wanting to remain competitive in the
international trade space will have to ensure their exports meet sustainability standards to maintain access
to current markets (Council for Integrated Innovation Strategy, Cabinet Office, Government of Japan,
2021[7]). A prime example is the European Union’s Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism , which aims to
ensure the price of carbon in imports is accounted for and equalled to that of domestic products to prevent

Adequately responding to these drivers to achieve the underlying aim – fostering a strong advanced
materials innovation and manufacturing space  – requires action from a broad range of stakeholders ,
from academics pursuing fundamental research to industry deploying practical solutions. Each of these
players have different capabilities to act and do so in response to unique incentives. For example,
academics may decide to pursue further research due to new funding opportunities, whilst industry players
may invest more in expectation of greater return on investments. Governments, and associated public
agencies, can play a role in translating the se trends and drivers into concrete policies and
incentives to foster concerted action by different stakeholders.

Roadmapping and collective agenda building activities
Introduction
Anticipatory coordination is a term that encompasses all activities and fora designed to bring together the
various stakeholders need ed to power and drive advanced material value chains and innovation
ecosystems.  Acting together, either through direct collaboration or in full knowledge of each other’s
strategies, ecosystem actors coordinated to achieve a path to a functioning new innovation ecosystem or
value chain.
The most well-known anticipatory coordination mechanism is the International Technology Roadmap for
Semiconductors3, that set the pace for development an d performance improvement in the diverse
ecosystem of specialized component and process manufactures  – this coordinates the diverse members
of the ecosystem and sets the collective pacing of these activities .  By creating a regular roadmap, the
pace of cha nge is agreed to and suppliers can better align their development strategies with the overall
vision and milestones outlined in the roadmap. The roadmapping process, therefore, not only collectively
envisions the future  status of the semiconductors innovat ion ecosystem (i.e. all of the different
component developers and their supply chains) but also sets the pace for the innovation chain ,
collectively agreeing when certain new developments should be in place.
For advanced materials, the innovation ecosystem is much more fragmented and distributed – given that
advanced materials are both diverse in their nature and are themselves enabling technologies, connecting
to a variety of value chains and sectors.
Strategic intelligence production processes for anticipatory coordination can come in a variety of forms,
such as vision building exercises, participatory foresight and roadmapping activities, to name a few.
Selected country examples on anticipatory coordination processes
United Kingdom’s National Materials Innovation Strategy
Facilitated by the Royce Institute, the Strategy outlines  the vision of the UK’s world-leading advanced
materials ecosystem driving economic growth and tackling grand challenges . Since t he materials
sector is crucial for the UK's manufacturing output, (contributing significantly to employment, exports, and
R&D investments), the Strategy calls for substantially enhancing the value of UK -based industries in the
rapidly expanding multi-billion-pound materials markets. Recognizing as well that advanced materials are
integral to addressing societal and technological challenges like achieving sustainability goals, the strategy
also emphasizes the necessity of materials innovation in achieving a prosperous, net-zero economy.

Portugal’s Industry & Manufacturing Agenda 2030
This forward-looking exercise set out to evaluate the potential short - and long-term impacts of emerging
technologies on the Portuguese industry, including advanced materials as one of the five priority domains
selected by the experts engaged. It was orchestrated to create strategic intelligence to (a) identify the key
target areas for the Portuguese manufacturing ecosystem and (b) mobilise and build cohesion across
the ecosystem in advance. Experts did so by determining the key challenges to national innovation (e.g.
incorporating new knowledge and technological skills into firms , integrating Industry 4.0 and circular
economy concepts) and the g lobal trends shaping the national industry (e.g. climate change, resource

Upstream governance and “by design” approaches
Introduction
Developing novel advanced materials brings uncertainties about their safety, sustainability, and t he
adequacy of existing regulations. Early detection of these issues is essential for regulators and innovators
to prepare for potential actions. Such a proactive approach helps identify information gaps, concerns,
and regulatory needs early on, laying the  groundwork for relevant research, guideline development,
appropriate testing systems, and legislative updates to mitigate or avert potential negative impacts . This
could help prevent uncertainties in risk assessments from resulting in unsafe products being released into
the market, potentially leading to public backlash, reduced industrial investment hampering innovation, or
the need to urgently replace harmful substances.

Effective innovation policies, in addition to catalyzing and driving no vel advanced material development,
can ensure robust governance approaches that keep the regulation of potential risks at pace with
innovation. The timely identification of potential health, environmental, and sustainability impacts is not
only necessary t o comply with policies and strategic visions to foster the green transition but can also
enhance the innovation process by making it more responsive to the reality and needs  outside of the lab.
Anticipatory capacities and agile and adaptive governance  are key, as outlined in the recent OECD
Framework for Anticipatory Governance of Emerging Technologies , as forward-looking approaches can
provide a means of assessing existing risk governance approaches in a situation of paucity of data.
Emerging risks include new risks (stemming from novel materials or technologies) or familiar risks that are
becoming apparent in new or unfamiliar conditions. Such risks are highly uncertain and often have limited
evidence on which to base assessments. At the same time, i t involves not only the anticipation of
potential risks and benefits, but also the exploration how these are perceived and framed by
different stakeholders. Such framing is dependent on the values of those perceiving and thus unpacking
what are the underlying norms and values around an emerging technology and its possible impact is key.
One example is the International Risk Go vernance Council (IRGC) guidelines 10 for emerging risk
governance: a set of flexible guidelines that support public and private organisations in designing internal
processes to proactively deal with emerging and evolving risks. 11 Another challenge observed is that
current regulations may not adequately address the specific challenges posed by advanced materials,
requiring updated and agile regulatory frameworks founded on anticipatory governance.
Selected examples for anticipatory governance of advanced materials
European Commission: Safe and Sustainable by Design framework
Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) principles are increasingly being adopted by innovators aiming to improve
their sustainability, as these encourage consideration of environmental and social impacts throughout a
product’s life cycle, beyond the design and production stages. As uptake of these tools increases and they
become more sophisticated, specialised methods are arising, such as Environmental Life Cycle
Assessments (E-LCA) that measure the extraction, consumption and release of resources  like energy,
water and gases, as well as Social Life Cycle Assessments (S-LCA) that measure positive and negative
social impacts on affected stakeholders. These quantitative approaches to measuring impact provide
innovators additional insights that can inform design choices for new products or identify “hotspots” in a
life cycle which can be addressed for better environmental impact (KPMG, 2023[11]).
LCA can be complemented by following a  Safe and Sustainable by Design (SSbD)  approach, which
aims to ensure that new materials are not only high -performing but also sustainable and safe throughout
their life cycle. This involves integrating safety, environmental impact, and circularity considerations – such
as those gleaned from LCAs – from the early stages of material design and development  into production
and end-of-life. Specific focus areas include designing circularity in polymers and elastomers, developing
biodegradable and recyclable materials, and creating materials that reduce reliance on toxic or scarce raw
materials.
A practical example is the European Commission’s 2022 safe and sustainable by design (SSbD )
framework, which provides companies and member state authorities practical guidance on the design and
assessment of chemicals and materials . It evaluates the material’s hazardous properties  and the impact
on human health and the environment in the production , use and end-of-life phases. These insights can
be used to develop novel products or redesign existing ones  with improved safety and sustainability at all
or key stages. The framework is currently being tested by innovators, but is aiming to foster innovation,
alternatives to substances of concern and become a global reference (European Commission: Directorate-
General for Research and Innovation, 2022[12]).

OECD: Early4AdMa early warning system
Equally necessary for innovations to reach the market is regulators’ ability to effectively evaluate their novel
properties. This can be challenging as regulatory knowle dge and risk governance tools, given their need
to be extensively validated, may struggle to keep pace with innovation. The concept of Regulatory
Preparedness (RP) calls to improve regulator’s capacity to anticipate technological developments and
have the knowledge and tools for effective risk assessment. This could involve information sharing
between innovators and regulators from early in the design process, under trusted environments
(confidential conditions that protect commercial interests). (Soeteman-Hernández et al., 2019[13])
The concepts of Safe and Sustainable  by Design and Regulatory Preparedness  have been brought
together to form the Safe(r) and Sustainable Innovation Approach (SSIA) , whose aim is to reduce the
time between a technological innovation emerging and the development of suitable risk assessment tools
and frameworks. This concept has been operationalised in the OECD’s Early4AdMa 12 system. Existing
screening methods for nove l materials primarily focus on safety and often neglect sustainability but the
unique properties of advanced materials necessitate distinct safety and sustainability assessments
compared to general chemicals. Recognising that there is a need for a screening approach that addresses
this in a fast -evolving technological field, the OECD Working Party for Manufactured Nanomaterials
developed an anticipatory screening approach labelled the Early4AdMa system as an agile and adaptive
approach to identify both safety and sustainability concerns and to inform governance approaches. 13

Advanced materials offer many technological solutions but may also pose unforeseen risks towards the
environment or human health. Anticipating these issues can help ensure safety and sustainability early
in the innovation process and foster innovation. Towards this goal, a new early warning system
(Early4AdMa) has been developed by the OECD Working Party for Manufactured Nanomaterials

issues in a timely manner, promoting Safe and Sustainable by Design (SSbD) principles.
Early4AdMa’s methodology is divided into two tiers, each with several steps. Tier 1 starts with 1)
scanning the field of innovative advanced materials, selecting a set of materials for further research, 2)

sustainability concerns and regulatory applicability  and based on the resulting score, 3) a
preliminary warning is developed explaining any potential concerns.
Tier 2 continues with 4) collecting additional information, based on the preliminary warning and to feed
into 5) scoring by a multidisciplinary and multistakeholder group of experts  on four major topics
(safety assessments for human health and for the environment, regulatory applicability and
sustainability). Each topic is broken down into sub -questions and the uncertainty is included in
considerations. This is followed by 6) an assessment and prioritisat ion of the scoring results,
determining follow -up actions  (e.g. generate additional safety data to reduce uncertainty, share
insights with regulatory agencies, ensure recyclability…) which are then communicated to decision
makers. After enough time, experts would be asked to 7) reflect and evaluate the impact of the warning
and the actions proposed.

What is strategic intelligence?
“Strategic intelligence” on the potential developments and implications of new and emerging technologies
is a critical resource for building national strategies for emerging technology fields. Strategic intelligence
refers to analysis and knowledge of the possible directions and economic stakes of its development, levels
of societal support, possible ethical and societal aspects that may need to be considered, and potential
impacts, benefits and risks. Methods include foresight, technology assessment, forecasting, and a number
of other tools (a first introduction to these tools can be found in (Robinson and Doherty, 2025[16])).
For national strategies on critical and emerging technologies, a number of strategic in telligence tools and
approaches are mobilised.  For advanced materials, the case studies included in this report incorporate
different mixes of tools and approaches. The different approaches include:
• Horizon scanning:  the search for weak signals of disruptive technologies (or other areas of
interest) that may become impactful, or areas of concern, in the near future. With the speed of
technological innovation increasing, the need for regular and robust horizon scanning is becoming
increasingly important.
• Text mining: advances in both computation (speed and complexity), the availability of organised
digital data sets that can be mined (scientific articles, patents, investment databases, to name a
few) and the increasing number of policy-related data analysts, means that big data analytics can
provide historical and near-real-time evidence of emerging technologies that can be mobilised for
“near casting”.15 Text mining is a relatively mature example of big data analytics.
• Technology Forecasting:  involves identifying trends in technology developments through
mobilising multiple sources, and in some cases using modelling.
• Foresight: mobilising multiple techniques to imagine, in a systematic way, alternative futures,
foresight draws on diverse and multidisciplinary perspectives to ensure robust and resilient
assumptions about the future, helping to “future proof” governance of emerging technologies ,
underlying policy strategies and approaches. Foresight helps policymakers identify blind spots and
potential future vulnerabilities with existing strategies as well as interconnections and opportunities
for synergies between different actors or domains.
• Vision building (towards a roadmap): collective articulation of a vision to pursue is a key part of
building national strategies. Identifying the shared expectations from key actors in the emerging
technology domain (those that will develop, grow and deploy technologies) are translated into an
agenda of action – embodied in a roadmap.

• Expert-driven technology assessment: involving deep-dive analysis and structured engagement

Canada – PRIMA Quebec
This case combines a variety of forms of strategic intelligence, text mining, horizon scanning, technology
forecasting, expert-driven technology assessment, and a vision building exercise, elaborated below.
PRIMA Quebec undertook a vision building exercise to develop an “Advanced Materials 2035 Roadmap”.
The activity mobilised key actors in the advanced materials ecosyste m with a view to contributing to the
decarbonization of the economy; further develop industry-research relationships to position Quebec in the
marketplace, and better position the advanced materials sector as a pillar of Quebec's decarbonization
objectives. The creation of the Roadmap 2035 allowed the collective envisioning of scalable solutions to
the challenges surrounding the energy transition, the improvement of competitiveness and the security of
supply chains. It was built in four key phases: 1) updat es on global trends and ecosystem (via studies,
surveys and interviews); 2) identification of strategic sectors and themes; 3) Vision 2035 workshop and
preliminary definition of guidelines; 4) Co -drafting of objectives and actions (via working groups). It is
comprised of 7 broad guidelines (e.g. promote SME growth, develop skilled workers) each broken down
into more detailed objectives.  This activity was supported by technology forecasting and horizon scanning
to identify international trends and weak signals of what may be coming that may influence demand
for advanced materials, which trends have been observed and retained in the 2018 report updates.
Reported in the publication “Advanced Materials: A Strategic Sector for Quebec” report published in 2018,
this technology forecasting and horizon scanning was mobilised to gain a better understanding of the
industrial advanced materials ecosystem in the province of Quebec, factors that may be taken into
consideration when envisioning its evolution, and to posi tion advanced materials as a strategic sector
essential to other industries.
In this process, the first step was to identify a series of international trends likely to strengthen and stimulate
demand for advanced materials, followed by a series of needs a nd end markets for each trend. Next, an
analysis of local demand was conducted via a survey of industry associations and research consortia
actively working with an industrial clientele. Although by no means exhaustive, the analysis offered an
initial overview of local perceptions with regard to key advanced materials, their impact and barriers to
demand.
Expert-driven t echnology assessment activities were conducted. For example,  in 2018, industry
(including SMEs), government and academic stakeholders were engaged to identify the needs of industries
and determine Quebec’s competitive advantages, a questionnaire was sent to the 340 companies in the
database, with questions about their advanced materials activities, company size and potential (innovation,
exports, investment, growth), and whether they faced any barriers to their development. The approach
adopted helped create an initial statistical portrait of the types of industrial expertise in Quebec,
characterized by either the nature of the advanced materials involved, or the sectors of application served
by the businesses in Quebec’s industry. This activity also explored three major societal challenges for
Quebec: 1) Complete the energy transition and decarbonize industry to achieve net zero by 2050 throug h
collaboration with the innovation zones; 2) Increase productivity with a digital transition to help maintain
and improve our standard of living; 3) Secure the country’s strategic supply chains by reducing its
dependence on foreign minerals, materials, co mponents, and services needed to fuel the energy and
digital transitions.
A key element of this activity included  text mining:  bibliometric analysis (2019, 2024) of scientific
publications and patent requests for research and innovation in advanced materials in Quebec between
2003-2020. This provided key insights into the broad developments of advanced materials R&D over the
previous five years. In particular, it identified the main and emerging research themes, as well as to what

Advanced materials, with their unique properties and performance advantages, promise to drive economic
growth and help tackle societies’ grand global challenges. For example, materials innovation can be used
to build more sustainable and higher -performing products that reduce waste and carbon intensity to
support the green transition. Countries, increasingly recognising them as a critical technology, are seeking
to deliver the policy investment needed to accelerate their development and implementation.
This report analysed the current policy practices in contemporary advanced materials strategy
development and their strategic intelligence capabilities . It first landscapes the national approaches
pursued by different countries to foster advanced materials research and development, as well as the

What was not analysed in this report was the quality of the anticipatory processes and their outcomes, nor
the quality of the strategic intelligen ce tools and outcomes that were part of these exercises.  Moreover,
whilst anecdotal evidence was provided on who undertook the strategic intelligence activities, it was not

strategies drew upon.  These elements where beyond the scope of this study, but would be worthwhile for
deeper investigation in a broader analysis of the role of strategic intelligence for critical and emerging

1. Canada: Advanced Materials as a Strategic Sector for Quebec
Case study authors: Tyler Norsten (National Research Council Canada, Emerging Technologies) and Marie-
Pierre Ippersiel (PRIMA Quebec)
Introduction
Quebec is Canada's largest province by area with a population of ~8.6 million, it comprises ~ 21% of
Canada’s population. Manufacturing has and continues to be an important economic sector for Quebec
particularly in aerospace, mines & metallurgy, automotive, and technology industries. Advanced materials
adoption and integration into the manufacturing sector is pivotal in this context as advanced materials have
the ability to impart superior physical and function properties relative to the conventional materials they
replace. However, the integration of advanced materials into existing manufacturing lines and processes
is often challenging due to the inherent differences in the materials properties and processing.
Furthermore, many traditional manufacturing sectors are unaware of advanc ed materials and the related
manufacturing processes that are emerging. Recognizing the importance of advanced materials integration
into the manufacturing sector, along with actors of the ecosystem, the province of Quebec created a non-
profit organization in 2014 called PRIMA Quebec with a mission to support and facilitate the advanced
materials ecosystem by promoting collaborative innovation in the province’s materials and manufacturing
sector to ensure that Quebec remains at the forefront of technologica l advancements, market demands
and sustainable economic development.
Technology Scope: Advanced Material Specific? Or a broader strategy that includes
Advanced Materials
The "Advanced Materials: A Strategic Sector for Quebec"1 document published in 2018 by PRIMA Quebec
outlines Quebec's strategic focus on developing and promoting advanced materials as a key economic
driver. It emphasizes the province's strengths in research and innovation, its robust industrial base, and
the collaborative efforts between academia, industry, and government. It was an essential tool for an initial
categorizing and mapping of the ecosystem with an industry focus while improving the sector’s market
position and facilitating the implementation of public policies to better support advanced materials.
The document highlighted the potential for advanced materials to revolutionize various sectors, including
aerospace, health, and energy, through enhanced performance and sustainability of advanced materials.
It also underscored the imp ortance of investing in talent, infrastructure, and international partnerships to
maintain Quebec's competitive edge and foster economic growth.

2. Japan: Materials Innovation Strategy
Case study authors: Asako Okamura (NISTEP, MEXT) and Ayari Takamura (CRDS, JST)
Background
The Materials Innovation Strategy1 was approved by the Council for Integrated Innovation Strategy at the
Cabinet Office in April 2021 as one of the sectoral strategies in line with the 6th Science, Technology, and
Innovation Basic Plan 2. Other sectoral strategies are formulated for the areas of artificial intelligence ,
biotechnology, quantum technology, fusion technology, health and medicine, space, ocean, and food,
agriculture, forestry, and fisheries.
Under the Science, Technology, and Innovation Basic Law , the government is required to formulate the
Science, Technology, and Innovation (STI) Basic Plan  (for which the current 6 th Plan covers the period
from 2020 to 2025) . The STI Basic Plan requires that the government to prepare an annual Integrated
Innovation Strategy3. This Integrated Innovation Strategy is developed aligned with the sectoral strategies.
The Integrated Innovation Strategy Secretariat leads the formulation and implementation of the sectoral
strategies through cross-ministerial efforts.
Declining Japan’s Competencies of the Material Industry and Science Base
With limited natural resources, Japan has had a strong industrial competitiveness in manufacturing,
supported by a solid foundation of leading materials science. The strength in material science is the basis
for its technology development and production capabilities, covering a wide range of products. These
include general-purpose products, specialty function products, and functional materials. For years, Japan
has held a significant share of the world market in these areas.
The country's strong scientific base in materials science has also led to groundbreaking discoveries. Two
notable examples are blue light -emitting diodes and lithium -ion batteries. The developers of both these
innovations were awarded Nobel Prizes, further highlighting Japan's significant contributions to the field of
materials science.
In recent years, however, Japan has faced challenges in the materials industry. Its production share of
some materials has declined due to several factors , including increased research and development in
emerging markets, the commoditisation of products, and increased price competition. The industry is also
grappling with supply chain security issues, which are largely due to the uneven geographical distribution
of rare metals and rare earths  that are essential for advanced electronic parts and functional chemical
products. Given these challenges, securing sustainable competitiveness has become indispensable for
Japan's manufacturing sector.
Academia in the materials science field also faces significant challenges. The number of research output
has been declining, with a sharp drop i n the international share of materials -related publications. In
addition, the ageing of research talent has become a pressing issue. To address these issues, there is a
growing need to improve the research environment to attract and retain younger researchers in the field.
Opportunities and challenges: Environments, Key Enabling Technologies and External
Factors
Materials are increasingly expected to contribute to global sustainability goals, such as the Sustainable
Development Goals (SDGs). This is in line with growing environmental and societal concerns.
New opportunities for innovative technologies have emerged, particularly in material s informatics and
manufacturing process technologies. These key enabling technologies are driving progress in the field.

4. Japan: Toward regulation of carbon nanotube: activity of an industry

Case study authors: Ayari Takamura (CRDS, JST) and Asako Okamura (NISTEP, MEXT)
Carbon nanotube: its broad applications and structural variety
Carbon nanotube (CNT) is a category of nano -scale tubu lar-shape carbon substances that contribute
widely to a variety of applications, including batteries, memories, transmission cables, composite materials
and medical/health cares. CNT is expected to be one of the key advanced materials for realizing
sustainable ecosystems and individual well-being.
Such a wide range of applications for CNT depends on a great variety of structural features, including
length, diameter, density, single- or multi-walled, surface property, rolled angle, morphology (e.g., tangled,
cross-linked, junction), and carbon purity. These structural features are linked to the characteristic
physiochemical properties of each CNT product.
The regulation of CNT, like other nano materials, is difficult to characterize and risk assess due to th eir
diverse structures and properties. This situation is quite different from chemical compounds, which are
defined with unique structural formulas.
Process toward CNT regulation in Europe
The evaluation of CNT, especially multi -walled CNT (MWCNT), had be en started by Federal Institute for
Occupational Safety and Health (BAuA)  in Germany under Community Rolling Action Plan  by European
Chemicals Agency (ECHA), which was reported in 2019 1. Followingly, BAuA submitted a CLH report on
MWCNT to ECHA for consideration toward CLP (classification, labeling and packaging) regulation in March
2021. The report proposed that MWCNT with diameter range ≥ 30 nm to <3 μm and a length ≥ 5 μm  and
an aspect ratio ≥3:1, should be classified as Carcinogenic Category "Carc.1B" (presumed carcinogenic) in
the CLP classification. Through public consultation and discussion in the Committee for Risk Assessment
(RAC-60) in ECHA, the proposal was adopted as a RAC opinion  in September 2022. Currently, the
proposal is under discussion in the meeting of Competent Authorities for REACH and CLP (CARACAL),
involving RAC-60 in ECHA, and DG GROW and DG ENV in European commission2. Moreover, European
Commission has issued a WTO/BTB Notification on MWCNT based on the BAuA proposal in January
2024.
Activity of NBCI along the process in Europe
NBCI is a n industry association for nanotechnology business and the infrastructure in Japan , which is
composed of more than 170 companies. CNT is one of the most important substances in Japanese industry
including the application fields. Thus, the Social Acceptance and Standardization Committee in NBCI has
contributed to disseminate information a bout evaluation of CNT to other institutes and communities in
Japan and abroad.

7. United States: U.S. Government Accountability Office Assessment of Critical
Minerals Recovery Technologies
Critical minerals such as lithium, cobalt, and rare earth elements are essential for technologies used in the
military and the broader economy , including airplanes, rechargeable batteries, computers, cell phones,
electrical systems, and advanced electronics , but these minerals are vulnerable to supply-chain
disruptions. Domestic, nontraditional sources of minerals have been identified as a potential mechanism
to reduce reliance on foreign suppliers and the need to open new mines, among other benefits.
Critical minerals are often produced from  hardrock mines, though it is possible to produce them from
nontraditional sources . The assessment identified several available and developing technologies for
recovering critical minerals from nontraditional sources, including mining wastes, wastewater from mines,
waste from coal -fired power plants, and saline water from underground formations (geothermal brines) .
For example, with the use of sorbent -based direct lithium extraction, lithium ions in a geothermal brine
adhere to the surface of solid material called a sorbent while other types of ions may stay in the brine. This
technology could offer advantages over the conventional process for recovering lithium from brines which
uses evaporation, such as a smaller footprint and faster lithium recovery.
Several c hallenges to recovering critical minerals from nontraditional sources  were also identified,
including
• Liability. Recovery operations on previously mined sites could result in operators being
responsible for historical liabilities. There is little appet ite in industry to take on this financial risk,
according to experts.
• Economics. Due to factors such as high fixed costs and unstable prices potential recovery project
operators may be uncertain that their investments will be financially viable.
• Public engagement and tribal consultation . Stakeholders and experts identified engagement
with local communities, and when appropriate, government-to-government consultation with Tribal
Nations as important steps to a successful critical mineral recovery project.
Three policy options  were identified by the TA practitioners  that could help address the challenges or
enhance the benefits of recovering critical minerals from nontraditional sources . Additionally, the report
described ongoing legislation and deferral and  private sector projects that could enhance U.S. efforts
related to critical mineral recovery from non -traditional sources. If policymakers find these efforts to be
sufficient, or if they find other sources of critical minerals more suitable to their goals  they may choose to
sustain current efforts by maintaining the status quo.
