About This Report
In this report, we present a vulnerability-centric framework for analyzing the security of generative
artificial intelligence (AI) systems. Moving beyond lists of attacks or incidents, we systematically map
where and how weaknesses arise within the architecture of large models: from training data and
tokenization to transformer layers and deployment interfaces. We identify 31 classes of AI
vulnerabilities, most of which differ from traditional software flaws, often emerging from probabilistic
learning dynamics and optimization trade-offs rather than deterministic code errors. Using structured
threat and impact metrics, we characterize risk and reveal that the greatest vulnerabilities occur during
data ingestion and at user interaction boundaries. We propose practical mitigations that emphasize
provenance validation, architectural safeguards, and continuous monitoring. By reframing AI security
around structural weaknesses rather than adversarial techniques, we offer a foundation for integrating
AI-specific vulnerabilities into global standards and for shaping future policy on trustworthy AI
deployment.

Summary
In this report, we present a vulnerability-centric framework for analyzing the security of generative
artificial intelligence (AI) systems. We systematically map where and how weaknesses arise within the
architecture of large models, from training data and tokenization to transformer layers and
deployment interfaces. Using structured threat and impact metrics, we characterize risk and reveal
that the greatest vulnerabilities occur during data ingestion and at user interaction boundaries.
Specifically, we address the following research questions:
• What are the vulnerability types in AI systems?
• What are the features of those vulnerabilities that would increase the probability of
exploitation, the magnitude of impact to the AI system, and therefore the overall risk to the AI
system?
• What practical mitigations could be applied to reduce the risk of malicious exploitation of
these vulnerabilities?
Key Findings
Several key findings emerged from our analysis:
• First, many of the vulnerabilities in generative AI systems arise from fundamental properties
of data, model architecture, and optimization objectives. Like certain architectural weaknesses
in traditional computing systems, these vulnerabilities can persist across model versions and
deployments and may be difficult to fully eliminate. However, unlike many conventional
software vulnerabilities, AI vulnerabilities often emerge from statistical learning processes and
model behavior rather than discrete implementation errors, making them more difficult to
characterize, detect, and remediate.
• Second, among the AI system components examined, vulnerabilities associated with training
data present the greatest overall risk. Poisoned or unverified training data can embed
persistent weaknesses during model development that propagate across deployments and
influence downstream behavior over time.
• Third, user-facing inference interfaces represent major points of exposure. Components such
as the context window, retrieval-augmented generation pipelines, and prompt boundaries
provide opportunities for adversaries to manipulate model behavior through carefully crafted
inputs or injected contextual information.
These findings suggest that traditional software security approaches, particularly patch-and-fix
remediation models, are insufficient for addressing many AI vulnerabilities. Because many weaknesses
arise from stochastic model behavior or architectural design, mitigating risk requires a deeper

===== PDF_PAGE 6 =====
vi
understanding of where vulnerabilities occur within the system. A vulnerability-centric analytical
approach helps clarify this structure by mapping weaknesses to specific architectural components and
identifying the conditions under which they can be exploited.
Applying structured threat and impact metrics further enables reproducible risk assessments by
linking such factors as accessibility, exploitability, and patchability to potential confidentiality,
integrity, and availability consequences, thus enabling designers to make calculated decisions that can
improve the security of generative AI. The analysis also highlights the importance of optimization and
reward design, because misaligned objectives or over-optimization toward proxy metrics can lead to
systemic behavioral failures. At the same time, limited interpretability of internal model
representations continues to hinder detection and accountability when failures occur. Finally, we
identify a growing class of efficiency-based attacks that target system availability and operational
stability. Such techniques as resource exhaustion or context flooding can degrade system performance
and increase operational costs, demonstrating that risks to AI systems extend beyond information
security to include computational and economic dimensions.
Recommendations
The following recommendations summarize practical steps that developers, security practitioners,
and standards organizations can take to reduce the risk of exploitation and improve the management
of AI vulnerabilities:
• Prioritize security controls at the data layers. Security efforts should prioritize training data,
context windows, and retrieval systems, in which the highest-threat vulnerabilities are
concentrated. Strong dataset governance, provenance tracking, and controls on contextual and
retrieved inputs are critical.
• Treat certain AI vulnerabilities as structural risks rather than as patchable bugs. Because
several weaknesses arise from inherent properties of machine learning systems rather than
discrete implementation flaws, they often cannot be fully eliminated through conventional
patching. Instead, risk must be managed through architectural safeguards, operational
constraints, monitoring, and other compensating controls, which may reduce but not entirely
remove the underlying vulnerability.
• Incorporate component-level risk assessments into AI system design. Evaluating
vulnerabilities at the architectural component level helps identify where risks cluster and
allows engineering teams to prioritize mitigations during system development.
• Develop advanced exploitation detection and monitoring capabilities for AI systems.
Many exploitation pathways are difficult to detect with existing tools, making improved
logging, anomaly detection, and behavioral monitoring essential for identifying adversarial
activity.
• Develop evaluation methods that reflect stochastic exploitation. Security testing should
incorporate stochastic simulations, adversarial prompting, and large-scale behavioral
assessments to better capture how vulnerabilities may manifest under variable conditions.

• Establish criteria for cataloging AI vulnerabilities. While such initiatives as MITRE
ATLAS (Adversarial Threat Landscape for Artificial-Intelligence Systems) provide valuable

management frameworks.

Chapter 1
Introduction
The pace of development in artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities, coupled with the rapid
adoption of these systems across consumer, enterprise, and government applications, has been
unprecedented. Large language models (LLMs) and other generative AI systems are now widely
integrated into search engines, productivity software, coding tools, customer support platforms, and
scientific research workflows. This rapid diffusion of AI technologies has been accompanied by
significant optimism regarding their potential economic and societal benefits. However, the speed of
deployment has also outpaced our collective understanding of the risks that these systems introduce
and the mechanisms by which they may fail or be exploited (Bommasani et al., 2022; Brundage et al.,
2024).
In response, a growing ecosystem of industry, academic, and civil society initiatives has emerged to
document and analyze the risks associated with AI systems. A growing body of research literature
describes adversarial attacks, prompt injection techniques, data poisoning strategies, and other
mechanisms by which AI systems can be manipulated (Alhajjar, Maxwell, and Bastian, 2021; Ghosh
et al., 2025; Vassilev et al., 2025).
Much of the existing literature and data collection focuses on attacks, incidents, or misuse cases
rather than on the underlying vulnerabilities that make such attacks possible. That is, many studies
describe how AI systems are attacked, but fewer systematically identify the specific structural
weaknesses within AI systems that enable those attacks to succeed. Without a clear conceptualization
of these vulnerabilities, it becomes difficult to reason systematically about risk, compare mitigation
strategies, or integrate AI security issues into existing vulnerability management frameworks.
Part of the difficulty lies in the fact that vulnerabilities in AI systems challenge conventional
notions of software vulnerabilities. In traditional software security, vulnerabilities are typically
identifiable as specific flaws in program logic or implementation, often localized to particular lines of
code, and their behavior is largely deterministic (Dowd, McDonald, and Schuh, 2006). These
weaknesses can often be addressed through code revisions, configuration changes, or software patches.
Over decades, the software security community has developed robust mechanisms for cataloging and
managing such vulnerabilities, including the Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) system
and the Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE) taxonomy (CVE Program, undated; CWE,
undated). Risk associated with these vulnerabilities is often quantified using standardized scoring
systems, such as the Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) and the Exploit Prediction
Scoring System (EPSS), which characterize both the likelihood of exploitation and the severity of
potential impacts (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams, undated-a; Forum of Incident
Response and Security Teams, undated-b).
AI vulnerabilities differ from traditional software vulnerabilities in several important respects.
First, many AI failures arise from statistical properties of trained models rather than deterministic

===== PDF_PAGE 12 =====
2
code defects. Weaknesses may emerge from training data composition, representational structures
within the model, optimization objectives, or deployment architectures. Second, AI vulnerabilities
often manifest probabilistically rather than deterministically; the same input may produce different
outputs depending on model stochasticity or contextual information. Third, some weaknesses in AI
systems may not be fully patchable in the conventional sense because they arise from fundamental
trade-offs in statistical learning and model architecture, such as generalization, representation
capacity, or alignment between training objectives and deployment contexts. As a result, AI security
challenges require conceptual tools that differ from those traditionally used in software vulnerability
management (Open Worldwide Application Security Project [OWASP] Foundation, undated-a).
In this report, we seek to address this gap by developing a systematic framework for identifying
and analyzing vulnerabilities in generative AI systems (Figure 1.1). For the purposes of this research,
we define a vulnerability as a flaw or weakness in an AI system component that could be exploited to
compromise the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of the inputs to or outputs from that system.
This definition extends traditional vulnerability concepts to account for the fact that, in AI systems,
harms may arise not only from unauthorized access to system components but also from manipulation
or degradation of model behavior (Aslan et al., 2023).
Figure 1.1. Research Contributions of This Report

A central contribution of this work is the adoption of a vulnerability-centric approach to AI
security analysis. Rather than focusing primarily on specific attack techniques, we seek to identify the
underlying weaknesses within AI systems that enable multiple classes of attacks to succeed. By
examining where these weaknesses arise within the architecture of generative AI systems and how they
influence the likelihood and impact of exploitation, we aim to provide a more structured foundation
for understanding and mitigating AI security risks.
Specifically, we address the following research questions in this report:
• What are the vulnerability types in AI systems?
• What are the features of those vulnerabilities that would increase the probability of
exploitation, the magnitude of impact to the AI system, and therefore the overall risk to the AI
system?
• What practical mitigations could be applied to reduce the risk of malicious exploitation of
these vulnerabilities?

===== PDF_PAGE 13 =====
3
To answer these questions, we developed a structured decomposition of generative AI systems,
identifying the major components involved in both their training and deployment. This schematic
representation provides a principled basis for locating vulnerabilities within the AI stack. Building on
this structure, we clustered attacks against AI systems into five broad categories and analyzed how
these attacks exploit underlying system weaknesses. Across these components, we enumerate more
than 30 distinct classes of vulnerabilities, providing a characterization of where and why generative AI
systems are susceptible to exploitation.
To assess the practical significance of these vulnerabilities, we introduce a set of structured metrics
that capture two key dimensions of risk: the likelihood that a vulnerability can be successfully
exploited and the severity of the impact if exploitation occurs. By combining these dimensions, we
developed a reproducible framework for evaluating the overall risk associated with different classes of
AI vulnerabilities. Finally, we propose criteria that could inform how AI-specific vulnerabilities might
be incorporated into existing vulnerability standards, such as CVE and CWE, thereby contributing to
the development of more-systematic mechanisms for cataloging and managing AI security weaknesses.
Together, these contributions aim to move the discussion of AI security beyond lists of attacks or
incidents toward a more rigorous understanding of the structural vulnerabilities embedded within
modern AI systems. This report is organized as follows: In Chapter 2, we describe the research
approach used to derive vulnerabilities and analyze their associated risks. Chapter 3 presents a
taxonomy of attacks against AI systems. Chapter 4 enumerates vulnerabilities across the major
components of generative AI systems. Chapter 5 introduces a framework for characterizing risk by
evaluating both the likelihood of exploitation and the severity of potential impacts. Chapter 6
discusses mitigation strategies applicable to different classes of vulnerabilities. We finish the report
with a set of recommendations in Chapter 7 and a brief conclusion in Chapter 8.

Chapter 2
Research Approach
Methodology
We outline the research approach in Figure 2.1 and explain it below.
Figure 2.1. Research Approach

To identify the set of possible vulnerabilities in AI systems, we applied two approaches. First, we
examined prominent literature of AI systems, model diagrams, and other reference material and
constructed a schematic that identifies each component of an AI system, from the training of a base
model to the deployment of a functional large model (depicted in Figure 4.1 in Chapter 4).
Then, for each component, we documented the flaws or weaknesses that could compromise the
operation of the AI system or the information generated from it. Next, we collected examples of both
real-world and theoretical attacks against AI systems by monitoring multiple cyber and technology
mailing lists daily from August 2025 through March 2026 and by reviewing blogs, news stories, and
research papers. For each attack, we coded the name of the attack (if named), the publication date, the
source, the type of attack, the weakness(es) exploited in the AI system (where possible), and whether
it was a real-world or a theoretical attack. These two steps combined to produce our final list of
vulnerability types.
We defined a set of scoping decisions for this research:
• Our focus is on generative AI, which we define as large models that generate text, image, video,
speech, and other elements. We acknowledge that the focus of most literature is on LLMs,
although many of the vulnerabilities identified in our analysis stem from properties of data-

===== PDF_PAGE 15 =====
5
driven learning, representation, optimization, and deployment that are also present in
multimodal generative models.
• In our analysis of AI risk, we did not explicitly consider harms resulting from AI bias, such as
racially, demographically, or politically biased outputs.
1
• We did not examine attacks using AI tools but rather just vulnerabilities in AI systems.
In our analysis of threats and impacts, we considered direct impacts to the AI model and did not
include indirect impacts to external systems. Consequently, we did not examine vulnerabilities in
computing, networking, or supply chain components.
We need to highlight two important notes. First, this list represents vulnerability types, or classes of
vulnerabilities, not specific vulnerabilities in specific AI systems. Just as the CWE schema defines classes
of regular software vulnerabilities (with CVEs being specific instantiations of a vulnerability class in a
specific product version), the AI vulnerability types we discuss in this report are types or classes.
With a working list of vulnerability types, we next sought to characterize the features of the
vulnerability that would affect the probability of exploitation (threat) and the severity of impact to the
AI system if a vulnerability of this type were to be exploited. This approach was heavily influenced by
the efforts of the CVSS and its approach to enumerating features of the vulnerability that affect these
risk metrics (Chapter 5).
Through our literature review and vulnerability analysis, we identified and captured mitigations
that could be applied to each AI vulnerability. We first investigated and identified vulnerabilities
across each component of AI development and deployment, beginning with training, then fine-tuning,
followed by inference and deployment. We also integrated mitigations from the Secure AI Framework
(SAIF), segmenting vulnerabilities by life-cycle stage and categories, such as governance, assurance,
data, and structure (or infrastructure) (Google, undated). This analysis reveals how some mitigations
can be applied over multiple phases, while other mitigations address only a single phase or are relevant
to only a single component.
This research differs in both scope and methodology from existing efforts that catalog AI security
concerns. AI incident databases, for example, compile news reports and documented cases of AI
systems causing harm. Although valuable for tracking real-world impacts, such efforts focus on
outcomes rather than the underlying structural weaknesses that make those outcomes possible. Our
objective was not to aggregate incidents but to identify and characterize the architectural
vulnerabilities that give rise to exploitable risk.
Other initiatives, such as the OWASP Top 10 for LLMs, provide practitioner-oriented lists of
common attack types (OWASP Foundation, undated-b). These lists are useful for awareness and
defensive guidance but are not structured around distinct vulnerability classes. Many listed items
overlap conceptually or map to multiple underlying weaknesses. Moreover, such efforts tend to focus
specifically on LLMs, whereas our framework considers generative AI systems more broadly,
including multimodal models and systems augmented with retrieval and memory components.

Importantly, our approach emphasizes a systematic derivation of vulnerabilities from architectural
decomposition rather than a curated enumeration of prominent attack patterns.
Similarly, MITRE ATLAS (Adversarial Threat Landscape for Artificial-Intelligence Systems)
offers a detailed taxonomy of adversarial machine learning techniques, organized around tactics,
techniques, and procedures across an attack life cycle (MITRE ATLAS, undated). This work is
valuable for threat modeling and red teaming, but it is attack-centric. In contrast, our analysis is
vulnerability-centric: Rather than cataloging how adversaries operate, we sought to identify the
structural weaknesses within generative AI systems that enable exploitation across multiple attack
classes.
In the same context, the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) AI Risk
Management Framework provides a comprehensive governance framework for identifying, managing,
and communicating risks associated with AI systems (NIST, undated). It emphasizes organizational
processes, such as risk governance, measurement, monitoring, and documentation, across the AI life
cycle. While this approach is valuable for establishing institutional practices and accountability, it
operates at a high level of abstraction and does not attempt to enumerate specific technical
vulnerabilities within AI system architectures. In contrast, our analysis focuses on the structural
components of generative AI systems and systematically derives vulnerability classes from those
components. Rather than providing general guidance for risk management, our framework was
designed to identify the specific technical weaknesses that may give rise to the risks that organizations
are expected to manage under broader governance frameworks.
Limitations
We do not claim to provide an exhaustive catalog of all possible vulnerabilities across all AI
systems but rather vulnerabilities that relate to observed attacks at the time of writing. Generative AI
architectures continue to evolve rapidly, and new modalities, system integrations, and deployment
configurations may introduce novel weaknesses that are not captured in this report. Our enumeration
should therefore be understood as a structured baseline rather than as a definitive taxonomy. The
framework is designed to be expandable, allowing additional vulnerability classes to be incorporated as
systems and threat models mature.
Enumeration of vulnerabilities is inherently imperfect. Vulnerabilities do not exist as neatly
separable natural categories; rather, they often overlap conceptually, share underlying mechanisms, or
differ in only degree or context. In some cases, what appears to be a distinct vulnerability may instead
represent a variant or manifestation of a more general weakness. Conversely, seemingly similar
vulnerabilities may have materially different implications depending on system architecture or
deployment context. In developing this classification, we sought to balance completeness with
usability, avoiding both excessive granularity that obscures structural patterns and over-aggregation
that masks meaningful distinctions.
A related limitation concerns the architectural decomposition of AI systems into components.
The boundaries between components, such as tokenizer, embedding, and transformer layers, are
analytically useful but not always cleanly separable in practice. Modern AI systems increasingly blur
distinctions between training and inference, model and tool use, core architecture and orchestration

===== PDF_PAGE 17 =====
7
layer. Our component framework reflects a conceptual decomposition intended to facilitate
vulnerability mapping, not a rigid representation of every system’s implementation. Alternative
decompositions may be equally defensible depending on analytical objectives.
Additionally, the analysis is limited to vulnerabilities that arise from the architecture and
operation of AI systems themselves. It does not address risks originating from external factors, such as
supporting infrastructure, supply chains, hardware platforms, organizational processes, or broader
governance failures. Consequently, the risk landscape presented here reflects only the subset of risks
that are intrinsic to AI system components and their interactions.
Despite these limitations, the vulnerability-centric approach presented in this report produces a
structured framework for analyzing AI vulnerabilities by linking architectural components,
vulnerability classes, risk characteristics, and mitigation strategies within a common analytical
structure.

Chapter 3
Attacks Against AI Systems
Generative AI systems are exposed to a broad and evolving landscape of exploitation techniques.
These techniques vary in timing, mechanism, required capability, and intended effect. Some involve
direct adversarial manipulation of inputs or training data; others exploit inherent statistical or
behavioral properties of the model without modifying its parameters. This chapter introduces a high-
level categorization of attacks against AI systems. We use these categories as a diagnostic tool to
illustrate how diverse attack techniques map onto a shared set of underlying vulnerabilities. We
identify five broad categories of exploitation: evasion, extraction, poisoning, misalignment, and
efficiency. Together, these categories span both training time and inference time threats, as well as
manipulation of system boundaries and internal representations.
Evasion
Evasion attacks manipulate inputs at inference time to cause the model to produce unintended or
unsafe outputs without altering its underlying weights (Hackett et al., 2025). In generative AI
systems, these include techniques such as (direct or indirect) prompt injection, adversarial context
manipulation, encoding tricks that bypass filters, and crafted token sequences that distort semantic
interpretation. Evasion attacks exploit weaknesses in how models process and prioritize input
information. These attacks do not require access to training data or model internals, making them
particularly relevant in widely deployed application programming interface (API)–based systems.
Extraction
Extraction attacks aim to recover information, capabilities, or functionality from a model. These
include membership inference, reconstruction of memorized training examples, model extraction,
capability distillation, and attempts to reverse engineer model parameters or proprietary knowledge
(Das, Amini, and Wu, 2025). Some extraction attacks exploit the model’s tendency to encode and
occasionally reproduce artifacts from its training corpus, whereas others seek to replicate the model’s
learned behavior or capabilities through repeated interaction. In large generative systems trained on
heterogeneous data, memorization and overconfidence may enable partial disclosure of sensitive or
proprietary information. Extraction can occur even in the absence of direct access to internal weights,
relying solely on repeated inference queries.

Poisoning
Poisoning attacks target the training or fine-tuning pipeline by introducing manipulated data
designed to alter model behavior. They may include backdoor triggers, label manipulation, or
systematic insertion of biased or malicious examples (Fendley et al., 2025). Poisoning exploits
weaknesses in data validation and governance processes. Because large-scale training pipelines often
rely on automated data collection and aggregation, subtle manipulations can persist undetected and
become encoded in model parameters. Unlike evasion, poisoning operates upstream and can produce
persistent behavioral shifts that manifest long after deployment.
Misalignment
Not all exploitation requires adversarial modification of inputs or training data. Generative models
exhibit inherent behavioral tendencies, such as hallucination, overconfidence, or sycophancy, that may
be leveraged or amplified in deployment (Zhou et al., 2023). Here, the system’s statistical or
optimization-driven properties produce outputs that may be unreliable, misleading, or misaligned
with stakeholder intent. Although these weaknesses may arise without malicious intervention, they
can be operationalized by adversaries or result in systemic harm in high-stakes settings.

highlights an important distinction: Some risks are induced externally, whereas others are intrinsic to

Efficiency
Efficiency-based attacks aim to degrade system performance, exhaust computational resources, or
induce denial-of-service conditions (Brachemi Meftah et al., 2026). Examples include token flooding,
excessive context expansion, and repeated high-cost queries intended to increase inference time or
resource consumption. These attacks exploit architectural constraints, such as context window size,
memory allocation, and inference scaling properties. Although they do not necessarily alter model
outputs directly, they affect system availability and operational stability.

Vulnerabilities in Training and Fine-Tuning Data
At the training and fine-tuning stages, vulnerabilities emerge from weaknesses in data governance
and validation. These vulnerabilities include unverified provenance, the presence of manipulated or
poisoned data, and a lack of quality control (Zhu et al., 2024). Because large models ingest vast and
heterogeneous corpora, insufficient validation can introduce persistent structural weaknesses,
including backdoors, memorization of sensitive information, or skewed behavioral priors. Data-level
vulnerabilities are particularly consequential because they become encoded in the model’s weights and
are difficult to remove post hoc without retraining.
Tokenizer and Embedding Vulnerabilities
Within the tokenizer and embedding layers, vulnerabilities arise from representational brittleness.
Non-robust sub-word segmentation, sensitivity to Unicode sequences, adversarial token strings, loss
of contextual nuance, and semantic overcompression reflect limitations in how inputs are encoded.
These weaknesses enable evasion techniques, semantic ambiguity, and unintended associations
(Wang, Li, et al., 2025). Although they do not directly alter model weights, they shape the model’s
interpretation of inputs and therefore influence downstream behavior.

===== PDF_PAGE 24 =====
14
Transformer Vulnerabilities
Within the transformer weights and internal representations, vulnerabilities reflect the statistical
nature of large-scale learning. These include overgeneralization beyond the training distribution,
nondeterministic behavior under small perturbations, accidental reconstruction of training data, and
opaque internal reasoning processes (Latibari et al., 2024). Such vulnerabilities are often inherent to
probabilistic modeling and high-dimensional representation learning, and they reflect trade-offs
among generalization, memorization, and expressivity.
Reward Function Vulnerabilities
The reward-loss functions introduce another class of weaknesses. Misalignment between the
training objective and desired behavior, over-optimization toward proxy metrics, static objectives that
fail to reflect deployment context, and overconfidence or miscalibration all stem from how
performance is defined and optimized (Zhao et al., 2026). Unlike data or representational flaws, these
vulnerabilities arise when the objective used for optimization imperfectly captures the desired behavior
of the system. Such gaps can create opportunities for reward hacking, proxy optimization, or other
forms of exploitation in which the model achieves the specified objective while producing outcomes
that diverge from stakeholder intent.
Context Window and RAG Vulnerabilities
Finally, vulnerabilities emerge at the system boundary. These include lack of segmentation
between system and user prompts, context overflow susceptibility, absence of provenance checks on
contextual input, weak trust boundaries in RAG systems, and insufficient transparency regarding
retrieved content (Lin and Mohaisen, 2024). These weaknesses are often exploited through evasion or
manipulation techniques but are fundamentally architectural in nature. They arise from the lack of
architectural separation between instructions and data.

Chapter 5
Characterizing the Risk of AI
Vulnerabilities
Characterizing the risk of generative AI vulnerabilities presents a unique set of challenges that
traditional cybersecurity frameworks are ill-equipped to address. Unlike many conventional software
vulnerabilities, which can often be traced to specific implementation flaws, AI vulnerabilities
frequently arise from learned representations, optimization objectives, training data characteristics,
and interactions among multiple system components. As a result, the risk associated with a given
vulnerability may vary substantially across models and deployment environments depending on such
factors as system architecture, component exposure, contextual inputs, and operational use.
Thus, our approach shifts the focus from attack-centric characterizations of AI security toward a
vulnerability-centric analysis of architectural weaknesses. Rather than organizing risk around
adversary behaviors or attack techniques, vulnerabilities are analyzed according to the AI components
in which they arise, enabling risk characterization grounded in the properties of underlying
weaknesses.
To operationalize the risk framework, vulnerabilities are aggregated and evaluated at the
component level, with each component scored across five threat metrics and five impact metrics. This
aggregation shifts the analysis from individual vulnerabilities to the broader risk profile of the
architectural components in which they reside. The resulting scores characterize (1) the likelihood
that vulnerabilities associated with a component can be successfully exploited, based on their shared
threat characteristics, and (2) the potential impact of successful exploitation. These scores reflect the
inherent properties of the vulnerabilities themselves rather than mission- or application-specific
consequences, which may vary across deployment contexts.
Importantly, the scores presented in this chapter represent typical values for each vulnerability
class as observed in the literature and practice. We acknowledge that characterizations around any
specific instantiation may vary depending on model architecture, deployment, and the operational
environment. Nonetheless, this framing provides a basis for prioritizing mitigations by identifying
which vulnerability classes and components exhibit the greatest threat and impact characteristics.
Also, the scoring presented here is intended as a structured, reproducible assessment based on defined
rubrics and informed by existing literature rather than as a statistically validated predictive model.
Validation is therefore performed through internal consistency checks and alignment with known
empirical patterns in AI security research.

Threat
In developing the threat metrics, we identified features of the vulnerability that could affect the
probability of a successful attack. Because our unit of analysis is the vulnerability, we sought to capture
the properties of the vulnerability, rather than strictly attacker motives, emerging threat vectors, or
geopolitical factors, that would lead to changes in the threat. This approach aligns with the logic of the
CVSS, which treats exploitability as an intrinsic property of the weakness, but we have adapted it to
the probabilistic complexity of LLMs. Focusing on variables or features that directly influence the
likelihood of a successful attack, our approach evaluates the practical reachability and exploitability of
vulnerabilities within a standard deployment environment. In this context, threat refers to properties
of the vulnerability that affect exploitation likelihood rather than adversary intent or motivation. The
threat metrics are shown in Table 5.1.

The specific threat metrics in the vulnerability-centric framework are accessibility, exploitability,
determinism, resource requirements, and patchability (Table 5.1). Below is a detailed explanation of
each metric:
• Accessibility. This metric captures how reachable a vulnerable component is from outside the
system, whether through direct user prompts, indirect content (e.g., webpages, documents,
RAG), retrieved artifacts, or other external data sources. A severe vulnerability is unlikely to
be exploited if the component is effectively isolated (e.g., embeddings), whereas modest
weaknesses become high threats when continuously exposed (e.g., vulnerabilities exploited
through the API). This metric can distinguish between high-barrier attacks (e.g., gradient-
based model inversion) and low-barrier exploits (e.g., basic prompt injection).
• Exploitability. Exploitability approximates the engineering sophistication needed to elicit
failure modes (including dependence on specialized prompting, data placement, trial and error,
or model-specific variations). While some flaws can be exploited with simple natural language
(e.g., direct prompt injection), others require a deep understanding of tokenization
irregularities or the ability to craft sophisticated adversarial perturbations in the embedding
space. Higher exploitability scores indicate that the vulnerability’s characteristics lower the

===== PDF_PAGE 27 =====
17
barrier to entry, increasing the likelihood that a broader variety of actors can successfully
trigger the flaw.
• Determinism. Determinism measures the consistency with which exploitation succeeds across
repeated attempts or minor input variations. Vulnerabilities with high determinism produce
reliable outcomes when triggered, whereas vulnerabilities with low determinism exhibit
variable success rates even under similar conditions.
• Resource requirements. This metric evaluates the material assets, such as specialized
hardware, compute, or access to vast datasets, required to successfully exploit the vulnerability.
It measures how expensive or resource-intensive it is to successfully exploit the vulnerability.
For example, exploiting memory or the tokenizer requires significant resources and specialized
expertise to execute a campaign,
7 whereas exploiting a lack of input sanitization in an API
requires little effort. These requirements help assess whether the vulnerability’s characteristics
limit the threat to well-resourced adversaries or broaden susceptibility to low-cost, high-
frequency exploitation.
• Patchability. This metric assesses whether the vulnerability is a flaw that can be fixed through
such measures as semantic filtering, context-aware fine-tuning, or hardening training data
validation or whether it is an inherent, unpatchable property of the model’s architecture.
Vulnerabilities that are patchable via common engineering (e.g., API sanitization) represent
transient threats, whereas intrinsic vulnerabilities (e.g., non-robust sub-word segmentation)
provide a persistent surface for attackers.

Impact
In developing the impact metrics, we sought to identify the features of the vulnerability that would
affect the severity of impact of exploitation to the AI model. We recognize that there may well be
variation in impact from the successful exploitation of different vulnerabilities in a class, so when
considering impact, we sought to capture the most likely worst-case scenario in the event the
vulnerability was exploited. Characterizing impact in foundation models is inherently more difficult
than in classical computing (Kapoor et al., 2024), given the evolving internal states of neural networks
and the expanding ecosystem of interconnected systems. AI failure modes stem from novel
vulnerability classes, such as semantic sensitivity or behavioral drift, rather than a binary system crash
(Huang et al., 2025; Li, Kreuzwieser, and Peters, 2025; Rando et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2026).
However, the impact of the exploitation of generative AI can be captured using more standard
cybersecurity measures. Indeed, our impact metrics intentionally borrow the familiar confidentiality,
integrity, and availability (CIA) triad from conventional vulnerability scoring—e.g., the disclosure of
sensitive user data (confidentiality), corruption or untrusted modification of outputs and downstream
decisions (integrity), and disruption or degradation of service through resource exhaustion or denial-
of-service behaviors (availability). Where our approach departs from the CIA triad is by explicitly
adding scope, because of its usefulness given that generative AI vulnerabilities can facilitate the
propagation of exploits across boundaries, and detectability (Cohen, Bitton, and Nassi, 2025). The
impact metrics are detailed in Table 5.3. They are not intended to be fully independent; rather, they
capture complementary aspects of the consequences associated with successful exploitation.
Table 5.3. Impact Metrics
Metric Description
Confidentiality The degree to which the vulnerability discloses sensitive information about the
model or data
Integrity The extent to which the vulnerability compromises the correctness or reliability
of system outputs
Availability The extent to which the vulnerability, if exploited, causes a reduction in
performance or access to the model
Scope The extent to which impacts from exploitation extend beyond the AI system,
encompassing both persistence and propagation of impact across sessions,
users, or connected systems
Detectability The ability to detect when a vulnerability is exploited

The five impact metrics included in the vulnerability-centric framework are confidentiality,
integrity, availability, scope, and detectability (Table 5.3). Below is a detailed explanation of each
metric:
• Confidentiality. This metric measures the extent to which the exploitation of a vulnerability
results in the disclosure of sensitive data, such as the model’s training set, system prompts, or

===== PDF_PAGE 29 =====
19
private user session data. This ranges from benign leakage of nonsensitive internal formatting
to reconstruction of the underlying model (e.g., model extraction or inversion).
• Integrity. The integrity metric assesses the extent to which an exploit compromises the
correctness, reliability, or truthfulness of the model’s outputs or actions. Because AI systems
are often used for decisionmaking, vulnerabilities that lead to factually incorrect information
could cause widespread harm to the information ecosystem. This becomes far more
consequential when AI is integrated into critical systems or enterprise workflows, in which
integrity failure could be catastrophic. Accordingly, high-impact integrity failures occur when
a vulnerability allows an attacker to systematically subvert the model’s reasoning process or
underlying logic, leading to consistently flawed or maliciously steered content (Lucas, 2024).
• Availability. This metric captures the reduction in the system’s performance or the loss of
access for legitimate users. Availability impacts often stem from efficiency attacks, such as
context window exhaustion or resource-heavy queries that spike inference latency (Brachemi
Meftah et al., 2026). Unlike traditional distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks that
target network bandwidth, efficiency attacks exploit the specific computational bottlenecks of
the transformer architecture, potentially causing disruption of the AI service or unsustainable
operational costs.
• Scope. The scope metric measures the potential for the vulnerability to affect an exploit’s blast
radius or cascading effects across sessions, users, or integrated systems, as well as its
persistence. A vulnerability with a low scope impact is confined to a single interaction, whereas
high scope vulnerabilities could involve cross-session leakage or the ability to jump trust
boundaries between the LLM and connected enterprise applications. This dimension is critical
for identifying vulnerabilities that threaten the broader environment’s security posture beyond
the local session.
• Detectability. This metric evaluates the ease with which an exploit can be identified by system
monitors, audit logs, or the end user. This is a vital component of impact because deeply
embedded silent exploits, such as subtle poisoning of training data or unverified knowledge
retrieval (e.g., with a hidden instruction for a back door), can persist for long periods without
remediation. Vulnerabilities that allow attackers to leave no detectable trace in system logs or
produce outputs that are indistinguishable from nominal model behavior carry a much higher
risk, because the harm remains latent and unaddressed.

Risk
Risk assessment in cybersecurity is typically framed as an estimate of expected loss from a threat
event or hazard, characterized by its likelihood of occurrence, its potential impact, and vulnerability,
systematized methodologically by organizations, such as NIST (NIST, 2012; NIST, 2023). We
applied the threat and impact scoring rubrics (Tables 5.2 and 5.4) to vulnerabilities aggregated at the
component level for our risk assessment. Because comprehensive empirical data do not exist for many
AI vulnerability classes, scoring relied on expert judgment informed by published research,
documented incidents, and technical analysis of the underlying vulnerabilities. We aggregated
vulnerabilities at the component level to reflect the underlying system architecture and to enable a
structural, component-based analysis of risk. This approach also yields more-consistent scoring
patterns within components, supporting interpretable comparisons across the AI system. Anchoring
the analysis at the component level also makes the results more interpretable for engineers and
facilitates the mapping of mitigations to specific parts of the AI system.
The resulting scores (Figure 5.1) reveal a clear stratification of risk, with higher concentrations
appearing in the foundational training stages (e.g., training data) and at the inference interface (e.g.,
the context window). This pattern aligns with findings in recent frontier AI research suggesting that
the most significant risks often originate either during model development, in which weaknesses
become embedded in the model, or at the point of user interaction, where attacks such as prompt
injection can exploit those weaknesses (Wu et al., 2026). More details about the scoring appear in
Appendix B.

Regarding the threat metrics, the results show a strongly bimodal distribution across the
accessibility and exploitability dimensions and are closely correlated with one another across system
components. In practice, accessibility to AI system components is sharply bifurcated between
externally exposed interfaces and internally controlled model components. Although accessibility and
exploitability are conceptually distinct, the results suggest that they are correlated for many AI
components. Externally exposed components tend to be both easier to reach and easier to exploit,
whereas internally controlled components tend to be more difficult to exploit along both dimensions.
As a result, vulnerabilities at inference interfaces, such as the context window or retrieval systems, tend
to dominate the practical threat landscape, whereas vulnerabilities in internal components (e.g.,
tokenizer, embedding, transformer weights) are primarily relevant in scenarios involving supply chain
compromise or privileged access. With the exception of two components, the accessibility and
exploitability scores are nearly identical, suggesting that many successful attacks on generative AI
systems arise from the presence of externally accessible interfaces that allow adversaries to interact
with vulnerable components.
A second notable finding is that vulnerabilities associated with approximately half of the system
components score medium on patchability, meaning that remediation would generally require
retraining, architectural modification, or other substantial intervention rather than conventional
software updates. Examples include weaknesses in representation learning, model generalization, and
alignment between training objectives and deployment behavior that reflect fundamental properties of

===== PDF_PAGE 32 =====

statistical learning systems. Unlike conventional software vulnerabilities, these weaknesses cannot be
eliminated without altering model architecture or training procedures. Consequently, many AI
vulnerabilities should be understood not as discrete software bugs but as inherent limitations or trade-
offs in model design and learning dynamics. Risk management strategies must therefore rely more
heavily on compensating controls, monitoring mechanisms, and architectural safeguards than
traditional patch-based remediation.
The analysis also reveals that most AI vulnerabilities exhibit variable exploitation outcomes:
Successful exploitation does not necessarily produce consistent results across repeated attempts and
may depend on such factors as prompt structure, contextual inputs, retrieval content, or model
sampling behavior. This variability has two important implications. First, adversaries may need to
perform repeated attempts or carefully tune inputs to achieve desired outcomes. Second, defenders
face challenges in testing and validation because vulnerabilities may not manifest consistently during
evaluation, making it difficult to determine whether a mitigation has fully addressed the underlying
weakness. Mitigation strategies must therefore focus on reducing the probability or impact of failure
rather than guaranteeing complete prevention. This dynamic distinguishes AI security from many
traditional cybersecurity problems (and mitigations), in which vulnerabilities often produce
deterministic and repeatable exploitation conditions.
With respect to impact severity, our assessment indicates that vulnerabilities associated with
roughly half of the AI system components exhibit low and medium detectability scores, suggesting
that successful exploitation may be difficult to identify using current monitoring and evaluation
approaches. In particular, failures that arise from internal model representations or training dynamics
may not produce observable indicators that clearly distinguish malicious exploitation from normal
model behavior. This creates challenges for monitoring and incident response because many attacks
may manifest only as subtle degradations in model reliability or accuracy rather than as clearly
identifiable security events. Additional research is therefore needed to develop methods for detecting
anomalous model behavior and attributing it to potential exploitation of underlying vulnerabilities.
Finally, the scoring results indicate that attacks on generative AI systems rarely manifest as losses
of system availability. Instead, the dominant impacts involve compromises of confidentiality and
integrity. These findings suggest that AI security challenges differ fundamentally from traditional
software security in their impact profile: Rather than disrupting system operation, most attacks target
the information produced or processed by the system.

and downstream deployments, affecting model behavior over long periods (Cui et al., 2025; Hubinger

propagation and persistence across different users and systems.
At the same time, there is significant variation in risk across the different data-related components
of AI systems. In particular, attacks targeting training data pose substantially higher risk than those
targeting fine-tuning data or retrieval corpora used in RAG pipelines. This difference is primarily
driven by the scale and influence of pretraining datasets, which often contain billions of characters and
directly shape the core representations learned by the model. In contrast, fine-tuning datasets tend to
be smaller, and attacks are often easier to identify, isolate, or reverse than vulnerabilities embedded in
pretraining corpora (Carlini et al., 2024). Similarly, RAG-based systems incorporate external data
only at inference time, which limits the persistence of manipulation to the duration of a single query or
session. As a result, while all data components can be exploited in principle, vulnerabilities in training
data pipelines have a uniquely broad and persistent impact on model behavior.

unchanged, the effects of such attacks are typically transient and localized rather than systemic. In
other words, the scope of impact is structurally constrained: Although adversaries may influence

In contrast, vulnerabilities associated with the transformer architecture and the reward-loss

these components is substantially more difficult because they are generally inaccessible to external
actors and typically require control over training pipelines or internal development processes.
However, if such vulnerabilities were successfully exploited, the consequences could be significantly
more severe. Manipulations at the level of model parameters or optimization objectives could alter the
behavior of the model for all users and across all interactions. For this reason, these components
warrant continued attention, particularly as advances in offensive AI capabilities could increase the
feasibility of exploiting such vulnerabilities in the future.
Finally, the results indicate that vulnerabilities associated with the reward-loss function appear to
pose a lower threat level than might be expected given the growing attention to such risks as deception,
scheming, or other forms of misaligned behavior in advanced AI systems (Carlsmith, 2023). One
reason for this discrepancy is that many misalignment-related risks arise from internal training
dynamics rather than adversarial exploitation by external actors. In other words, these behaviors may
emerge from the interaction among training objectives, optimization processes, and model capabilities
rather than from deliberate attempts by adversaries to manipulate the system. Additionally, the
reward-loss function is typically accessible only within internal training pipelines, which limits
opportunities for direct adversarial exploitation. As a result, although vulnerabilities in this
component may produce significant consequences if triggered, the probability of adversarial actors
successfully exploiting them remains comparatively low under existing deployment architectures.

Chapter 6
Mitigations for AI Vulnerabilities
Having identified and characterized AI vulnerabilities across system components, we next examine
the extent to which those vulnerabilities may be mitigated. Our goal in this chapter is to systematically
identify interventions that directly address the mechanisms underlying each vulnerability. Mitigations
are evaluated along two dimensions. First, we assess the plausible effectiveness of risk reduction, defined
in terms of our ten-dimension rubric spanning threat and impact characteristics. Second, we evaluate
feasibility, reflecting implementation complexity, architectural disruption, maturity of existing
techniques, and notional operational cost.
Note that not all vulnerabilities are equally mitigable. Some admit significant risk reduction
through well-established engineering controls, whereas others are structurally tied to the statistical
and optimization properties of generative models and therefore admit only partial or marginal
mitigation. The mitigations presented in this chapter are not intended to provide an exhaustive or
one-to-one mapping to each vulnerability. Rather, they illustrate representative strategies for
addressing classes of vulnerabilities at the component level. This reflects the existing practice in AI
system security, in which defenses are typically applied at the system or pipeline level rather than
tailored to individual vulnerabilities (NIST, 2023).
Training and Fine-Tuning Data Mitigations
Vulnerabilities that originate in training and fine-tuning data are addressed through governing
what information enters the model and how it is prepared prior to training. Core measures include
source validation and provenance checking to verify the credibility and origin of datasets; adversarial
data testing to identify poisoned or manipulated samples; and data sanitization procedures, such as
deduplication, filtering of poisoned records, and removal of sensitive or controlled information. These
interventions aim to impose discipline on large-scale data collection practices, which are often
heterogeneous and weakly curated (Kayali et al., 2024). Because data are foundational to model
behavior, these mitigations operate upstream and shape the statistical properties of the learned system
itself.
In terms of effectiveness, data-level controls meaningfully reduce exploitability for poisoning and
backdoor scenarios by increasing attacker effort and decreasing deterministic trigger reliability. They
also materially reduce confidentiality risk, where memorization stems from identifiable data artifacts.
However, they do not eliminate emergent correlations, distributional blind spots, or hallucination
behaviors that arise from generalization dynamics rather than explicit data flaws (Pal et al., 2024).
Feasibility is uneven. Deduplication and personally identifiable information filtering are mature
and widely deployable. In contrast, large-scale provenance verification and subtle poisoning detection
require sustained monitoring, adversarial expertise, and significant computational resources. Although

===== PDF_PAGE 36 =====
26
emerging approaches, such as model editing and machine unlearning, may reduce the need for full
retraining, remediation of training data vulnerabilities remains operationally challenging because it
requires identifying, removing, and verifying the elimination of the affected behavior from the model
(Yan et al., 2024).
Tokenizer and Embedding Mitigations
The tokenizer and embedding layers jointly determine how raw inputs are transformed into
structured vector representations for downstream processing. Mitigation strategies focus on improving
representational stability and semantic separation. Robustness and stress testing can reveal
segmentation inconsistencies or encoding behaviors that distort meaning. At the embedding level,
regularization techniques, dimensional adjustments, and clustering validation help preserve
meaningful distinctions between concepts and reduce unintended proximity between unrelated inputs.
These interventions aim to stabilize how inputs are encoded and interpreted before higher-level
reasoning occurs (Pope and Patooghy, 2025).
These measures can reduce the effectiveness of evasion techniques that rely on representational
distortion or encoding tricks. They meaningfully improve stability and reduce sensitivity to malformed
inputs, thereby lowering deterministic manipulation risk at the input boundary. However, they do not
address higher-order reasoning flaws or objective misalignment within the model (Yang et al., 2024).
Feasibility is relatively favorable compared with core architectural interventions. Stress testing and
normalization pipelines can be implemented without retraining. Embedding-level modifications
require retraining but are typically less costly than transformer-level changes. Because this layer sits
near the system boundary, iterative testing and refinement are operationally tractable.
Transformer Mitigations
The transformer architecture and its learned weights encode the statistical relationships derived
from training data. Mitigation strategies include sensitivity analysis to identify unstable behavioral
regions, interpretability techniques to analyze internal activations and attention mechanisms, and
privacy-preserving training methods to limit memorization of sensitive data. Additionally, inference-
time mechanisms, such as out-of-distribution detection and grounded generation, can constrain model
responses when inputs fall outside learned competence (Liu et al., 2024).

behavior rather than remove structural susceptibility. Nondeterminism and representational opacity

Feasibility at this level is constrained by computational cost and performance trade-offs.
Differential privacy and robust training methods often reduce model accuracy or increase training time
(Blanco-Justicia et al., 2023). Architectural changes require retraining at scale, which is expensive and
time-intensive. Inference time controls are easier to deploy but act as safeguards rather than fixing the
underlying problem (Li et al., 2024).
Reward Function Mitigations
Vulnerabilities that arise from reward design and optimization pressures require interventions at
the level of training objectives and evaluation criteria. Multi-objective training introduces competing
constraints into the optimization process to balance performance goals with safety and reliability
considerations. Context-aware fine-tuning enables the adaptation of model behavior to domain-
specific or deployment-specific requirements rather than relying solely on static training objectives
(Mao et al., 2024). Multiple evaluation metrics are used to prevent the model from optimizing only a
single narrow measure. Also, calibration techniques adjust the relationship between predicted outputs
and expressed confidence, aligning model certainty with empirical performance.
Effectiveness at this level primarily influences the integrity and scope dimensions. Expanding
objectives and calibrating confidence helps reduce systematic behavioral errors and some forms of
misalignment. However, mitigations at this level cannot fully eliminate the structural gap between
tractable optimization targets and complex human intent (Chhikara, 2025). Objective mismatch
remains an inherent limitation of large-scale supervised and reinforcement learning systems.
Feasibility is moderate. The technical mechanisms for multi-objective optimization and calibration
are established, but defining appropriate objectives requires continuous evaluation and organizational
coordination (Gupta et al., 2023). Deployment context may evolve faster than retraining cycles,
creating lag between optimization targets and operational needs.
Context Window and RAG Mitigations
A distinct class of mitigations applies at the system boundary during deployment. These measures
focus on structuring how the model interacts with users, tools, and external data sources. Prompt
separation mechanisms enforce clear distinctions between system instructions and user inputs (Zverev
et al., 2025). Context window controls, including truncation thresholds, regulate how much
information enters the active context window. Provenance tracking and attribution mechanisms
document the sources of contextual inputs to reduce semantic ambiguity. In RAG systems,
mitigations include separating retrieved content from generated content in outputs, scoring and
validating retrieved sources, and exposing retrieval attribution at inference time (Liu et al., 2026).
These architectural controls define trust boundaries between internal model reasoning and external
data sources.
These interventions can reduce the scope of exploitation by preventing contaminated information
from persisting or spreading across sessions or retrieval layers. By constraining context propagation

Feasibility is high relative to training-time controls. These mitigations are typically implemented
at the orchestration or API layer and do not require retraining. They can be deployed incrementally
and refined through monitoring. Trade-offs primarily involve latency, usability, and filtering precision
rather than architectural overhaul.

Chapter 7
Recommendations
Our findings highlight several structural characteristics of vulnerabilities in generative AI systems
that have implications for both system design and risk management. In particular, the analysis shows
that risk is unevenly distributed across AI system components, with higher concentrations of risk
appearing in training pipelines and user-facing inference interfaces. It also shows that many AI
vulnerabilities arise from probabilistic model behavior or architectural design choices rather than
deterministic software flaws that can be easily patched. The following recommendations build on
these observations and outline practical steps that developers, security practitioners, and standards
organizations can take to better manage vulnerabilities in generative AI systems.
Prioritize Security Controls at the Data Layers
Our analysis shows that the highest threat vulnerabilities concentrate at three components in the
AI life cycle: the training data, the context window, and the retrieval systems (RAG). Security efforts
should therefore prioritize these layers. Examples include stronger governance over training data
pipelines, dataset provenance tracking, and controls on inputs and retrieval pipelines at inference time
(Padovani, Anantharaj, and Fiore, 2025; Zeng et al., 2025).
Treat Certain AI Vulnerabilities as Structural Risks Rather Than as
Patchable Bugs
Many vulnerabilities identified in this analysis cannot be addressed through conventional patching
because they arise from structural properties of machine learning systems, such as probabilistic
behavior, representational compression, or misalignment between objectives and deployment contexts.
Organizations should therefore treat these weaknesses as persistent risk conditions, requiring layered
mitigations—such as monitoring, architectural safeguards, and operational constraints—rather than
relying solely on software updates (Dong et al., 2025).
Incorporate Component-Level Risk Assessments into AI System
Design
Traditional vulnerability management frameworks focus on individual flaws or attack techniques
(Aslan et al., 2023). Our findings suggest that risk should also be evaluated at the architectural
component level, where vulnerabilities tend to cluster and exhibit similar threat and impact
characteristics. Engineering teams should incorporate component-level risk assessments during system

===== PDF_PAGE 40 =====
30
design to identify where vulnerabilities are likely to emerge and should prioritize mitigation strategies
accordingly.
Develop Advanced Exploitation Detection and Monitoring
Capabilities for AI Systems
A significant portion of vulnerabilities identified in this analysis produce failures that are
practically infeasible to detect with existing monitoring approaches. Developing techniques for
detecting anomalous model behavior, adversarial inputs, and latent backdoor activation should
therefore be a research priority (Rao et al., 2026). Improved logging and evaluation frameworks would
help organizations identify exploitation attempts that would otherwise remain indistinguishable from
normal model behavior (Hohensinner et al., 2026).
Develop Evaluation Methods That Reflect Stochastic Exploitation
Because many AI vulnerabilities manifest stochastically rather than deterministically, traditional
security testing methods may fail to identify them. Security evaluation practices should therefore
incorporate stochastic testing, adversarial prompting, and large-scale behavioral evaluations to better
estimate the likelihood of exploitation and the range of possible outcomes (Kumar et al., 2025; Zizzo
et al., 2025). Although such methods cannot demonstrate the absence of vulnerabilities, they can
provide stronger empirical evidence regarding model behavior under adversarial conditions.
Establish Criteria for Cataloging AI Vulnerabilities
Since their introduction in 1999 and 2006, respectively, CVE and CWE have been the de facto
standards used by the international information security community to manage, mitigate, and track
vulnerabilities (Galhardo et al., 2020). However, these frameworks are being stressed in light of the
features that make up AI vulnerabilities, as identified in this analysis. Therefore, we propose four
criteria that should be resolved (one way or another) because the outcome will affect the cataloging of
AI vulnerabilities:
• Patchability. If a vulnerability is fundamentally unpatchable, should it still be CVE-able? On
one hand, one might argue that the primary purpose of the CVE catalog is to provide
actionable information so that network defenders can patch high-risk vulnerabilities, and
including unpatchable vulnerabilities simply clutters the catalog and confuses users. On the
other hand, it is only through cataloging these vulnerabilities that they can be tracked and
mitigated, even if not fully patchable.
• Prevalence. If a vulnerability exists in all AI models, should it still be cataloged? The concern
here is that if some of these vulnerabilities are inherent to all generative AI models, where is
the value of enumerating each one individually?
• Reproducibility. Given that some AI vulnerabilities occur probabilistically, what should be
the degree of reproducibility to validate the presence of and therefore catalog a vulnerability?

===== PDF_PAGE 41 =====
31
There is an argument to be made that there should be a minimum bar for reproducibility
because, absent this threshold, the CVE Numbering Authority or any other user would have
no verifiable proof of the existence of the vulnerability.
• Model outputs. When considering assignment of a CVE ID, should CIA violations apply to
only model inputs (i.e., training or system data) or also to model outputs? This property
becomes particularly important for misalignment vulnerabilities (e.g., hallucinations,
scheming) that may simply be considered artifacts of a properly functioning model.

Chapter 8
Conclusion
In this report, we have presented a structured, vulnerability-centric framework for understanding
and managing security risks in generative AI systems. By decomposing model architectures into
components, from data ingestion and tokenization through embedding, transformer layers, and
deployment interfaces, we identified 31 distinct classes of vulnerabilities that differ fundamentally
from traditional software flaws. These weaknesses often arise from probabilistic learning dynamics,
data composition, and optimization objectives rather than deterministic code errors, making many of
them only partially patchable.
The analysis demonstrates that the highest risks occur at two points in the AI life cycle: during
training, in which poisoned or unverified data can embed persistent weaknesses, and at inference, in
which exposed interfaces enable prompt injection and other evasion attacks. The proposed threat and
impact metrics provide a reproducible basis for comparing vulnerabilities and integrating AI-specific
risk into existing standards, such as CVE and CWE. Mitigation strategies emphasize data governance,
architectural boundary enforcement, and continuous monitoring rather than conventional patching.
Overall, the findings highlight the need for policy frameworks that treat AI vulnerabilities as
structural properties of learning systems, requiring coordinated technical, organizational, and
governance responses to ensure the secure and trustworthy deployment of generative AI technologies.

Explanation of Threat and Impact
Scores
Scores for threat and impact, by AI system component, are shown in Tables B.1 and B.2,
respectively. These scores are derived from the rubrics described in Chapter 5, which evaluate
vulnerabilities across five threat dimensions (accessibility, exploitability, determinism, resource
requirements, and patchability) and five impact dimensions (confidentiality, integrity, availability,
scope, and detectability). The tables summarize how the structural characteristics of each component
influence the likelihood and potential severity of exploitation across the vulnerabilities identified in
this report. Because comprehensive empirical data do not exist for many AI vulnerability classes,
scoring relied on expert judgment informed by published research, documented incidents, and
technical analysis of the underlying vulnerabilities.
