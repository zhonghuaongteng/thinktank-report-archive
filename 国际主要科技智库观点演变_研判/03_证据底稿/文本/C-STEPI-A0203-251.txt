1. Introduction

During the global financial crisis and the slowdown of the worl d economy, Indonesia
m a i n t a i n e d r e l a t i v e l y h i g h G D P g r o w t h , a v e r a g i n g 5 . 9 % b e t w e e n 2009 and 2013 (OECD,
2014). Despite the weak global co ntext and lower export prices, growth has remained
relatively robust. Indonesia’s Gross Domestic Product (GDP) was 1015.54 billion US dollars
in 2017 (OECD, 2015), representin g 1.64 percent of the world ec onomy (OECD, 2015;
OECD, 2016). Indonesia h as made great stride s in improving its economic and social
outcomes. Note, however, that the industry in Indonesia is mainly composed of primary
industries such as agriculture, f orestry, and fisheries (Wangdo ng Kim et al., 2018).
Industrial advancement through fo reign capital, etc. in this co untry was also slow
compared to other ASEAN countries such as Thailand, Cambodia, and Singapore (CRDS,
2015). As shown in Figure 1-2, the main science and innovation indices of Indonesia are
lower than the median values of OECD, except the network, cluster, and transfer indices.
The poverty issue is also being addressed by expanding various social programs, although
food policies protect the most vulnerable well. The Indonesian government is strongly
pushing for technological innovation policies for continuous growth via advanced industry
and poverty eradication (Wangdong Kim et al., 2018).
During the 1980s to the 1990s, Indonesia implemented ambitious S&T policies, developing
specific industries from aircraf t to shipbuilding through its s tate-owned corporations.
Having enough financial resource especially from oil money, the Indonesian government
had no difficulty mobilizing resources to fund the development of the so-called "strategic
industries." Technically, these efforts have led to technological progress, especially in the

aircraft industry. Starting from manufacturing licensed aircraf t, state-owned aircraft
c o m p a n y I P T N ( c u r r e n t l y k n o w n a s P T D I ) p r o c e e d e d t o d e v e l o p a i rcraft jointly with
Spanish aircraft company CASA. Finally, Indonesia pushed its am bition even further by
developing its own designed aircraft, N250, a twin-engine aircraft that can accommodate
50 passengers. Nonetheless, the success in engineering has not been accompanied by the
success in business, which shows that technological development is much more than just
creating technological artifact.
Despite the current position in S&T development, Indonesia has a relatively long history of
research. The richness of its biodiversity has attracted overseas researchers for more than
two hundred years. The Dutch colonial government has established research institutions
to explore and exploit its rich natural resources (Goss, 2011). After the country gained its
independence, the research institutions were steadily taken ove r by local researchers.
While buildings could be easily being taken over and occupied, however, the same could
not be said for knowledge and scientific culture.
The missing link of knowledge and scientific culture during the transition from Dutch
researchers to their Indonesian counterparts cannot be solely blamed for the current S&T
condition of the country. Countri es such as South Korea, even w ithout a rich S&T
institution heritage from their colonial government, could also achieve technological
progress.
A s a m a t t e r o f f a c t , I n d o n e s i a h a s m a i n t a i n e d i t s l o w R & D i n t e nsity in the past. R&D
intensity was falling steadily from 0.54% in 1982 to around 0.1 4% of GDP in 2013. The
government sector contributes to around 80% of national R&D activity, and the rest is the
share of the private sector (Pappiptek-LIPI, 2013, 2011, 2007, 2006). The private industrial
sector mostly generated innovation through learning without formal R&D. It suggests that
Indonesia had been less successful i n d e v e l o p i n g S T I c a p a c i t y in the past, including
intellectual capital formation, knowledge mastery accumulation, technical capabilities
acquisition, and STI policy governance. Low STI capacity resulted in low absorptive and
learning capacity by actors in the system of innovation (Schot, J. and Steinmueller, W.E.,
2018).

The system of innovation may not work effectively due to proble ms in the elements of
i n n o v a t i o n s y s t e m a n d i t s f u n c t i o n i n g . A k e y e l e m e n t o f t h e i n novation system is
i n s t i t u t i o n , w h e r e i n s t i t u t i o n a l f a i l u r e c r e a t e s a s y s t e m i c p r oblem in the system of
innovation. Creating an adequate institutional framework facili tates collective learning
among actors of those elements of the innovation system (Intara kumnerd, P. and
Chaminade, C., 2007). The collective learning among actors is more than just R&D activities
b u t f o c u s e s m o r e o n S T I c a p a c i t y building and networking. In sh o r t , t h e r o l e o f t h e
institution is important in promoting STI and R&D governance.
S T I p o l i c y a n d R & D g o v e r n a n c e a r e e s s e n t i a l l y a b o u t S T I c a p a c i ty building, which deals
with the problems of building of scientist and engineer competency, physical technological
infrastructure, entrepreneurial and managerial capacity, and sc ientific knowledge
accumulation through R&D. STI capacity building is expected to solve the pressing social
and economic problems of the country, transform its societies, and positively impact the
standards of living and quality of life (Watkins, A. and Ehst, M., 2008).
Discussing STI policy issues and R&D governance problems in Ind o n e s i a h a s b e e n
documented in literature -- Sigurdson, J. and Polanka, K. (2002), OECD (2012), OECD (2012),
Naudé, W. (2013), and ADB (2019), among others. STI policy issues and R&D governance
problems need a “fitting way” (w ith the Indonesian STI realities ) o f t h i n k i n g t o p r o v i d e
policy alternatives and governance solutions.
The objective of this study is to find the way forward in promo t i n g S T I a n d R & D
governance in Indonesia and prov ide policy consultation based o n the lessons of the
Korean experience. This report will discuss the mid-/long-term STI policy of Indonesia and
the governance and institutional aspects of its S&T development while comparing with the
Korean experience of STIP and R&D governance system.
The discussion of S&T governance in Indonesia should proceed in a d i f f e r e n t d i r e c t i o n
from that in developed countries. In Europe, for example, people are no longer concerned
with advancing S&T development and industrial utilization since S&T has been developed

2. Objectives
The project is titled "STEPI-L IPI Project 2019 Policy Consultation on STI Policy and R&D
Governance.” Before the STEPI-LIPI Project 2019, STEPI signed an MOU with LIPI in 2015 to
strengthen cooperation. This project was developed based on the MOU signed by STEPI
and LIPI in 2015 to strengthen co operation between the two orga nizations. STEPI will
operate the STI Policy Program ( STIP) in partnership with LIPI. Indonesia is also one of
Korea's major partner countries in development cooperation (Wangdong Kim et al., 2018).
The Science and Technology Policy Institute (STEPI) is one of t he government-funded
research institutes in Korea and a major leading global think tank conducting research on
policy issues in science, techno logy, and innovation. STEPI wor ks closely with various
ministries, regional governments, and relevant institutes to support policy design. It also
has a long experience in providing STI policy consultation for developing countries, and it
recently launched the International Innovation Cooperation Center (IICC) with the aim of
conducting STI development cooperation activities with partner countries. The program
duration is from January 2, 2019 to December 31, 2019(Wangdong Kim et al., 2018).
T h e I n d o n e s i a n I n s t i t u t e o f S c i e n c e s ( L I P I ) i s a n o n - m i n i s t e r i al government research
institution reporting to the President under the auspices of the Ministry of Research and
Technology. The vision of LIPI i s to be a world-class research institution in research,
development, and use of science to improve the nation’s competi tiveness. In order to
achieve said mission of the institution and to strengthen the capacity of the agency, LIPI
has requested the Science and Te chnology Policy Institute (STEPI ) t o e v a l u a t e t h e S T I
p o l i c y a n d i t s i m p l e m e n t a t i o n i n Indonesia and also review the STP policy and
management (Wangdong Kim et al., 2018).

4.2 Condition and consequence of low R&D intensity
R&D intensity is a main indicator for judging a country’s STI governance. Low R&D intensity
is generally related to low or weak STI indicators including intellectual capital formation,
knowledge mastery accumulation, technical capabi lities acquisit ion, and STI policy
governance. The following section explains the condition and co nsequence of low R&D
intensity in Indonesia.

government attention on R&D; and (iii) continuously low share of private R&D intensity.

products in the economy as shown by the large portion taken up by the LMT industry in
industrial sectors (Aminullah, 2011; 2012).

industry produces low-technology products that do not require R &D facilities.

1.3 More consideration to public sector research
Public sector research organizations have been developed in many countries to achieve
national or regional goals, develop the strategies of science, technology, and innovation
policy, or to solve social and po litical problems, through rese arch. They produce
knowledge and services as crucial elements of national research and innovation systems
and of the public services governments deliver to people and society. They have, however,
been understood and configured in some different theoretical rationales which result in
different strategies, governance structures, funding schemes and management styles etc.
P u b l i c s e c t o r r e s e a r c h o r g a n i z a t i o n s a r e , a s a w h o l e , a n a r e a where complicated

various approaches to them. This thesis showed that three diffe rent conceptual
approaches to public sector rese arch organizations based on dif ferent focuses (namely
institutions, activities, and a networked system) and reviewed the various theoretical
rationales for public research organizations (namely linear model approach, market failure
approach, government failure approach, and system or network failure approach). Those
different perceptions and rationales provide different objectives, strategies, governance
structure, funding schemes and management tools in public sector research organizations,
all of which are interactively connected to one another, shaping a public sector research
organizations’ regime.
Like any other organizations, public sector research organizations themselves always try to
change their instrumental settings such as institutional objectives, strategies to achieve
the objectives, internal governance, funding priorities for the ir R&D and management
t o o l s i n o r d e r b o t h t o c o n d u c t t h e i r R & D a n d o p e r a t e i n s t i t u t i ons better. Such kinds of
changes usually take the form of incremental change which alters their settings partially by

within the same or similar theoretical rationales.

sector research organizations become unstable and a government policy can change the
settings abruptly and lead the whole aspects of public sector research organizations into
non-incremental transformation (Ko, Y.J., 2005). The non-incremental change is usually
initiated not by reference to evaluations of the stages in an existing policy process or policy
