The EU's Horizon Europe framework programme promotes European 
innovation. The current Horizon Europe programme uses 'Widening' 
measures to target Member States , support their capacity to 
innovate, close gaps between EU countries  and move towards  
performance convergence. This study provides a framework and 
associated indicator options for establishing a systemic 
'Widening 2.0' methodology which could prove instrumental in 
discussing post-Horizon Europe 'w idening' support measures. It is 
based on assess ment of  research and innovation indicators  of 
targeted 'widening' countries, looking at the economic and research 
impact that Horizon Europe may have had between 2018 and 2025.

Executive summary 
Despite broad consensus that science, research, and innovation are vital for Europe 's future 
prosperity and security, the EU 's innovation system is failing to deliver. The gap with the U nited 
States is deepening and China has overtaken the EU -27, alongside weak convergence among 
Member States. Evidence shows that this lag stems less from public research and innovation ( R&I) 
funding than from weak business  sector innovation, with Europe struggling to translate strong 
science into globally successful firms – an increasingly serious weakness in a fast-moving, artificial 
intelligence ( AI)-driven and geopolitically competitive innovation landscape. While boosting EU 
innovation capacity is now a central policy priority and debates focus on increasing public funding, 
a key unresolved challenge remains how to balance excellence- based funding w ith broader 
participation, given persistent disparities in research and development ( R&D) and innovation 
performance across Member States. 
The 'Widening participation and spreading excellence actions' introduced in the previous framework 
programmes have been supporting Member States in improving their capacities to adopt and adapt 
new technologies. These initiatives aimed to strengthen research institutions by facilitating 
networking, partnerships with leading institutions, and recruitment of skilled sta ff. Although it is 
premature to evaluate the full impact of these 'Widening measures', recent assessments show that 
these are likely to deliver some early evidence of success stories. However, there seem to be 
persistent barriers for participation of 'Widening' countries and impacts are highly heterogeneous, 
often depending on complementary national efforts. Genuine sustainable change tends to depend 
on R&I investment and reforms at national level, challenging the attraction of international 
researchers, and long-term sustainability through effects generated from research results. 
The EU's next framework programme for research and innovation will continue to support 'Widening' 
measures aimed at reducing R&D performance gap s between Member States, ( European 
Commission communication of 4  July 2025). According to the draft proposal, the 'Widening'  
programme should focus on delivering ' concrete measures ' for building the R&D capacity of 
countries that have been lagging in terms of participation in Horizon Europe. More emphasis will be 
placed on incentivi sing them to catch up, e.g. by mon itoring and recogni sing efforts in their 
commitment to using national strategies to benefit from additional EU support, tailoring access to 
'Widening' funding schemes on this commitment and by using the European Semester process for 
monitoring. It could also involve a larger share of structural funds invested in R&I . Consideration is 
also given to splitting eligible countries into categories, depending on their position in their advance 
towards catching up. 
The overall aim of this study is to provide a framework for establishing a systemic ' Widening 2.0' 
methodology, which will be instrumental when discussing ' Widening' support measures once the  
Horizon Europe framework programme ends. It discusses new or improved existing metrics which 
can be used for assessing which Member States to select for which support measures, considering 
both the needs, but also the capacity for effectively 'catching up'. Overall, the proposed framework 
allows for sound policy option s when discussing new R&I support programme  structures for 
'Widening'. The report proposes options for possible different choices, in which case their respective 
strengths and weaknesses are discussed.  
The study used the latest 2025 version of a broad tool for assessing a country's innovation capacity 
- the European Innovation Scoreboard (EIS) developed by the European Commission 's Joint 
Research Centre (JRC). The EIS variables were used as indicators for the various dimensions 
composing a country's margin for innovation capacity building, complemented, where relevant, with 
other sources. The dimensions for ' Absorptive capacity ', 'Access to technology ', and ' Creative 
capacity' (multiple dimensions) allowed the classification of 'Widening' countries into five categories 
of 'Potential for Innovation Capacity' (PIC).

Two principles guided the underlying analysis. First, as the analysis has shown the importance of 
'systemic' performance, countries need to perform well on all the prerequisites for innovation 
capacity building. Second, when defining benchmark levels for scores, the study considered the 
position of countries on their innovation  development path, i.e. scores will be compared with the 
overall 'Widening' benchmark. 
The analysis illustrates that addressing new 'Widening' measures to unleash the innovation capacity 
of lagging countries to catch up is a complex challenge that requires more than simply addressing 
participation in EU f ramework programmes. It requires considering the heterogeneity among 
'Widening' countries in their position and potential for innovation capacity building. Continued 
'Widening' support could use the proposed PIC classification to tailor 'Widening' instruments 
according to identified Member St ate bottlenecks and weak spots. Hence, the focus could be not 
only on supporting participation in framework programmes  in general, but on supporting 
participation in those programme s/pillars, tackling a country 's weaknesses, and which measures 
have the best scope for improving a country's PIC.

1. The background to the 'Widening' EU policy scene 
There is no lack of reports these days arguing how important science, research and innovation are 
to take Europe into a prosperous, clean, safe and healthy future for its citizens. 'It is investment in 
research and innovation that will ensure Europe's (and its Member States') future security, strength 
and freedom and thus Europe's ability to successfully navigate in turbulent times' (Draghi (2024)1). 
Yet, the EU's innovation system is currently not in shape to deliver. The evidence showing how big 
the gap has become between EU's research and innovation system with the US and China is not new 
(e.g. Veugelers (2024 )2), but the Draghi (2024)  report proved to be a loud wake- up call.  The 
innovation gap relative to the US is 'Widening' rather than shrinking, and China has managed to 
overtake the EU27. Together with the lacking EU catching up worldwide, there has been very slow 
convergence intra EU27, hampering EU's overall innovation performance. 
A deeper dive into the evidence reminds us that the major source of EU's persistent lagging behind 
and failure to catch up with the US is not so much in its public R&I funding, but in its business sector 
R&I. While Europe may have nurtured pockets of great science, it typically succeeds less in turning 
them into innovative corporate successes, compared to the US, and more recently China. This has 
been a long -diagnosed challenge for the EU, which policy does  not seem  able to address.  This 
missing dynamic business innovation has become even more critical in the current fast changing and 
highly competitive global AI powered innovation environment.  In addition, the global innovation 
scene is increasingly being characteri sed by a race between US and China for technology 
sovereignty and dominance, protected by domestic fences.  
Powering EU's innovation capacity has become centr al in current EU policy priority discussions.  
Reports of various stakeholders preparing their position on the upcoming new EU budget for 
Research, FP10 (e.g. the Heitor (2024)
3 report), complemented the momentum. Most of the policy 
discussion calls for an increase in public funding, although there is no consensus between Member 
States on whether and how to support R&D  through EU-level instruments or through coordination 
of instruments at the level of Member States. 
Considering evidence indicating weak or no convergence in R&D and innovative performance across 
Member States , the question is how to reconcile broader participation in the public and private 
R&D&I ecosystems in the EU,  with excellence-based public and private selection.  Differences in 
R&D&I performance create a divide that affects participation in public programme s such as the 
Framework Programmes (FPs) where funding is awarded based on excellence. Lower participation 
further limits underperforming countries' ability to improve their research and innovation systems.  
The ''Widening' Participation and Spreading Excellence actions' introduced in the current FP Horizon 
Europe (HEU), is targeted at supporting Member States in improving their capacities to adopt and 
adapt new technologies. These initiatives aimed to strengthen research institutions by facilitating 
networking, partnerships with leading institutions, and recruitment of skilled staff.  
Although it is premature to evaluate the full impact of these ' 'Widening' measures', several recent 
studies, e.g. by the EC itself and the European Court of Auditors (ECOA) have assessed the changes 
brought by the initiative (ECOA, 2022)4. Although this evidence shows that the measures seem to

2. This report:  Developing a framework for a 'Widening' 
methodology 
Evaluating and monitoring of 'Widening' measures requires an effects-based approach entailing ex-
ante and ex-post micro and macro assessments of long - and short -run impacts of specific 
instruments and of the FP portfolio in general.  To what extent does  ''Widening' Participation and 
Spreading Excellence' unleash the innovation capacity of lagging Member States? The relevant key 
performance indicator to use is the contribution of the portfolio of instruments to nurture the next 
scientific and technology ideas that will boost business innovation and growth. 
In the light of the ongoing discussions on the role of ' 'Widening' measures' in the next FP10 (2028-
2034) or its alternatives (e.g. European Competitiveness Fund )6, it is important to reassess and 
update existing metrics defining the distribution of these measures in the R&I support programme  
after HEU.  
The overall aim of this study is to provide a framework for establishing a systemic ' 'Widening' 2.0' 
methodology, which will be instrumental when discussing ''Widening'' support measures after HEU. 
It will discuss new or improved existing metrics (''Widening' 2.0' indicator) used for assessing which 
Member States to select for which ' 'Widening'' support measures, considering both the need for 
''Widening'' support, but also the capacity for effectively 'catching up'.  
The framework for such a systemic ' 'Widening' 2.0' methodology will focus on indicators that are  
relevant, measurable, achievable, and time-bound: 
• The methodology should be multidimensional, building on various metrics involving 
Science, Research, Technology, Innovation, Business Structure, Productivity. 
• The methodology will be relevant for an ex-ante assessment of strength and 
weaknesses in R&D&I of Member States as well as for an assessment of the capacity 
for catching -up through 'Widening' (openness, absorptive capacity, creative 
capacity). Consequently, metrics should go beyond R&I investment level as % of GDP 
(total, public, private) to also include metrics such as number of people and skills 
trained for R&D&I, diffusion/connectedness measures such as metrics of researchers' 
mobility and cooperation.  
• The framework and associated indicator options will consider the dynamic dimension 
of metrics that are used to assess initial positions, as well as trends and patterns of 
growth/catching up.  
• The framework should consider (trends in) Horizon participation metrics. 
• The framework will critically assess its composite dimensions, with appropriate 
differentiation of dimensions when needed. 
Overall, the proposed framework will allow for sound policy options when discussing new R&I 
support programme structures for ''Widening''. The report will propose options for possible different 
choices, in which case their respective strengths and weaknesses will be discussed.  The proposed 
framework will also be useful for future bigger in-depth studies for evaluating R&D&I capacity 
development across Member States and time.

3. Trends in EU science, technology and innovation  
performance and cohesion  
The innovation capacity of nations measures their ability to generate new ideas and to translate 
them into economic growth and prosperity (Furman et al, 2002) 7. Because of differences in initial 
conditions and because of differences in how EU countries have sought to create innovation-based 
growth, we can expect substantial differences between European countries in terms of innovation 
capacity. We would however expect that the process of EU integration would allow lagging countries 
to catch up faster, pushing convergence within the EU in terms of innovation capacity, along with 
economic convergence.  
A broad tool for assessing a country 's innovation capacity is the European Innovation Scoreboard 
(EIS) developed by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC).

indicators (32 in recent editions), but the overall score for each country is represented by Summary 
Innovation Indicator (SII).

in innovation capacity, as well as trends in the cohesion within the EU-27 on this indicator.

SII score relative to the rest of the world, is showing, in line with Draghi report et al, how EU is losing 
in relative position in innovative performance. US and China are all scoring consistently better than

4. The 'Widening' programme in Horizon Europe 
With its dedicated 'Widening' participation and spreading excellence' programme in its Framework 
Programme, the Commission has the objective to address the cohesion within the EU on innovative 
performance, by 'Widening' the participation in the Framework Programme of lagging countries and 
to improve their capacities in research and innovation, as well as to support the reforms of national 
policies, within an overall objective of strengthening the European Research Area. 
Countries which are targeted ' 'Widening' countries', are less performing in terms of research and 
innovation with respect to the average EU 27.  Within the Horizon Europe regulation 'Widening'  
countries are: Bulgaria (BG), Croatia (HR), Cyprus (CY), Czech Republic (CZ), Estonia (EE), Greece 
(EL), Hungary  (HU), Latvia (LV), Lithuania (LT), Malta (MT), Poland (PL), Portugal (PT), Romania 
(RO), Slovakia (SK), Slovenia (SI) and all associated countries with equivalent characteristics in terms 
of R&I performance and the Outermost Regions. 
The actions within this ' 'Widening'' objective are mainly  Coordination and Support Actions,  i.e. 
funding schemes sustaining activities that contribute to the objectives of Horizon Europe 
Programme, normally excluding research and innovation activities. All organisations eligible in 
Horizon Europe can participate in the 'Widening' actions but only organisations based in 'Widening' 
countries can participate as coordinators. Its main types of actions are: 
• Teaming: action of support to the creation of new centres of excellence or the upgrade of 
existing ones, located in 'Widening' countries, building on partnerships at European level.  
At least two parties must be involved in the action, a legal entity established in a 'Widening' 
country operating as coordinator and, a counterparty, a university or research organisation, 
internationally recognised for excellence in research and innovation, established in another 
EU Member State or Associated Country. 
• Twinning: supports joint research projects, new or ongoing, aimed at strengthening 
scientific excellence and innovation capacity in a defined research area as well as the 
scientific quality of the partners involved in the collaborations. Under this action, a research 
institution located in a 'Widening' country act as coordinator in a partnership with at least 
two other internationally leading institutions/bodies located in other Member States or 
Associated Countries. 
• Pathways to Synergies: This action supports beneficiaries of regional funding to increase 
cross-border collaborations and reduce access barriers to different funding systems.  
• Hop-on Facility;  This action aims to facilitate 'Widening' countries' research institutions 
joining already ongoing Horizon R&I projects.

6. Drivers for innovation capacity and for capacity for 
catching up: A short review of the literature 
What can we learn from the literature in terms of heterogeneity in innovative capacity and 
convergence in innovative capacity? (see also Veugelers & Schweiger (2016), Veugelers (2010) a.o.).  
An important dimension explaining the heterogeneity across countries in their innovation capacity, 
is differences in levels of initial development and a country's initial position relative to the technology 
frontier (Verspagen (1991)12; Aghion & Howitt (1998)13).  
For countries at still early stages of development, far from the technology frontier, the contribution 
of technology to growth runs through the country 's ability to effectively absorb new technologies 
(World Bank (2008)14; Lall (2002)15). There are two key ingredients explaining differences across 
countries in effective technology take- up. The first is access to foreign technology . This requires 
openness through trade, foreign direct investment (FDI) and other forms of international 
cooperation. Second, access to (foreign) knowledge needs to be combined with a sufficiently 
developed 'absorptive capacity ' (Cohen and Levinthal ( 1989)
16) or ' social capability ' 
(Abramovitz (1986)17), in order to deliver growth. This absorptive capacity  depends on many 
factors, including the extent to which a country: has a technologically -literate workforce and a 
highly-skilled elite; promotes a pro-investment climate; permits the creation and expansion of firms 
using higher -technology processes; permit s access to capital; and has adequate public -sector 
institutions to promote the take-up of critical technologies when private demand or market forces 
prove inadequate (World Bank (2008)). 
For countries at higher levels of development, closer to the technology frontier, another factor that 
explains heterogeneity in innovation capacity comes into play, namely divergence in countries' own 
indigenous innovative capacity (Hoekman et al (2006)
18). First, a country's own R&D complements 
the take -up of existing technology because it is a component of absorptive capacity. Foreign 
technologies frequently need modifi cation so that they are suitable for domestic circumstances 
(Acemoğlu et al. (2006)19). But, at higher levels of development, a country 's own R&D can 
increasingly substitute adoption of existing technologies, allowing the generation of new 
technologies, pushing the technology frontier. At this stage, countries require technological know-
how, reflected in public and private R&D resources. They also need to be able to incentivise or 
reward innovation (e. g. Furman et al  (2002)
20). In well -functioning product markets , that are

To summarise, a set of factors, shaping the country's capacity for a virtuous innovation growth link, 
need to be present. In addition to R&D, technology and IT infrastructure, these additional factors 
include access to large markets, (international) openness of markets, competition, access to a highly 
educated and skilled population (especially tertiary  level), well-developed financial markets and, 
quality research institutions. It is important to note that these factors should not be seen in isolation, 
but as part of a system of key prerequisites for innovation capacity building.  
The relative importance of each these factors in the required system depends on the level of initial 
development and a country 's initial position relative to the technology frontier. We can therefore 
expect substantial heterogeneity among European countries, because of different capacities to put 
in place a virtuous innovation growth ecosystem, but also because of differences in initial conditions 
requiring differently composed ecoinnovation systems.

7. Indicators for assessing the potential of countries to catch 
up on innovation capacity 
Following the literature (see sections above), the conditions influencing innovative capacity building 
can be divided into those affecting access to technology, those affecting firms ' capacity to adopt 
and fully understand existing technologies, and those affecting the ability to create innovations new 
to markets. The importance of each of them will depend on differences in the position of a country 
on its path to development.21 
Access to technology depends on a country 's economic openness, the availability and use of ICT 
infrastructure and the extent to which openness (to FDI and trade) facilitates the transfer of 
technology.  
Absorptive capacity is underpinned by the quality of secondary and undergraduate education and 
the effectiveness of on-the-job training. 
What matters for creative capacity is: the quality of postgraduate education; the availability of highly 
qualified scientists and engineers; the quality of the public research infrastructure; effective 
cooperation between public research and industry; the protection of intellectual property an d the 
availability of risk financing. Creative capacity is further underpinned by favorable incentives to 
engage in building innovative capacity . Business environment conditions for innovation include 
broader business environment conditions (or general framework conditions). Such conditions affect 
the operations and decisions of all firms in the economy, including firms that innovate. Business 
environment conditions include the quality of institutions and the functioning of product, labour and 
financial markets.

dimensions composing a county 's capacity for innovation capacity building.  For a detailed 
description of the variables included, we refer to the EIS website.

and continued to develop further as Estonia progressed along its innovation development path.

innovation policy is further illustrated by the consistent spending on public R&D which was able to

9. An assessment of the potential of 'Widening' countries for 
innovation capacity building  
In this final section, we classify all 15 'Widening' countries on their position on the innovation 
development path and on their potential for innovation capacity building.  The evaluation of their 
potential for innovation capacity building will be dependent on the initial position of the country.  
'Widening' countries need to perform on the prerequisites for innovation capacity building. Two 
principles guide our analysis in this respect. First, as the analysis has shown the importance of 
'systemic' performance, countries need to perform well on all  of the prerequisites for innovation 
capacity building. Second, when defining benchmark levels for scores, we will take into account the 
position of countries on their innovation  development path, ie scores will be compared with the 
overall 'Widening' benchmark.  
Compared to taking the average across all EU27 countries, our procedure reduces the weight of the 
creative capacity prerequisites, as most of the 'Widening' countries are still far from the technology 
frontier.  
A country is judged as having a bottleneck if it scores at the minimum value of all 'Widening'  
countries (see '-' in Table 12). A weak spot is defined as a score below the average for all 'Widening' 
(see '0' in Table12).  
If we would use as an algorithm (Option 1) that a 'Widening' country with potential for innovative 
capacity building cannot have more than one bottleneck, not more than two weak spots and cannot 
accumulate with one bottleneck one other weak spot, the following countries would pass on these 
criteria: Estonia, Malta, Slovenia, Portugal, and the Czech Republic. 
Among these, Slovenia has no weak spots; Estonia has only one weak spot, but this is on the Trade 
components in Effects, not on Productivity.  
Malta also has Effects as a weak spot, but not on Productivity. Malta however also has a bottleneck 
on Finance. If we use as algorithm (Option 2) that a 'Widening' country with potential for innovative 
capacity cannot have a bottleneck, Malta would drop out of this group.

10. Participation of 'Widening' countries in the EU 's 
Framework Programme and their Potential for Innovation 
Capacity 
Having constructed a classification of 'Widening' countries on their position on innovation capacity 
and on their potential for innovation capacity building and catching up, we match this classification 
with their patterns of particiption in Horizon Europe.  
For success rates in HEU (see also Table 2), all 'Widening' countries score below the EU27 average, 
warranting their status as 'Widening'.  Among the 'Widening' countries, there is no clear correlation 
between their PIC status and their HEU success rate.  
But if we look at the application behavior (relative to the population of S&E graduates) there is a 
clear correlation with their PIC score (see Table 14 below) . When looking among 'Widening' 
countries, countries with a higher PIC have on average a higher tendency to apply, taking into 
account their relevant size. Countries at the bottom of the PIC ranking, with the exception of Latvia, 
have a lower tendency to apply. This holds also for the s ize of HEU funding received related to the 
country's GERD. This suggests that inducing 'Widening' countries to participate in HEU goes hand 
in hand with a stronger PIC.  
Romania, Poland and Hungary are 'Widening' countries which have the lowest application rates 
(relative to the population of S&E graduates). But at the same time they also have the lowest success 
rates in HEU, culminating in the lowest HEU funding received per GERD. For Romania, this is 
correlated with its low scoring on innovation capacity. Also Bulgaria, the other low PIC scoring 
'Widening' country, scores at the bottom of the 'Widening'  countries for applications, has below 
average success rates, culminating in bottom performance in HEU funding received per GERD. For 
Poland and Hungary, this is related to their several weak spots on PIC, among which the quality of 
their Research Systems and Innovative Enterprises. All these countries are facing public budg et 
deficit problems, exceeding the 3% target, making it difficult to mobili se national funding for 
improving their potential for innovation capacity building. Particularly Bulgaria and Romania will rely 
quite heavily on HEU particpation and funding, to unl ock their access to finance as bottleneck for 
innovation capacity building.  
Latvia also has a low score on potential for innovation capacity building with several bottlenecks 
(quality of its research system and innovation active enterprises) and several weak spots. 
Nevertheless, it has an above average application behavior, an abo ve average success rate, 
culminating in an above average HEU funding received per GERD. Latvia does not exceed the 3% 
budget deficit target. Lifting Latvia's potential for innovation capacity building through ''Widening'' 
requires thinking beyond increasing Latvia's participation in HEU.  
Countries at the top of the PIC ranking can gradually be phased out of the 'Widening' programme, 
conditional on them being able to sustain their growth trend in Innovation Capacity Building and 
conditional on addressing any small weak points they still might have.  
The analysis illustrates that addressing the ' 'Widening' Participation and Spreading Excellence ' to 
unleash the innovation capacity of lagging countries to catch up, is a complex challenge that requires 
more than simply addressing participation in EU 's Framework Programmes. It requires taking into 
account the heterogeneity among 'Widening' countries in their position and potential for innovation 
capacity building.

11. 'Widening' index options 
Based on Tables 12, 13 and 14, several options for 'Widening' countries support in the next FP could 
be considered. 
For all three options and regardless number of selected countries, it is very important to note that 
future 'Widening' measures should not only on support participation in FP in general, but specifically 
target those programmes/pillars tackling a country 's weaknesses with the best scope for PIC 
improvement. 
'Widening' index option 1: Strict scenario 
The first scenario omits the  top five best-performing 'Widening' countries. Using an algorithm to 
select 'Widening' countries with potential for innovative capacity building according to the criteria 
that: they do not have more than one bottleneck, have no more than two weak spots and do not 
accumulate one bottleneck with one other weak spot; the following countries would pass: Czechia, 
Estonia, Malta, Portugal and Slovenia. Among these, Slovenia has no weak spots; Estonia has only 
one weak spot (on the trade components under 'effects', not on productivity). These countries can 
be gradually phased out of the ' Widening' support, on the condition that they will sustain their 
innovation capacity building growth trend and that they will address any persisting weak points. 
'Widening' index option 2: Moderate scenario 
The second scenario loosens the above selection criteria. Using a more conservative algorithm  to 
select 'Widening' countries with potential for innovative capacity that do not have a bottleneck, 
Malta would drop out of the top group , as it has a bottleneck on f inance. Only Czechia, Estonia, 
Portugal and Slovenia. would therefore remain in the best-performing group. As in Option 1, these 
countries can be gradually phased out of 'Widening' support.  
'Widening' index option 3: Inclusive scenario 
The third scenario would retain all 'Widening' countries within the 'Widening' support. All 'Widening' 
countries still score below the EU -27 average, when cross -referencing their PIC ranking with the 
success rate of their HEU funding applications, confirming their status as 'Widening'. While there is 
no clear correlation between ' Widening' countries and  their PIC status, ranking currently 
corresponds (except for Greece and Latvia) to the Horizon Europe funding received per their gross 
domestic expenditure on research and d evelopment (GERD). Continued support could use PIC 
ranking to tailor widening instruments according to bottlenecks and weak spots  for identified 
Member States.

12. Concluding recommendations 
Based on our analysis of the evidence on the innovation development and HEU involvement of 
'Widening' countries, we propose the following recommendations, very much in line with the draft 
proposal on (European Commission, 4 July 2025 communication).  
• Call for differentiating among 'Widening'  along their position in the  innovation capacity 
development path and depending on their capacity to further develop (see PIC). 
• This differentiating should be translated into 'Widening' instruments tailored to these 
subcategories.  
• 'Widening' instruments could even be tailored made for individual countries depending on 
their weak spots (eg international connectivity; quality of HEI institutes, digital skills…). 
• Focus not only on supporting participation in FP in general, but on supporting participation 
in those programmes/pillars tackling a country's weak spots and which have the best scope 
for improving a country's PICs. 
• Select and evaluate 'Widening'  programmes and participation in FP into effectiveness on 
PIC.  
• Select and evaluate 'Widening' programmes and participation in FP on leveraging these into 
national public and private capacity building.  
• Remove bottlenecks for 'Widening' countries (esp ecially those in 3  % deficit) to activate 
national funding for interventions that are PIC cleared.  
• Remove bottlenecks for using other EU funding ( e.g. Structural Funds) for funding 
interventions that are PIC cleared. 
• Do regular country profiling on PIC (eg see OECD Innovation System profiling of countries).  
• Monitor, evaluate and remedy 'Widening' countries usage of national and EU instruments, 
using e.g. the Semester toolbox.

13. Caveats and suggestions for further analysis  
The methodology to classify countries into 'Widening' status and within 'Widening' status into their 
PIC status relies on EU-JRC Innovation Scoreboard data, which are tested and widely used indicators 
in innovation policy circles. Nevertheless, indicators could be improved and added to better reflect 
the factors that shape innovation capacity building.  
• E.g. on linkages, patent and publication citation information could be used to finetune the link 
between science and technology.  
• E.g. on innovation active enterprises,  more information on new and young firms,  including 
science-based start-ups could measure deeper the scope for business dynamism.  
• E.g. on international connectedness, more information on international mobility of researchers 
and entrepreneurs to capture brain circulation, brain drain, brain gain.  
The proposed methodology composes factors by using multiple indicators as input, and thus 
reduces the sensitivity of the classification outcome to individual factors and outliers, increasing the 
robustness of the classification.  
Also, the systemic nature of innovation capacity building,  requiring a simultaneous presence of 
prerequisites, lead to a rather robust classification, as performance correlates across factors. 
Bottom performers score typically low on all indicators, top performers high.  
Nevertheless, robustness checks with various scenarios of different weights to individual 
components within factors and to different weights of factors in the overall PIC score, should be on 
the radar, if only to show the sensitivity of the results to each component.  
More analysis is needed to match the size and format of 'Widening' support to the various PIC 
categories.  
More analysis is needed to evaluate how 'Widening' support contributes to  
• improvements in PIC scores for recipients,  
• reduce gaps between 'Widening' countries and the EU27 average,  
• improve EU27 cohesion and to improve EU27's overall innovation capacity.

Annex 2 : Estonia's innovation policy (based on Veugelers & 
Schweiger (2016)) 
Estonia's innovation policy formally began in 2000 with discussions regarding the first 
Knowledge-Based Estonia (KBE) strategy, which covered the period 2002 -06. This strategy drew 
on the experiences of Finland and Sweden (see, e.g., OECD 2010; Polt et al. 2007), taking account 
of specific development opportunities, the existing research potential and the country 's economic 
structure, as well as other Estonian development strategies, most notably its education policy 
strategy 'Learning Estonia'. The two mai n objectives were updating Estonia 's knowledge pool and 
increasing the competitiveness of its companies. The three key areas for Estonian research, 
development and innovation (RDI) were (i) user -friendly information technology and the 
development of an information society; (ii) biomedicine;  and (iii) material technology (see KBE 
2002-06). 
In order to achieve these objectives, the KBE strategy established a set of measures spanning four 
key areas: financing of R&D, development of human capital, increasing the effectiveness of RDI 
systems and international cooperation. These measures sought t o increase gross domestic 
expenditure on R&D (GERD) to 1.5 per cent of GDP by 2006. They also aimed to rebalance 
expenditure on research versus development, seeking to shift the breakdown between the two from 
90:10 to 60:40 by 2006.  
To increase the effectiveness of its RDI system, Estonia adopted location -based policies, creating 
science parks and regional business incubators. Lastly, Estonia used international cooperation not 
only as a means of attracting foreign knowledge and technology, but also as a way of building 
research teams with critical mass and avoiding 'brain drain'. 
The KBE strategy for the period 2002 -06 has been followed by similar strategies for the periods 
2007-13 and 2014 -20. A number of governmental and independent bodies have conducted 
assessments looking at the progress made under the first two strategies. Eac h strategy has taken 
account of the experience and expert recommendations resulting from the preceding period and set 
more ambitious objectives, with targets increasing in number and scope. The key areas have been 
adjusted over time, but the overall priori ties have not. The focus continues to be on ICT, health 
technology and services, and more efficient use of resources.
  
Private-sector involvement helped to strengthen publicly funded education and training 
programmes. The Estonian Association of Information Technology and Telecommunications (EAITT), 
an industry association, played a leading role in the development of clusters and the design of 
vocational and university education programmes.

The EU's Horizon Europe framework programme 
promotes European innovation. The current Horizon 
Europe programme uses 'Widening' measures to target 
Member States, support their capacity to innovate, 
close gaps between EU countries and move towards 
performance convergence. This study provides a 
framework and associated indicator options for 
establishing a systemic 'Widening 2.0' methodology 
which could prove instrumental in discussing post -
Horizon Europe 'widening' support measures. It is based 
on assessment of research and innovation indicators of 
targeted 'widening' countries, looking at the economic 
and research impact that Horizon Europe may have had 
between 2018 and 2025.
