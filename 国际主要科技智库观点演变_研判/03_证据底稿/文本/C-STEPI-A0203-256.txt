The government of Lao PDR plans to have the country graduate from one being the Least

With the goal of formulating a master plan and enhancing support for the development of
the 8th National Socio-Economic Development Plan and contributing to the preparation of
the 9th National Socio-Economic Development Plan, which will be followed in the coming
years, and the country's Development Strategy and Vision up to year 2030 w h e r e i n t h e
Nat iona l Sci en ce and Techn ology Development Program has been identified as a driving
f o r c e f o r e c o n o m i c g r o w t h t o b e s u s t a i n a b l e , g r e e n , a n d c o n s i s tent with the SMEs
Development Strategies associated with the transformation of SMEs for the old business
production going to the new modern economics of science, technology, and innovation.
The Second Master Plan will reach the goal, but this project really requires support from

To see the results of implementation of National Socio-Economic Development in 5 years,
period VIII, national strategy on science and technology develo pment, as the national
master plan for technology and innovation development and application, is necessary and
important to encourage and promote the implementation of sectoral strategies through
studying and selection of advanced and appropriate technologies. To determine the project
on technology development and application for the priority sector, R & D for innovation
products and research on science and technology are a major task to promote National
Socio-Economic Development and Growth. Thus, the government has designated the
research and development of science and technology as the main driving force of
commercial productivity and production, which has affected the changing economic
structure of sustainable growth, aiming to achieve the development goal of graduating from
being the least developed countr y in 2020 to one of the middle- income developing
countries. Green growth and sustainability in 2030 are stated in the vision until 2030 and
the 10-year socio-economic development strategy (2016-2025).

In order to ensure the achievement of this goal, the National Master Plan for technology
and innovation development and application in Lao PDR and National Policy on Science and
Technology Research must be developed, with the aim of focusing development on the
science and technology research master plan, survey and study development indicator of
the relevant sectors, human resources, science and technology r esearch innovation
networking, and infrastructure development such as research facilities as well as building a
c e n t e r f o r s c i e n c e a n d t e c h n o l o g y r e s e a r c h t r a n s f e r k n o w l e d g e for increasing trading,
research competitiveness, and industrial development to become more innovative.

Science and Technology to formulate policies and strategies and manage and promote the
transfer technology and innovation of Lao PDR. The Cabinet of Academy of Science and
Technology (CAST) acts as a secretariat to the Ministry of Scie nce and Technology and

2. Objectives

1. Lao PDR’s National Development Strategies
1.1 Overview 1
1.1.1 Political Situation
After gaining its independence from France in 1945, Lao PDR went through the Indochina
War and officially won its independence through the Geneva Agreement in 1954. Later, with
the support of Vietnam, the socialist “Lao People's Democratic Republic” was established in
1975. Lao PDR maintains a one-party dictatorship with the Lao People's Revolutionary Party
at the center and a collective leadership system wherein key government posts, including
the president, head of state, pr ime minister, and speaker of th e National Assembly
concurrently serve as the top political bureau in charge of decision making of the party's
central committee.
The central government has an indirectly elected president as its head of state with a five-
year National Assembly resolutio n, with the current Bounnhang B orachith (Bunnyang
Borachit) elected in 2016 serving his term. The prime minister is appointed by the president
with the approval of the National Assembly, and 18 ministries i ncluding the Ministry of
National Defense, Ministry of Public Security, Ministry of Interior and Safety, Ministry of
Agriculture and Forestry, Ministry of Justice, Ministry of Labor and Social Welfare, Ministry
of Energy and Mining, Ministry o f Commerce and Industry, Minist ry of Science and
Technology, Ministry of Education and Sports, Ministry of Public Affairs, Ministry of Planning,

3.1.3 TVET: Technical Vocatio nal Education & Training

 The organizer of TVET in Lao PDR is the Ministry of Education & S p o r t s , a n d e a c h

this regard.

1.1 Introduction
Korea's economic development has been described as "compressed growth." Formerly the
poorest country in the world and a country with $87 per capita income in the 1950s, Korea
is now the 10th largest economy in terms of GDP and a member of the OECD (as of 2020).
Remarkably, Korea has developed an indigenous R&D capability that has allowed the export
of state-of-the-art goods, such as semiconductors, LCD panels, and smartphones. Among
many factors that have contributed to the economic development of Korea, the policies
associated with science and technology (S&T) are regarded as crucial (Yoon, 2014).
The Korean government has developed and adopted a number of pow erful policy
instruments to advance quickly and boost the level of S&T growth capability that ultimately
led to economic development. The government has tried to foster S&T R&D in the private
sector through a range of policy approaches, such as tax relief, incentives, loans, staff, and
procurement. It has also set up numerous technology creation funds in the forms of direct
subsidies, technology loans, and investment. In addition, the g overnment has made
attempts to resolve human resource problems by offering improved research and financial
assistance using a specialist re searcher framework. Korea 's ap proach to technological
acquisition has had both positive and negative results.
On the positive side, the strategy of Korea allowed it to procure technology at a lower cost

1.2 Long-term Science and Technology Policy
The Long-term Science and Technology Policy in Korea has played a pivotal role in setting
the direction of Korean S&T. “The long-term plan of the 1980s” (1967~1986) was drafted
based on the vision of reaching the level of mid-industrialized country by the middle of the
1980s. The “Long-term plan for 2000s” (1987~2001) set the vision of ranking among the
world’s TOP 10 technology advanced countries and executed the program of 5 technologies
(information, materials, energy, resource, welfare), 2 institutions (human resource, support
measures), and 6 strategies (R&D personnel, target technologies, R&D expenditures). The
"2025 Vision" (2000~2025) set the vision to be the top seven in sci en ce and tech n ol og y
competitiveness by 2025. To this end, a step-by-step objective to create global technological
leadership in selected fields by 2025 was proposed to ensure su perior science and
technology competitiveness over Asian rivals by 2005 including a research center in Asia
Pacific by 2015. The "2040 Vision" (2011~2040), with a view to making Korea the world's
fifth largest global science and technology leader, proposed th at the development of
potential core technologies in 25 fields and 5 policy positions b e d i v i d e d i n t o 3 p h a s e s .

1.3 Mid-term Science a nd Technology Policy
1.3.1 Early Stage of Laying t he Foundation (1960s~1970s)
In the 1960s and 1970s, the government aimed to establish an institutional foundation for
the development of S&T. Accordingly, S&T policy was a crucial p a r t o f t h e f i v e - y e a r
economic development plan, with the relevant laws and institutions created. In the latter
part of the 1960s, the Ministry of Science and Technology (MST) and a government-funded
research institute were established to implement policies for fostering the advancement of
S&T. Moreover, the Korean government focused on learning about and absorbing foreign
technologies by licensing and importing capital goods. Accordin g to Chung (2017), the
Foreign direct investment (FDI) was not a preferred method of financing economic growth,
as the public perceived FDI as being dependent on foreign count ries’ technology and
systems.

The Korean government proliferated technological learning throu gh various policy
measures in the 1960s–1980s/1990s. To begin with, it created de mand for new
technologies by consistently implementing industrial development projects that opened up
new business opportunities for private enterprises. In order to seize opportunities, private

1.4 Policy Shift from Technology Learning to Developing (1980~1997)
In the second phase (1980~1997), under the administrations of CHUN, ROH, and KIM, “The
Korean government shifted its policy direction from technology learning to the growth of its
own science and technological capability in the hi-tech sectors that needed indigenous R&D
capabilities" (Yoon, 2014). The government has initiated large-scale R&D initiatives aimed
at a variety of fields. By benchmarking the policies of advanced economies, the government
has been able to target promisin g S & T a r e a s . K o r e a c o n s e q u e n t l y made significant
investments for taking advantage of economies of scale in some sectors, such as the
semiconductor, electronics, steel, and chemical industries.

not have a sound, solid foundation to sustain economic growth. While the infrastructures
involved have been in place for 40 years and the innovation capacity for R&D has grown,

4.1.2 Overview of HRST in Korea
The National Master Plan for Human Resources in Science and Technology is rooted in the
human resources development policy in science and technology (“HRST policy”). This policy
was developed in tandem with the government-initiated economic development, and its
characteristic can be differentiated before and after enacting “the Special Act on Supporting
Scientists and Engineers for Stre ngthening National Science and Technology
Competitiveness” (KDI, 2016).
In the 1960s, the HRST strategy ce ntered on encouraging and securing qualified human
capital required to boost the light industry, since the Korean government selected the sector
as the top promotion priority and the key industry under the fi rst and second Five-Year
Economic Development Plans. The HRST program in the 1970s continued with the success
of the economic development strategy in the 1960s. Moreover, th e acceleration of the
industrial structure has caused the driving force of economic improvement to be replaced
by the heavy industry, which has seen the government adopt a strategy aimed at developing
scientists and engineers for the heavy industry.
In the 1980s, the government focused on establishing a system for developing highly skilled
scientists. This simultaneously created the necessity of stabil izing the heavy industry,
including textiles, petrochemicals, electronics, machines, and shipbuilding, which had been
fostered as a basic industry from the 1970s. Moreover, the technology-intensive hi-tech
industry affecting national compe titiveness in the future, such as the information
technology and energy resources industry, was highlighted. In the 1990s, the HRST policy
no longer supported industry development because of high economic and industrial growth.
Instead, it concentrated on fostering highly skilled S&T human resources with
competitiveness for technology innovation and development of fundamental technology as
the driving forces of industrial development.
