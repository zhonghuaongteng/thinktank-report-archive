Abstract
Mission portfolio management is a critical ingredient in the success of
mission-oriented innovation policies. This practice aims at ensuring that a
consistent and tailor-made set of diverse projects and activities contribute
together to the realisation of the mission objectives. It is essential for
generating the systemic impacts that missions aim to deliver. However,
many missions currently lack the necessary skills, resources, and tools to
implement portfolio management effectively. Traditional innovation portfolio
management practices offer limited applicability to missions. Moreover,
mission portfolio management requires flexible and adaptive processes to
ensure its effectiveness, which often conflict with the rigid structures and
practices typical of public administration. This paper introduces actionable
guidance for the development, management, and monitoring of mission
portfolios. It also provides targeted recommendations for adopting mission
portfolio management in a purposive and dynamic manner.

Executive summary
Mission Portfolio Management (MPM) puts portfolio management at the core of missions , adopting a
purposeful, system -wide approach to initiating, aligning, monitoring, and steering diverse activities to
achieve specific mission objectives. Unlike traditional portfolio management, MPM is embedded within
broader ecosystems, encompassing diverse organisat ions, activities, innovation stages, and societal
objectives. As a crucial element of mission implementation, it enables continuous coordination,
prioritisation, monitoring, and delivery. However, it remains a novel approach, with many mission
practitioners still in the early stages of the learning process.
Transitioning from traditional innovation portfolios to mission portfolios
Traditional innovation portfolio management provides a foundation for MPM but requires significant
adaptation. While innovation portfolio management focuses on optimising resource use, risk management,
and performance monitoring within single organisations, MPM expands and strengthens this approach by
broadening the portfolio's scope to include upstream research, downstream market deployment, and social
innovations. It shifts the emphasis from financial outcomes or isolated innovation results to societal impact,
ensuring that contributions from a diverse range of stakeholders are integrated and aligned with mission
objectives. Traditional practices often remain static, relying on retrospective tagging and mapping of
activities, whereas MPM demands a proactive, dynamic approach that can adapt to evolving mission needs
and maintain real-time alignment with the mission’s theory of change.
Leveraging the benefits from mission portfolio management
In principle, MPM provides several systemic advantages critical to mission success. By raising
management above individual projects, MPM enables a holistic view of systemic effects and pathways.
This practice provides mission partners with continuous intelligence, learning and adapting from the results
of projects and activities. Based on this deeper and broader dynamic sense -making, they can make
strategic decisions to prioritise and scale up activities that show promise and phasing out those that do
not. Within the mission portfolio, MPM supports the coordinated exploration of diverse options for realising
the mission (the “problem-solution space”) in a context of radical uncertainty. It also identifies opportunities
to leverage complementarities among activities, ensure their coherence and optimise the use of resources.
Overcoming the challenges of mission portfolio management
Despite its promise, MPM faces several challenges:
• Rigid public sector frameworks often impede the flexible and adaptive processes required for MPM.
• Designing dynamic portfolios while maintaining transparency and competitiveness poses a
“proactivity vs. fairness” dilemma.
• Many mission-relevant activities lie outside the direct influence of mission managers.
• Effective MPM demands techn ical, strategic, and cross -sectoral competencies, often scarce in
traditional public administrations.
• Budgetary and staffing limitations can hinder proactive and continuous portfolio management.

Developing, managing and monitoring mission portfolios
Based on insights from a variety of mission portfolio practitioners and experts, this paper analyses three
interconnected components:
• Developing the mission portfolio – initiating and/or bringing together activities in a portfolio and
strategically selecti ng and designing activities to meet the mission objectives in line with the
mission’s theory of change and/or strategic agenda.
• Managing the mission portfolio – proactive and continuous steering of the portfolio to ensure
continued prioritisation and engagement with objectives as the mission develops.
• Monitoring the mission portfolio – continuous mapping, monitoring and sense -making of the
activities making up the portfolio to ensure complementarity and reach and that the overall portfolio
proceeds towards the mission objectives.
Enhancing mission portfolio management practices
To operationalise MPM effectively, this paper proposes several recommendations.
Developing the mission portfolio
1. Ensure that the mission theory of change drives the continuous develop ment of the portfolios and
broadens research and innovation activities to address systemic challenges.
2. Develop explicit criteria for the systematic development of portfolios based on the mission theory
of change.
3. Distinguish the different layers of activ ities that are directly within the control of the mission from
those contributing to the success of the mission , but not under the mission ’s ‘sphere of strategic
influence’.
Managing the mission portfolio
4. Perform portfolio reviews and stage -gate portfolio mechanisms to create strategic flexibility and
avoid portfolio lock-in.
5. Foster a culture of trust and experimentation and ensure risk acceptance in portfolio processes.
6. Establish clear portfolio management responsibilities within the mission management team.
7. Promote interlinkages and exchanges between the different projects and activities within the
portfolio.
Monitoring the mission portfolio
8. Monitor the mission's success based on the po rtfolio's contribution to realising the mission goal,
not the aggregation of individual projects’ success.
9. Develop specific visualisation tools to monitor mission portfolios.
10. Integrate monitoring with learning processes to ensure continuous improvement.
Conclusion and next steps
MPM is a challenging practice that integrates diverse actors, activities, and objectives across boundaries
into a coherent and adaptive system to realise the mission goals . It aligns systemic efforts with societal
goals, fostering innovation and collaboration. However, reali sing its potential requires overcoming
institutional inertia, resource constraints, and capability gaps. Future efforts should focus on refining MPM
tools and methodologies, fostering institutional capabilities, and building shared languages and
frameworks across sectors. Collaborative initiatives like the Mission Action Lab can support practitioners
in adopting MPM practices effectively, ensuring missions deliver on their ambitious societal objectives.

This section explores Mission Portfolio Management (MPM) as a strategic approach to coordinating
diverse projects and activities to achieve missi on objectives. It examines key benefits and challenges,
draws insights from innovation portfolio practices, and proposes a framework for developing, managing,
and monitoring mission portfolios, offering actionable recommendations for policymakers and practitioners.
Portfolio management is an emerging topic both in innovation management in the public sector and more
recently in connection to mission -oriented innovation policies (MOIPs). The practice of Mission portfolio
management (MPM) consists in ensuring that a consistent and tailor-made set of diverse projects and
activities contribute together to the realisation of the mission objectives. While they do not solve all mission
coordination challenges, portfolio management practices are an integral part of t he toolbox in various
missions, under different forms. Successful portfolio management is seen as particularly important for
tackling missions that require system -wide action, as well as for anticipating and adapting to change in
real-time. It is used to m aximise and keep track of the contribution of each initiative and its projects and
activities to the overall mission, support exchanges and leverage synergies between initiatives, and build
clarity around the intent of innovation and experimentation among an ecosystem of actors connected to
the mission.
However, existing tools and methods for innovation portfolio management stemming from public and
private research organisations are only of limited use in terms of their direct application in a mission
context. Such tools tend to concentrate on maximising financial returns, managing individual projects of
comparable nature and are mostly based in single organisations. They also focus on shorter timelines that
differ from the context of missions. MPM should cover the breadth of actions under multiple partners (and
the levers of change available to them) in the mission ecosystem, while assuring the continued validity of
the overall theory of change and final achievement of the mission target. The current practice of portfolio
management in the context of mission itself falls short of the guiding principles. It often consists of a rather
static tagging of supported and related activities without the expected purposive steering, promotion of
exchanges and monitori ng against the mission theory of change. M any missions currently lack the
necessary skills, resources, and tools to implement MPM effectively. Furthermore, the organisational and
institutional framework conditions in which mission operate are often not conducive to MPM, which requires
flexible and adaptive processes to ensure its effectiveness.
This paper seeks to contribute to the rapidly developing practice of MPM by framing it in the context of the
mission concept at large as well as that of established portfolio management practices in the public sector.
The purpose of this paper is to enhance the evidence -base for the effective leveraging of portfolio
management in order to provide actionable advice on MPM to mission managers. To this end, the paper
first frames the concept of mission portfolio management, analysing which learnings from innovation
portfolios could be applicable to MPM. Then the paper addresses both the expected benefits and
challenges in adopting a mission portfolio management approach in current public administration settings.
Based on these insights, a framework for establishing MPM practices is presented involving key insights
for the development, management and monitoring of mission portfolio. The paper concludes with concrete
recommendations for MPM practitioners and guidance on developing potential tools for MPM.

This section first positions portfolios within missions, then describes the progressive development of this
concept, from 'traditional’ innovation management portfolios to MPM, and from static to proactive MPM.
Positioning portfolios at the core of missions
Mission portfolio management (MPM) is the application of portfolio management practices to systematically
initiate, align, monitor and steer a set of activities leveraged to achieve a specific mission. At the core of
this practice lies the mission portfolio itself , that is the collected set of pro jects and activities that are
purposefully selected, supported and combined so as to contribute together to realise the mission
objectives. Similarly, mission portfolios were recently defined in a European Project as groupings of
complementary actions (going beyond research and innovation, R&I) that are managed in a system -wide
way to achieve the mission’s goals (European Commission, 2024[1]).
Figure 2.1 presents a simplified MOIP initiative with three missions. Each mission sets the goals and
targets and mobilise a coordinated suite of policy and regulatory interventions (the policy mix) that support
the various projects and activities (the portfolio) which together contribute to the mission realisation.

From innovation portfolios to mission portfolios
Therefore, innovation portfolio management practices and tools are not inherently suitable for missions.
First, mission portfolios are broader in scope than innovation portfolios. The strategic orientation of a
mission and its respective activities towards the mission statement is prioritised in mission portfolios;
innovation, thus, serves that purpose rather than drives it. This means that mission portfolios must include
both upstream and downstream activities that enable new solutions to have enough scale to achieve the
mission, rather than only dealing with innovation funding. As such, a mission portfolio can be composed of
projects and activities that span i) different components of the innovation system (fro m knowledge and
innovation to skills, infrastructure, etc.), ii) different stages of development (from basic research to market
deployment) and iii) different types of solutions (OECD, 2024 [3]). When designing and managing their
portfolio, mission practitioners must consider a wider variety of criteria when mapping and making sense
of the portfolio.
Second, the end goal of traditional innovation portfolio management, often driven by financial and risk-
containment rationales, is too narrow. Achieving the mission, which is to address a complex and ambitious
societal challenge, should be the primary criteria and focus for missions, with cost-effectiveness, feasibility,
expected impact, etc., being assisting variables that help categorise activities. Since missions have wide
and cross-cutting scopes, analysing the contributions of various actors to the mission portfolio is essential.
Third, mission portfolios involve several layers of activities contributing in different ways and to various
degrees to the common objectives. Mission managers must engage not only with the activities and projects
directly unde r their control (i.e., that they steer), but also with the activities and projects in the wider
ecosystem that contribute more indirectly (or at least less formally) to the mission success.
From static to proactive mission portfolio management
Innovation portfolio tools and practices often fail to enable continuous and proactive management so new
tools and methods are needed. Currently, the same holds for many missions. Often mission portfolio
management practices are static, involving tagging and mapping activities relevant to the mission in
specific sense-making exercises. More advanced software is in use (for instance, at funding agencies),
but these are often case management systems or data visualisation tools in which the portfolio
management is often limited to static efforts and lacks proactive and continuous deployment. Activities are
ex-post “tagged to” particular missions based on their relevance, which helps to make sense of the
operational context and to raise mission awareness and a sense of belonging. However, it does not allow
for dynamic stewardship or coordination of the mission portfolio (se e Table 2.2 regarding portfolio
management functions).

This section explores the expected benefits and challenges of mission portfolio management (MPM). It
highlights how MPM enables greater coordination, strategic alignment, and systemic oversight of mission
activities, fostering more effective decision-making and resource allocation. At the same time, the chapter
addresses key challenges, such as navigating cross-sectoral complexities, ensuring adaptive governance,
and balancing flexibility with accountability.
The expected benefits of mission portfolio management
The strengths of the MPM approach resides in the ability of mission leaders to design, steer and monitor
the mission activities at higher and systemic levels rather than taken in isolation. This holistic and proactive
management of projects and activities has several expected benefits.
Raising the level of management above individual activities to capture the systemic
effects.
A project-centric view of innovation especially connected to missions can be problematic, and public sector
organisations have been found to struggle with “projectification” – the linear division of policy problems into
smaller, manageable, time-bound actions without a general overview of their collective impact (Hodgson
et al., 2019[4]). In the context of missions, more specifically, this holistic level of management is the one at
which it is possible to practically connect these activities and the mission strategic agenda, i.e. the different
pathways that are envisioned (and revised) by the mission partners to achieve the collectively defined
objectives (see Box 3.1).

Box 3.1. Mission portfolio strategy in Japan’s Moonshot R&D Programme
Japan’s Moonshot R&D Programme has set seven very ambitious Moonshot goals such as the “realisation of a
society in which human beings can be free from limitations of body, brain, space, and time by 2050”.
In each project, it funds frontier research focused on the achievement of these goals. At the level of each goal, it
also implements a portfolio approach to manage the high risk of failure of projects. The main idea is to create an
R&D portfolio system as a package of projects for each Moonshot Goal and evaluate the possibility of achieving a
goal at the level of this package, not at the level of each individual project. Between 3 and 13 projects were selected
for each of the seven goals (Figure 3.1). This strategy is reflected in the design of the programme. A Programme
Director is appointed for each Moonshot goal and tasked with the development and implementation of a Portfolio

Enabling the coordinated exploration of a set of uncertain potential solutions.
Traditional portfolio management reduces the level of risks of a pool of projects when taken together.
Supporting several projects following different paths maximises the chances that at least one project
succeeds. MPM go beyond this by coordinating differ ent actors in the innovation system and beyond to
explore different options in parallel until sufficient information is collected regarding their respective merits.

more than exploring solutions. They can also experiment with different pathways and markets, which often
have to do with different views on the relevant problem itself.

Within the same call procedure, although in the end the partners intend to select the projects that best respond to

Identifying and leveraging complementarities between the various projects and activities
within the mission portfolio
Projects within a portfolio do not perform in isolation, they form part of a broader organisational or systemic
context. Complementarities in missions can take the form of either, (1) technical/substantive synergies
between the different activities/projects in the portfolio or (2) more efficient management of resources. This
can result in better upstream and downstream management of mission RDI and the pooling of resources
to achieve the mission. It is expected that public authorities and the coordinators to whom they delegate
the task of orchestrating the mission ecosystem ar e expected to play a key role in incentivising and
promoting exchanges within the portfolio to maximise and leverage ‘technical’ complementarities between
interdependent activities pertaining to the same set of potential solutions to the mission.
Two different types of technical/substantive complementarities can be identified:
• Complementarities between activities within a given set of ‘innovation solutions’: These are the
complementarities between activities pertaining to a specific solution (i.e., a specific socio-technical
system or pathway). For instance, developing the production and commercialisation of carbon -
neutral hydrogen requires the development of new technologies of electrolysers, the deployment
of new infrastructures for the distribution/transp ort of hydrogen, possibly a capacity to produce
renewable energy for producing energy, the development of new engineering skills to produce and
maintain the electrolysers, etc. The same applies to many net -zero systems. For instance, a net -
zero farm will r equire new methods (e.g., for carbon -neutral fertilisation, for increasing farm
efficiency), new technologies (e.g., renewables, carbon capture systems, sensors), as well as
activities along the value chain, including buyers and customers. These different components of
the systems are interdependent, and the mission's success will depend on the consistent progress
of all of them.

• Complementarities between solutions at different stages of development: A mission often supports
different generations of solutions, from the more exploratory to those more advanced and closer to
market. Significant lessons learned are drawn from the early generations and fed into the
development of more ambitious but still underdeveloped solutions. There are , therefore,
interdependencies between activities at different stages of development (from exploratory research
to scale -up and market deployment) for achieving the same mission in the longer term, which
covers the different stages of the innovation chain.
Harnessing these complementarities through proactive and purposive management is beneficial to
achieving the mission. Mission portfolio managers and their partners must be able to identify the different
types of complementarities and, therefore, needed lin kages—between activities within a given mission
portfolio. A systemic approach to MPM is essential to ensure the various intervention points are working in
a coherent manner across the missions they are trying to influence.
Portfolio management can also be instrumental in linking and aligning mission activities with other relevant
initiatives of similarly minded initiatives, as illustrated in the case of the Viable Cities mission (Box 3 .3).

Box 3.3. Managing the climate transition through a portfolio approach – Viable Cities, Sweden

Mission portfolio management challenges
While MPM is a crucial dimension of missions, it is not implemented in all missions. It is new and presents
many challenges for public authorities. Most missions adopt only part of this approach and are still
experimenting with new methods and tools. In many respects, MPM is still very much a learning process,
with little information available on successful practices.

Setting the right framework conditions
MPM necessitates the creation of a novel and agile work framework, which is often not possible within the
current innovation funding and policy programme structures. This framework notably includes different
types of criteria for selecting activities, processes to steer and monitor activiti es, internal (administrative)
incentives, and rules to align staff with portfolio management logic.
The setting of criteria to select activities and design the portfolio is particularly challenging. Traditional
management often uses criteria related to re search excellence and relevance. MPM relies upon these
criteria as well to assess the proposed projects but adds an essential new family of criteria at the portfolio
level: the coherence of the portfolio relative to the mission objectives, i.e. how each pr oject contributes to
the mission objectives as part of a consistent set of projects and activities. However, when mission
portfolios become very large , then there also needs to be a cutoff point in terms of which activities and
projects are considered in a ctive MPM (based on critical size, expected impact, etc.). The
operationalisation of this type of ‘portfolio fit' criteria is not yet well formalised.
Combining proactive management with fair competition
How to proactively design the portfolio while still preserving a sufficient level of competition between
initiatives and ensuring a sound and equitable treatment of project proposals? Relying upon ‘distant’ call s
for proposals ensures a level playing field between different candidates and keeps the pr ocess
accountable. It is less the case when using more hands -on approaches, which existing government or
organisation-level administrative rules might even forbid.
In most cases, public administrations can only allocate funding using competitive mechanism s (e.g., calls
for proposal, tenders and public procurement), which do not easily allow for selecting specific
projects/activities based on their complementarity with other projects in a portfolio.
Another challenge related to the relationship between the mission approach and market competition
concerns the ways to promote exchanges between different projects while preserving the confidentiality of
certain knowledge and information. An essential dimension of portfolio management is promoting
exchanges between complementary activities within a portfolio to leverage potential synergies. However,
project participants – especially in private firms and Research and Technology Organisations (RTOs) –
can sometimes be reluctant to exchange with other organisations for fear of disclosing proprietary
information. Monitoring the portfolio in the collective setting of the mission can also face similar
confidentiality issues.
Managing activities that are ‘out of control’
As previously mentioned, the portfolio often includes activities that lie outside the mission managers' direct
control but are relevant to the mission’s progress and accomplishment. Therefore, the relation to these
different activities will be different, from direct coordination in the case of activities within their control to
only information exchange for more ‘remote’ activities. This is an essential parameter to consider. Although
missions are meant to be systemic and involve a broad array of public and private actors, mission
managers are often located in one part of the system, i.e., a specific administration or platform. Their power
to manage activities , therefore, depends on the nature of these activities. They often face difficulties
managing activities that pertain to a policy field or a thematic area outside their organisation's mandate.
A recent OECD study in the area of missions to combat climate change shed light upon the consequences
of their partial control over their portfolio (OECD, 2024[3]). As most missions are initiated and managed by
public authorities in the research and innovation area, portfolio management can only be proactively
exerted on the activities they can mobilise and support with their set of interventions. In this view, a mission
portfolio is often better understood as composed of nested concentric circles. At the core of the mission

Nurturing the necessary capabilities and tools to manage strategically the portfolio of
interventions
Many public administrations can be in limited supply of the needed skills and knowledge, as they
traditionally prioritise administrative and process -oriented skills over substantive and strategic skills.
Depending on the mission, MPM can , for instance, call for knowledge of specific technological and
industrial areas to plan and assess the portfolio's progress towards the objectives, identify arising needs,
and terminate underperforming or counterproductive activities. It also seeks to analyse and understand
how each project or activity, as part of the broader portfolio, contributes to the mission.
This demands some ‘T-shaped’ competencies that are not only deep but also broad since, as previously
mentioned, the portfolio might include activities of different natures in different sectors and cover different
stages of the innovation chain.
As most missions have complex project and activity portfolios, mission managers must have access to or
develop their own MPM tools. For this relying on a concise theory of change is a must to identify specific,
measurable and relevant indicators to analyse portfolio activities c ontinuously. Measures to quantify the
level of resources and potential impact may be required to make portfolio -level decisions. Portfolio
management also differs from traditional project management because it requires the analysis of risks and
impacts on the portfolio level, and usually, this is not done in the public sector, potentially necessitating the
reskilling of mission managers and teams.
Managing the portfolio at a reasonable cost.
The challenges set out above make clear that MPM requires increas ingly costly human resources. These
practices require additional budgetary resources in public administrations (notably agencies) and various
institutions serving as coordinators/orchestrators of the mission. These resources are difficult to secure in
administrations subject to budget austerity and strict staff limitations after years of New Public
Management.

This section introduces a three-pronged framework for mission portfolio practice, drawing on insights from
a diverse group of practitioners and experts. These perspectives were gathered during the project's
benchmarking phase through interviews and a knowledge exchange workshop :
• Developing the mission portfolio – initiating and/or bringing together activities in a portfolio and
strategically selecting and designing activities to meet the mission objectives in line with the
mission’s theory of change and/or strategic agenda.
• Managing the mission portfolio – proactive and continuous steering of the portfolio to ens ure
continued prioritisation and engagement with objectives as the mission develops.
• Monitoring the mission portfolio – continuous mapping, monitoring, and sense -making of the
activities making up the portfolio to ensure complementarity and reach and that the overall portfolio
proceeds towards the mission objectives.

Managing the mission portfolio
As the mission matures, the focus shifts to managing and steering the portfolio. Proactive decision-making
is critical, as the mission managers need to be reactive to the problems and results of individual projects
and activities, as well as the hypothesis testing and changing context of the overall mission. For this
purpose, mission managers must frame negative results from specific projects as successful learning
outcomes for the broader mission and re-engage stakeholders in other efforts when possible. This iterative
learning process and culture is key to dynamic mission portfolio management, where projects and activities
must inevitably be scaled up or down based on their progress.
In this line, the following key questions were raised with mission managers and experts:
• How can proactive portfolio management be enabled, in which projects and activities are scaled
down or up based on their results as well as evolving mission context, learnings, and strategies?
• What decisions at the portfolio level should the mission manager be able to make? Would the
previously discussed criteria allow those decisions to be made?
• How can exchanges and complementarities between the portfolio's instruments and activities be
supported to leverage complementarities and maximise impact? How to take into account the
possible competitive and confidentiality issues between various participants in the portfolio?
• How can dynamic portfolio management be established that encourages flexibility, minimises lock-
in from prior commitments, and remains open to new, experimental, and/or transformative options
along the way? Are there any adjustments to the portfolio criteria discussed that you would make
keeping this in mind?

Monitoring the mission portfolio
When mapping and monitoring the portfolio, it is essential to differentiate between the projects and
activities directly under the control of the mission team and those in the broader ecosystem that are
relevant to the mission but over which the mission manager has little to no agency. Once again, the
mission’s theory of change, established at launch and adapted throughout the mission process, is
important in informing and guiding active and responsive monitoring. Continuous visualising the project
portfolio – with simple means or more elaborated dynamic monitoring and visualisation tools – can assist
in information exchange amongst st akeholders and support proactive decision -making to realise the
mission objectives.
The following key questions were raised with mission managers and experts:
• How to ensure that portfolio monitoring informs both collective learning (on the mission ecosystem
level) and the strategic agenda?
• What are the metrics of success by which the portfolio's progress is measured?
• What visualisation and decision support tools are best used to map and monitor a portfolio?
Mission practitioners found that mission monitor ing should be carried out on many levels and at different
cadences (cycles of time) that are relevant to the actors at each level. Systemic, strategic, operational, and
tactical levels view activities and outcomes at different time scales. Often , this requires creating a matrix
of levers of change and transition phases that would allow a layer of different mappings in order to identify
synergies within the portfolio. Also, the ability to zoom in on different levels of portfolio activities (e.g., on a
particular strand of intervention or geographical level (EU-national-regional) is important as well. While the
wish list for mission monitoring is often quite long, first and foremost, it is essential to link the theory of
change to monitoring, evaluation and learning (MEL) frameworks much more explicitly. In practical terms,
the process may start by using unstructured tagging to identify projects that are either "missions aligned"
or "missions originated". This gives organisations leading the missions the bare m inimum of information
required to interrogate the portfolio (insofar as it sits within this organisation). Having a clear connection
between the ToC and practical portfolio activities makes it clear that different capabilities and attitudes also
need to be exhibited by mission managers and contributors. Often, opacity suits managers and mission
contributors as it allows them to continue business as usual – this is something that mission monitoring
should help avoid by actively helping participants to also feel less threatened and more reflective.
While key KPIs in mission portfolio monitoring may differ across missions and approaches differ depending
on the organisation (see Box 4.4 for an example from UNDP), broadly speaking mission practitioners are
looking at the following dimensions against the counterfactual of a linear, siloed programme: acceleration
(of impact delivery), orchestration (of system directionality), spillovers, mission configurations (of work
packages), portfolio configurations (of mission types). These , in practice, may include cumulated
investments, actor engagement/composition, and "tag" various categories of system effects and different
phases of development.

This chapter presents key conclusions and recommendations for mission portfolio management,
emphasising the importance of cross-sectoral and cross -organisational learning. A critical challenge is
ensuring that these efforts foster shared languages and methods for effective decision -making, ultimately
enhancing the successful realisation of missions.
Fundamentally, when performed well, mission portfolio management is a continuous activity that spans
different institutions across disciplinary, sectoral and administrative boundaries. This represents both a
major challenge and a key promise of the practice. A critical question going forward is, thus, how to ensure
that cross-sectoral and cross-organisational learning and capability building lead to shared languages and
methods for effective decision-making that support the realisation of the mission. To help do that, the paper
puts forward the following recommendations using the three MPM components.
Recommendations for developing the mission portfolio
1. Ensure that the mission theory of change drives the continuous development of the
portfolios and broadens beyond research and innova tion activities to address systemic
challenges.
In essence, the mission portfolio should not be driven by what the main partner organisations have at their
disposal but by what the mission theory of change requires. Given the origins of the mission -oriented
approach, it is frequent that governments rely on traditional science, technology, and innovation (STI)
instruments, such as research grants, to implement missions. The design of these instruments (mandated
objectives, scope of eligible expenditures, e tc.) constrains the portfolio to certain activities and options,
most often related to technological innovation. However, to capture the synergistic and transformative
outcome of missions, governments should also harness other policy instruments - ranging from regulation
and taxation to procurement, scale-up mechanisms and incentives, communication, or education – in order
to populate the portfolio with activities identified in the mission theory of change (or strategic agenda,
pathways, roadmap, etc.).
2. Develop explicit criteria for the systematic development of portfolios based on the mission
theory of change
The criteria for selecting activities that will constitute the portfolio should be mission -specific and include
variables on expected contrib utions to achieving the mission objectives, level of experimentation, market
readiness, scale, etc. These criteria should be specific enough to initiate or select activities that can fill
gaps in the mission theory of change, enable monitoring of whether the overall mission portfolio is likely to
deliver on the mission objectives and cut activities that are not contributing to the overall mission
continuously. These types of ‘portfolio fit' criteria (i.e. assessment criteria that take into account how the
different potential activities would contribute to the overall mission goal alongside the other activities in the
portfolio) should be explicit in the ‘mission operation guidelines’ (or related intervention guidelines), along
with and at the same level as traditional excellence, relevance and team experience criteria.

3. In developing and managing the portfolio, distinguish the different layers of activities, from
those directly within the mission's control to those contributing to the mission's success
but not under the mission's sphere of strategic influence.’
From the outset, the mission portfolio should be designed base d on a clear understanding of which
activities are within the control of the mission management team (operational environment) and which are
important, critical activities contributing to the success of the mission but not under the mission ‘sphere of
strategic influence’. Recognising this distinction is particularly important given that many missions operate
within a limited scope of activities compared to what is required to realise the mission entirely. This
understanding supports two key objectives: i) ensure a comprehensive strategic outlook as it helps the
mission managers and partners to move beyond a limited focus on their immediate responsibilities and
constraints (avoiding the "half -empty glass" perspective) ; ii) actively encourage and gradually enlist n ew
stakeholders by identifying and acknowledging critical external activities. This involves incorporating their
modes of intervention and supported activities into the broader mission framework . This will be
instrumental for the mission evaluations.
Recommendations for managing the mission portfolio
4. Perform portfolio reviews and stage-gate portfolio mechanisms to create strategic flexibility
and avoid portfolio lock-in.
Use a stage-gated approach whenever possible to cut or change activities that are not in line with mission
goals or have not produced the desired impact based on explicit and clearly communicated rules
communicated from the outset to all partners . Frequent portfolio reviews and regular theory of change
updates (e.g., every few years), reflecting and internalising learned lessons and changes in the broader
environment, should also help avoid implementation and confirmation bias.
5. Foster a culture of trust and experimentation and ensure risk acceptance in portfolio
processes.
Strive to establish clear goals and boundaries around experimentation so that stakeholders or mission
actors feel comfortable to engage in new activities, processes, or methods. If an activity does not provide
the expected desired outcome (activity failure), the mission manager should re -engage stakeholders in
other activities that aim to fulfil mission objectives. In other words, the mission manager should be able to
differentiate between an activity's ambition and the overall mission's ambition, emphasising that the failure
of a given project and activity does not equate to mission failure. This should also be reflected in the
mission monitoring and evaluation practices.
6. Establish clear portfolio management responsibilities within the mission management
team.
As missions need to be managed at several nested levels and/or in different areas , they should also
establish specific roles (like Mission Head, Mission Deputy, or Mission Lead) that oversee different levels
of portfolios or sub-portfolios and facilitate the differentiation of activities and outcomes under their control.
Certain missions have created a ‘portfolio manager’ function.
7. Promote interlinkages and exchanges between the different projects and activities within
the portfolio.
Mission portfolio management assumes that a single-shot project or activity will not be able to deliver on
the mission. Harnessing the complementarities between these projects and activities through proactive
and purposive management will be beneficial to achieving the mission. It is, therefore, essential to be able
to identify the different types of complementarities – and therefore needed linkages – between

projects/activities within a given mission portfolio. For example, three types of complementarities can be
identified under more technology-centric missions/MOIPs: 1) activities within a given set of ‘innovation
solutions’, 2) solutions at different stages of development, and 3) different potential solutions. While this
still needs to be further developed, several miss ion managers endeavour to promote exchanges and, if
possible, cooperation between various activities within their portfolio, if only under the form of regular
thematic events where the different supported organisations are gathered.
Recommendations for monitoring the mission portfolio
8. Monitor the success of the mission based on the contribution of the portfolio to the
realisation of the mission goal, not the aggregation of individual projects’ success
The most crucial part of mission portfolio management is the delivery of the mission rather than the number
of activities or their scope. Ongoing mission portfolio monitoring should be focused on providing mission
managers with the information needed to ensure that the portfolio will deliver the outcomes defined rather
than the success of singular projects. This requires a pragmatic approach where selected key metrics and
dimensions are tracked continuously (while avoiding administrative burden or information overload) , and
sense-making exercises are re-occurring to assess mission progress from different perspectives.
9. Develop specific visualisation tools to monitor mission portfolios.
Notably, portfolio visualisations should create the basis for a robust system for networ k analysis of the
portfolio so that users and stakeholders can better understand the connection among all projects and
portfolios. Some tools have been developed, such as the Future by Lund , a tool developed by Lund
University for ‘Innovation Ecosystem Por tfolio Tracking .’ (Commission, European, 2023 [13]). However,
these remain very few and are not adapted to all mission cases. Further efforts through action -oriented
experiments involving researchers and mission managers should be dedicated to developing and testing
portfolio management tools in different mission contexts.
10. Integrate monitoring with learning processes to ensure continuous improvement.
Mission portfolios are not static – they must adapt based on learnings from their experimental parts and
changing circumstances and the impact of established activities and projects . It is important that day -to-
day portfolio monitoring is also connected to other learning processes established for the mission (mission
strategic foresight activities, evaluation, sensemaking sessions, etc.). Hence, monitoring activities should
feed into other learning practices and vice versa.
Next steps
Further research is needed on how mission portfolio man agement can, in different contexts, fulfil its core
functions of sensemaking, mapping and visualisation, diversification, monitoring and evaluation, pro-active
co-ordination and stewardship. More exploration is also needed on the topic of which tools are o r can be
made available to effectively visualise, monitor and take action with regard to mission portfolios. Yet, tools
are only helpful if the individuals and roles they support are well-positioned and enabled to actively steward
mission portfolios, including those that span across and between organisations and sectors. Effective MPM
rests on many other practices and capacities, including actively using theories of change, developing
mission-specific portfolio monitoring criteria, and embracing digital tra cking tools and methods. Thus,
effective portfolio management requires broader institutional shifts and development.
The Mission Action Lab will continue to monitor MPM's emerging practice. Building on the insights provided
in this paper, the Mission Action Lab aims to give practical guidance on its different aspects in the future.
