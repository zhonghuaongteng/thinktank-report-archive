1. Project Background

Innovation has become a catalyst for advanced countries to harn ess their national
economies, thereby facilitating the rapid transformation of dev eloping nations into
developed economies. South Korea, officially known as the Republic of Korea, is a shining
example of a country that has harnessed innovation to drive economic development. Its
remarkable growth trajectory has placed it on par with the Organization for Economic Co-
operation and Development (OECD) nations, underscoring the critical role of research and
development (R&D) and technology in accelerating national economic progress. The South
Korean government's proactive engagement through its science-technology-innovation (STI)
policy is a prominent measure that has fueled breakthroughs in the country's journey to
economic development and prosperity.
A t t h e h e a r t o f t h i s i n n o v a t i o n - d r i v e n a p p r o a c h l i e s a n a t i o n a l innovation system (NIS),
which serves as a functioning fr amework wherein both R&D and no n-R&D endeavors
stimulate economic activities. It is essential to recognize that innovation extends beyond
traditional R&D activities: Rather, it encompasses a broad spectrum of activities, including
reverse engineering, imitation, adoption, diffusion, modification, and learning by doing.
Effective strategies for transferring innovation from developed to developing countries are
pivotal in ensuring sustainable progress. South Korea has excel led in learning from
developed nations such as Japan, t h e U n i t e d S t a t e s o f A m e r i c a , and various European
countries. This practice can serve as a valuable model for developing nations like Indonesia
to emulate and adapt to their specific contexts.

2. Project Objectives

The 2023 K-Innovation ODA program with Indonesia has three primary objectives. The first
objective is to assist Indonesian policymakers, researchers, and policy analysts in the design
and enhancement of research and innovation (R&I) infrastructures managed by BRIN. The
second objective is to support these stakeh olders in identifyin g and addressing the
challenges related to R&I funding within BRIN. The last objective is to strengthen STI policy
research capacities through a range of activities including STI policy consultation,
collaborative STI research, training or policy workshops, and STI dissemination forum.
From 2023 onwards, the long-term goal is to lay a firm foundation for STI cooperation and
collaboration between South Korea and Indonesia. Such will not be limited solely to
cooperation and collaboration between STEPI and BRIN but will open up to other public or
private agencies in Korea and Indonesia concerned about address ing STI issues. The
Republic of Korea is an essential partner for the Government of Indonesia in providing STI
policy consultation practices. In the 1960s, South Korea and Indonesia had similar histories
regarding the early years of their independence. Both countries were categorized as having
low-growth economies and difficulties in achieving prosperity. South Korea has undergone
one of the most remarkable economic transformations and industrial development in the
past 60 years. Beginning as an agriculture-based economy in the 1960s, it has become a
country renowned for its advanced technologies, achieving a high-ranking global economic
position by 2023, while Indonesia is growing slowly as a lower- middle-class economy.
Therefore, transferring the lessons learned from South Korea to Indonesia is necessary.

policy”, refers to intentional and unintentional governmental efforts to create, establish,

various, and do not aim merely to define or explain how to commercialize R&D products for
the market, but also how social, educational, economic, and political systems are pivotal to
formulating and implementing innovation policy. According to Patanakul and Pinto (2017),

3. Project Framework

The 2023 K-Innovation Partnership program consisted of five key activities: a kick-off
seminar, a policy consultation project, a joint research program aimed at producing a policy
consulting report, a capacity-building training program for policymakers, policy analysts and
researchers, and a dissemination workshop. The meetings and workshops were held offline
in two countries, Indonesia (Jakarta City) and South Korea (Seoul and Sejong City). The first
phase consisted in holding a kick-off seminar with the STEPI team and BRIN to discuss the
details of the activities and to evaluate the previous year’s activities. The kick-off meeting,
which was held at BRIN’s building in Jakarta (March 2023), consisted of presentations by
Korean experts on Korean practices and cases related to the themes of R&D infrastructure
and funding, with the participation of Indonesian experts, and o f f i c i a l s f r o m B R I N , n o n -
governmental organisations, and universities.
The Korean experts collaborated with Indonesian policymakers, p olicy analysts and
researchers for joint policy consultation and research on two t hemes: 1) designing and
improving public service in the field of research and innovatio n (R&I) infrastructures
delivered by BRIN; 2) designing and improving public service in the field of research and
innovation (R&I) funding as delivered by BRIN. The BRIN teams, under the supervision of the
K o r e a n e x p e r t s , a s s i g n e d f o c a l p o i n t s t o v a r i o u s g r o u p s c o m p o s ed of Indonesian
policymakers, policy analysts, and researchers. In post-pandemic period, kick-off meetings,
training, and workshops were held under a hybrid system comprising offline and online
means.
The offline or physical activities were the main channel for sharing the Indonesian situation
and Korean practices and exchanging policy ideas and suggestions. The last activity of K-
Innovation was a dissemination workshop wherein the research teams could present and
receive feedback on the results of their joint researches from STI research community of
Indonesia and Korea. Having comp leted the dissemination worksho p, Korean and
Indonesian researchers will work together to publish academic papers based on the results
of their joint research.

 Research Infrastructure and Economic Development
Fabiano (2020: 1-15) asserted th at research and innovation infr astructure is critical to
support the implementation of research activities aimed at improving a country's socio-
economic development and the quality of people's lives. This can be assessed from sectoral
and essential aspects, such as “ease of access to and availability of research facilities and
infrastructures”, “increasing the quality and capacity of human resources”, “collaboration
and transfer of knowledge or correlation of knowledge with work demands and global or
international insights”. Research infrastructure includes the f acilities, equipment and
t e c h n i c a l s u p p o r t n e c e s s a r y f o r high-quality research. The resu lts of using research
infrastructure as a resource for research institutions are the primary input in the research
and innovation ecosystem. Research infrastructure plays an important role in supporting
and improving the implementation of research and the creation o f innovations and
technologies.
Countries or regions that have a robust research infrastructure tend to be more competitive
on the global stage (Zendulková, 2022: 47-56). Countries with robust research infrastructure
can attract investment in resear ch and development and encourag e knowledge-based
economic growth. (Širá, 2020, p. 4). The resolution of certain problems in science and
technology requires special facilities, thereby encouraging eff orts to reach a better
understanding of problems in the fields of science and technology and to create solutions
that will have a positive impact on society. Institutions that can make significant research

2. Indonesia's Vision 2020-2024

The Indonesian National Medium Term Development Plan (RPJMN) 2020-2024 sets forth
the four main pillars of national development as follows:
① Human development and mastery of science and technology.
② Sustainable economic development.
③ Equitable development.
④ National security and good governance.

These four pillars will serve as the foundation for realizing Indonesia's vision of becoming
one of the world's largest economic powers by 2045 (“Indonesia Maju 2045”). This shift in
the focus of national development is not accidental. In the period 2030-2040, Indonesia is
predicted to receive a 'demographic bonus', namely when the number of productive age
population (15-64 years) is greater than the number of people under 15 years or over 64
years. This productive age population group is predicted to reach 64% of the total national
population, which is projected to reach 297 million people (Bappenas, 2017). However, in
order for Indonesia to gain the maximum benefit from this, the increase in human resources
(HR) must be balanced with an increase in quality and innovation capacity.

 Allocation and Adjustment System of the Indonesian
Government's R&D Budget: Before BRIN
There are various sources of R&D funds in Indonesia, including the State Revenue and
Expenditure Budget (APBN), Regional Revenue and Expenditure Bud get (APBD),
endowments, business entities, and other sources - both valid a nd non-binding. The
majority of Indonesia’s R&D expenditure budget comes from sources of government funds.
According to the Ministry of Research, Technology, and Higher Education (2017), only 43.74%
of the total GERD of Rp30.78 tr illion (0.25% of PDB) in 2016 wa s dedicated to research
activities, while the rest was allocated for non-research purposes. (STEPI & LIPI, 2020)
In Indonesia, the mana gement of R&D funding operates through tw o mechanisms:
institutional funding and project funding. The institutional funding mechanism adheres to
the national planning regulations outlined in Law No. 25 of 200 4 , w h i c h p e r t a i n s t o t h e
National Development Planning Sy stem. The budgeting process fol lows the guidelines
stipulated in Law No. 17 of 2003, which relates to State Financing. All public R&D institutions
receive a portion of the state budget (APBN) allocated specifically for their R&D activities,
based on the programs they have planned in advan ce. The allocat ion of funding is
documented in the Entry List of Budget Implementation (DIPA).
The second R&D funding mechanism is project funding, where funds are assigned to a group
or an individual to conduct a specific R&D activity with clearly defined parameters of scope,
budget, and timeframe. This type o f funding is typically grante d upon submission of a
project proposal outlining the intended research activities (Lepori et al., 2007). The funds
are often administered through intermediary institutions responsible for managing R&D
funds and generally entail a certain level of operational autonomy (Steen, 2012).
Project funding is obtained from three primary sources. First, it originates from the Entry

Stephan and Levin (1992) argued that researchers cannot do research without equipment,
and that research productivity is a causal relationship between research infrastructure,
research personnel, and research funding. This means that the performance and availability
of research infrastructure to support the research field has a very important impact on the
productivity of S&T R&D. In particular, recent R&D has been centred on state-of-the-art
research infrastructure, which i s supported by the state rather than individuals and
organisations. According to John Peter Dickinson (1989), as the practice of modern science
has evolved into a collective mega-science type of research, unlike in the past, hundreds of
researchers in the research infrastructure tend to work on one project. In addition, while
research infrastructure was mainly utilised for developmental research (OECD's research
stages: basic, applied, and developmental) in the past, large-scale research infrastructure is
now being used for purely theoretical disciplines (e.g. mathemaƟcs → mulƟscale large-scale
computers), while social infrast ructure that indirectly support s the huge research
infrastructure has become necessary in recent years, which has l e d t o t h e f o r m a t i o n o f
science and industry complexes (e.g. Technopoles).
Many research infrastructures in various countries are currently being operated with the
support of the state, and many research infrastructures in Korea also receive state support

researchers from fully utilising the facilities even though they are available.
• Underutilisation: Large amounts of equipment are not being util ised, and there is
no clear understanding of demand.
• Weak monitoring: There is a lack of effective measures for preventive maintenance

2.2.2 Historical Overview of R&D Inv estment and S&T’s Activities
In 1963, South Korea invested just US$ one million in national R&D. Some thirty years later,
in 1991, the country invested US$ 3.8 billion in national R&D, and after another thirty years,
in 2021, it invested US$ 89.3 billion. This rate of increase is remarkable.
Now, let’s look at the trend of the government R&D budget and major activities.
The first government-funded research institute, KIST, was established in 1966, while the first
government R&D program was launched in 1982, with an R&D budget of US$ 192 million.
By 2021, the government’s R&D budget had risen to US$ 23.8 billion. Clearly, the Korean
government has been focusing on R&D investment for our future.

2.2.3 The Necessity of Coordinati ng National R&D programs
As the government R&D budget has increased, we have recorded many remarkable S&T
achievements, including scientific outputs, industrial products, and global contributions.
However, we have also encountered unexpected side effects.
Managing an ever-growing number of projects and programs, and involving many ministries
in government R&D programs, has made efficiency and effectiveness in budget allocation
increasingly critical. F u r t h e r m o r e , i t i s e s s e n t i a l t o c o n s i d e r specialisation in the area of
science and technology budgeting because disruptive innovation occurs more frequently.
In the early 2000s, government R&D investment continued to increase, resulting in the
diversification of R&D activities across various ministries and agencies. At that time, there

 Allocation of the Government’s R&D Budget
The general budgeting process is as follows.
A t t h e b e g i n n i n g o f t h e y e a r , t h e M i n i s t r y o f E c o n o m y a n d F i n a nc e ( M O E F ) , w h i c h i s

However, the R&D budget flow differs from that of other sectors in order to accommodate
the specialisation of S&T. Since 1999, the Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT) has been
involved in R&D budgeting.
At the start of the year, MOEF, with assistance from MSIT, sets budget ceilings for each
ministry, and MSIT establishes the direction of R&D investment for the upcoming year. All
ministries submit their R&D budget proposals for the next year to MOEF and MSIT, which
then review them. MSIT distributes and coordinates the budget for the next year's principal
R&D programs and submits the results to MOEF. MOEF allocates the budget for all R&D
programs, including the principal R&D programs, and submits the budget bill to the National
Assembly. The R&D budget for the next year is finally deliberated and conf irmed b y th e
National Assembly.

 Issues and Suggestions
At the national level, R&D funding should be promoted while ensuring strong links with
other sectors within the nationa l science and technology manage ment system. R&D
program funding should be closely aligned with national science and technology policy, and
the budget for the following year should be prepared with sufficient consideration of the
results of R&D program evaluations.

At the national level, it is cru cial that R&D funding adequatel y reflects the unique
characteristics of science and technology in the budgeting process, distinguishing it from
budgeting in other fields. BRIN n eeds to assess ways to establi sh a mid- to long-term
national R&D investment strategy that can serve as a reference for other related
organisations. Furthermore, it should announce the direction and standards for national
R&D investment at the beginning of each year. Additionally, BRIN could provide the Ministry
of Finance (MoF) with the results of professional reviews of the budget proposals for major
R&D programs submitted by related organisations, while the MoF may need to consider
these results when preparing the budget allocation for the next year.
The procedures for efficiently promoting R&D funding and the roles of each entity must be
clearly established. The roles of the major implementing entities, such as Bappenas, the
MoF, BRIN, and R&D performing organisations, also need to be ef fectively defined and
established. Furthermore, it is essential to establish systems and processes aimed at
enhancing the efficiency and effectiveness of R&D budget allocation and adjustment.
This is because there are organisations within BRIN that manage R&D project funding and
institutions that perform R&D, making it necessary to establish a s y s t e m b y w h i c h t h e
budgets can be evaluated and managed independently.

 Expected Effects in the Future
The results of research and analysis of Indonesia's R&D funding system and a comparison
with Korea's R&D funding system were used to derive directions for improving Indonesia's
R&D funding system in the future.
It is crucial to establish a syst em that can adequately incorporate the characteristics of
science and technology into the national R&D funding process. To efficiently promote R&D
funding, it is essential to define effective roles for the major players, including Bappenas,
the MoF, BRIN, and R&D performing organisations, and to establish sophisticated systems
and processes for the efficient distribution and coordination of R&D budgets.

1. Summary

Research and innovation (R&I) infrastructure and funding have become critical factors in
developing the science-technology-innovation (STI) environment in many countries around
t h e w o r l d . S o u t h K o r e a , a s o n e o f t h e m o s t a d v a n c e d , c u t t i n g - e dge countries, has

Similar to Indonesia, South Korea h a s a l o n g i n s t i t u t i o n a l h i s tory concerning the
transformation of STI organizations. Currently, Indonesia’s STI organisation is solely
delivered by BRIN. As a new entity, BRIN should improve and accelerate the STI institution
to achieve the national development goals. It is the reason why Indonesia should learn more
from the South Korea’s practice in implementing R&I infrastructures and funding to bolster
and accelerate national STI development.
As regards the R&I infrastructure in Indonesia, especially at B R I N , o n e c a n r e f e r t o t h e
aforementioned definition of R&I infrastructure as “facilities, resources and related services

or facilities, or databases for disseminating research data.” As a newly integrated R&D
institution in Indonesia, BRIN is on the adaptation and consolidation phase. BRIN has faced
various obstacles and challenges since October 2021, and has resolved several of them, but
some still remain as major issues. An integrated and centralised R&I infrastructure delivered
by BRIN is one of the issues that urgently needs to resolved as soon as possible.

2. Policy Recommendations

South Korea’s explanation has given much insight for thought for Indonesia, especially for
BRIN as the sole R&D institution funded by the government. We have prepared two typical
policy recommendations aimed at improving the performance of BRIN in this regard: one
concerns the research and innovation infrastructure, and the other research and innovation
funding. The majority of our policy recommendations for Indonesia are taken from empirical
results and South Korea’s experience and modified accordingly.

A. Improvement of Research & Innovation (R&I) Infrastructure Performance
Establishing an R&I infrastructure ecosystem is the most appropriate policy that BRIN can
execute.
1. As a first measure, BRIN should create three plans by referring to South Korea’s practical
experience: i) establish a system for investing and managing th e R&I infrastructure and
unifying the decentralised management system across government ministries; ii) Lay the
foundations for utilising the research infrastructure by linking and integrating the utilisation
systems of ministries and technologies into one; iii) establish a field-centred management
and utilisation system.
2. Establish a system and management direction for providing comprehensive facilities and
equipment to support the R&I infrastructure; create and decentr alise duties and
responsibilities autonomously; and improve the quality and quan tity of personnel who
operate R&I infrastructures, such as training, career management, and specialised jobs.
3. Arrange sequential planning for the medium to long term (5-20 years), starting with the
identification of recent issues and the status of R&I infrastructures; create distinctive roles
for stakeholders by involving central and local government agen cies, R&D institutes,
researchers, engineers, policy an alysts, and other related user s; accommodate and
prioritise the diverse demands of researchers and users of R&I infrastructures including R&D

management planning supported by the legal system and a virtuous management cycle.
4 . P r o m o t e R & I i n f r a s t r u c t u r e i n v e s t m e n t b y a t t r a c t i n g p r i v a t e sectors, universities, and
overseas partners efficiently and effectively. This will require the creation of an efficient and

5. Monitor and evaluate existing and new schemes of R&I infrastructure investment at BRIN

B. Improvement of Research & Innovation (R&I) Funding Performance
1. BRIN should establish and strengthen an interlinked system with the Ministry of Finance,
other ministries, assemblies, and public boards in proposing R&I funding allocation. This
system is fruitful to accelerating in managing public R&I funding in Indonesia.
2. It is essential to plan a sequential budget process: This can be divided into short-term
funding planning (1-2 years), medium-term funding planning (3-5 years), and long-term
funding planning (more than 5 years). The master plan and budget priorities are the basic
elements to be formulated by BRIN in order to provide clear and responsible information to
stakeholders and society.
