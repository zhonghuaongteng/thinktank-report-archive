About This Report
China’s 2025 retirement reform raised some of the world’s lowest statutory retirement ages
as the country’s working-age population entered sustained decline and as artificial intelligence
began to reshape its labor market. This Research Report uses a monthly birth-cohort simulation
to estimate the statutory working-age window created by the reform and examines its
implications for China’s 2035 modernization agenda, productivity, pension timing, and labor-
market adjustment.
This analysis should interest policymakers and analysts concerned with China’s economic
trajectory, demographic and pension policy, and the labor-market effects of AI. It is part of a
broader RAND China Research Center project on China 2035.

Summary
China’s 2025 retirement reform creates a temporary statutory working-age window rather
than a solution to population aging. Using a monthly birth-cohort simulation, this report
estimates the reform’s effect through 2050 and examines what would be required for the
additional statutory headcount to become productive economic capacity. In the preferred
scenario, the reform keeps the statutory working-age population broadly stable through the mid-
2030s and leaves about 48 million more people below statutory retirement thresholds in 2035
than under the old rules. Its direct contribution to growth is limited, however, and its broader
value depends on older-worker participation, employer demand, productivity, service-sector
absorption, and how artificial intelligence affects the occupations represented among the retained
cohorts.
• The reform creates a temporary statutory working-age window, not a solution to aging. In
the preferred central scenario, it holds China’s statutory working-age population roughly
flat through the mid-2030s—a rise of about 5.5 million between 2024 and 2035, against a
decline of roughly 43 million under the old rules. Relative to the old rules, it keeps about
28 million additional people below the statutory retirement threshold by 2030 and about
48 million by 2035.
• The period of broad stability ends after the mid-2030s; the reform effect itself remains
large into the 2040s. The reform delays the decline in the working-age population but
does not reverse it. The magnitude of the buffering effect peaks in the early-to-mid 2040s
at roughly 66 to 79 million additional people depending on specification, and then it
erodes as the statutory population resumes a sustained decline. This quantity refers to
statutory headcount, not realized employment.
• The cushion depends disproportionately on extending the working lives of blue-collar
women. Women account for roughly 54 percent of the 2035 effect. Blue-collar women on
the lower-wage “old-50” track, who receive the reform’s largest increase of five years,
supply about 38 percent of the total.
• The reform’s direct contribution to growth is small because labor had already stopped
driving Chinese economic growth. Over 2016–2023, labor contributed essentially nothing
to GDP growth while capital and productivity carried almost all of it. Even on the
generous assumption that every retained person works, the reform would leave annual
growth through 2035 only about 0.3 percentage points higher than it would be under the
old rules. This is a minuscule number relative to the roughly 4.17 percent average annual
GDP growth target that Beijing has identified as necessary to achieve its 2035 income
target.
• Whether the window becomes true economic capacity depends on productivity, demand,
and AI’s effect on the retained workers. Meeting the 2035 target increasingly falls on
productivity as capital’s contribution fades. Many women assigned to the old-50 proxy
are in frontline, production, service, or manual occupation groups. Whether AI
complements or substitutes for tasks prevalent in those occupations is an open empirical

what AI does with it. The reform is therefore a complement to, but not a substitute for,
China’s productivity agenda. Its payoff will depend on whether older-worker

retained cohorts.

Chapter 1. Main Claim: A Window, Not a Solution
China’s pool of working-age people is shrinking. That decline weighs directly on economic
growth, and the impact deepens over time. Beijing has been searching for policy levers that can
slow the loss. China’s January 2025 retirement reform creates a temporary statutory working-age
(SWA) window through 2035. It buys time, but it does not solve population aging by itself.
China’s historic population decline reflects both past policies and current economic
constraints. Decades of birth restrictions, including the one-child policy, reduced the size of
younger cohorts and reshaped family norms; urbanization and the rising cost of raising children
have further discouraged births even after birth limits were relaxed (“China’s Population Drops
for Fourth Year as Fewer Babies Born,” 2026). China has one of the world’s highest childrearing
costs relative to gross domestic product (GDP) per capita (“China’s Childcare Costs Among
Highest in World—Think Tank,” 2024). In 2025, births fell to 7.92 million while deaths reached
11.31 million, marking China’s fourth consecutive year of population decline (“China’s
Population Drops for Fourth Year as Fewer Babies Born,” 2026). These conditions have
produced an increasingly top-heavy population pyramid, with smaller cohorts of future labor-
market entrants and growing older age groups (Figure 1.1). Fewer young cohorts will enter the
labor market, and larger older cohorts place rising pressure on pensions, health care, and elder
care.

Chapter 2. Why Statutory Working-Age Population Matters
Conventional working-age measures, such as the 15–64 band used in United Nations
demographic tabulations (United Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs,
Population Division, 2024) and China’s official 16–59 band (National Bureau of Statistics of
China, 2025b), are useful for broad demographic comparison. However, in the context of China,
they miss a central institutional feature of its retirement system: statutory retirement rules
historically differed by gender and occupation. Under the pre-reform system (from 1978 until
January 2025), male employees (男职工) generally retired at 60, women in cadre, managerial, or
professional-technical posts (女干部 / 管理技术岗位女职工) at 55, and women classified as
workers—often in blue-collar, frontline, or production roles (女工人 / 生产操作岗位女职工)—
at 50 (State Council of the People’s Republic of China, 1978; Standing Committee of the
National People’s Congress, 2024). The SWA measure is therefore a sharper proxy for the
population legally below retirement thresholds than a flat age-band measure, and it allows the
reform’s mechanical effect to be quantified as the increases phase in. For reference, China’s
National Bureau of Statistics (NBS) reported 858.0 million people aged 16–59 at year-end 2024,
while the constructed statutory measure in the preferred central scenario based on the three
retirement cutoffs was calculated to be roughly 781 million (National Bureau of Statistics of
China, 2025b). The discrepancy reflects women aged 50–59 (old-50 track) and 55–59 (old-55
track) who are past their statutory thresholds but inside the 16–59 band; men do not contribute,
because their threshold coincides with the band’s edge.
At the same time, the SWA population should not be confused with actual labor supply.
Some people below the statutory retirement age are out of the labor force; some above it
continue working, especially in informal or agricultural work. Many older rural migrants and
retirees continue working because pension benefits are inadequate, while the 2024 retirement
reform itself permits flexible early or delayed retirement under specified conditions (“In Rapidly
Ageing China, Millions Can’t Afford to Retire,” 2024; Standing Committee of the National
People’s Congress, 2024). Employer demand, health, disability, early-retirement choices, and
pension eligibility all affect realized employment. The value of the model is that it isolates the
policy lever: the change in legal thresholds and the population affected by those thresholds.

Chapter 3. Data and Methods
This report estimates how China’s January 2025 statutory retirement reform changes the
country’s statutory working-age population through 2050. The object of analysis is the number
of people below the statutory retirement age in each year. That is the quantity the reform directly
moves. The model counts the population from age 16, matching the lower bound of China’s
official 16–59 working-age population category, up to each group’s applicable statutory
retirement age (National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2025b). The headcount is distinct from
realized labor supply: it does not predict how many people will work, retire, or participate in the
labor force. Chapter 6 returns to that distinction.
The estimate comes from a monthly birth-cohort simulation, detailed in Appendix G. For
each year, the model divides the population by sex and single year of age into monthly
subcohorts and evaluates whether each subcohort remains below the applicable statutory
retirement threshold at the model’s mid-year reference point. Each subcohort is assigned a
statutory retirement age under two rule sets: the pre-reform rules in effect through 2024 and the
reform’s phased schedule beginning in January 2025. The reform raises retirement ages in
monthly increments, subject to the statutory three- and five-year caps. The reform effect is the
difference between the computed SWA populations under the old rules and the reform. Because
both paths are built on the same population data, the difference isolates the mechanical effect of
the policy change.
The analysis draws on four main data sources, detailed in Appendix A. Population by sex and
single year of age comes from the UN World Population Prospects 2024, accessed through the
UN Population Data Portal, with estimates through 2023 and projections from 2024 onwards
(United Nations, Department of Economic and Social Affairs, Population Division, 2024).
Female retirement-track shares are constructed from UN-published Chinese census occupation
tabulations for 2010 and 2020 under the International Standard Classification of Occupations,
2008 revision (ISCO-08), by sex and five-year age group (United Nations Statistics Division,
2026). Historical urbanization rates from 1949 onward are drawn from China’s National Bureau
of Statistics and are used only in the legacy urbanization-based specification described below
(National Bureau of Statistics of China, 2025a). The growth-accounting context in Chapter 7
uses Penn World Table 11.0 (Feenstra, Inklaar, and Timmer, 2015).
The central modeling choice is how to divide women between the lower, old-50 track and the
higher, old-55 track, because the reform treats the two tracks differently. Women in the old-50
track move from retirement at 50 to retirement at 55, a five-year increase. Women in the old-55
track move from 55 to 58, a three-year increase. Assigning a large proportion of women to the
old-50 track would increase the estimated reform effect, because more women receive a larger
statutory increase. This split cannot be clearly determined from official data. A woman’s track

7
follows her cadre or worker job classification, which China does not publish as a population
aggregate, so no comprehensive public dataset reports how the female working-age population is
divided between the two retirement categories. The share within each track must therefore be
estimated rather than observed.
The analysis reports seven specifications built on two approaches. The first is a legacy
urbanization-based specification, S1, detailed in Appendix E. It uses the urban share of the
population as a proxy for the share of women on the white-collar, old-55 track. This was a
simple starting proxy, but it has a clear weakness. The urban share overstates the white-collar
female share: in the 2020 census year, it implies that roughly 64 percent of women are on the
higher track, compared with roughly 22 percent in the census occupation data from the UN
(United Nations Statistics Division, 2026). Because the old-55 female group receives the smaller
statutory increase, this overstatement of its proportion tends to understate the reform effect. S1 is
therefore retained as a legacy high-old-55 comparison and lower-effect benchmark, not as the
preferred estimate or a formal lower bound.
The second approach is occupation-grounded. Specifications S2 through S7 assign women to
retirement-track proxies using census occupation shares under ISCO-08 rather than using
urbanization. These specifications vary two assumptions: how service-and-sales occupations are
allocated between the tracks, and how far occupation shares are projected across birth cohorts.
The central specification is S5, which sets θ = 0.25 and β = 0.5. Here, θ is the share of service-
and-sales workers assigned to the old-55 track, and β governs how much of the observed 2010–
2020 occupational upgrading is projected forward. The occupation-grounded model is the
primary basis for the report’s estimates. Full definitions of the seven specifications appear in
Appendix F.
The simulation went through several checks to verify the accuracy of the input data, the
implementation of the policy rules, and the sensitivity of the results to uncertainty. Model inputs
and policy parameters were checked against their sources. The retirement-schedule
implementation was checked at multiple checkpoints for every cohort’s timeline: initial policy
phase-in, intermediate phase-in points, and the concluding statutory three- and five-year caps.
The exact monthly simulation was cross-validated against an approximate cutoff method, which
matched the old-rules series and converged with the exact method once the reform fully phased
in. The seven specifications serve as sensitivity tests around the female retirement-track
assumptions. Key numerical outputs were checked manually and programmatically. The coding
notebook runs from a clean state with all checks passing.

Chapter 4. Simulation Results
The monthly cohort simulation applies the official reform schedule by birth month rather
than approximating it as a flat age increase. Men and women in the old 55-year track gain one
retirement-age month for every four birth months, capped at 36 months; women in the old 50-
year track gain one month for every two birth months, capped at 60 months (Standing Committee
of the National People’s Congress, 2024). The corresponding pre-reform SWA population is
computed in the same framework using the old thresholds, so the two series are produced from a
common population basis and differ only in the retirement age applied.
In the preferred occupation-grounded central scenario (S5), China’s SWA population stood at
roughly 781 million in 2024, the last full year before the reform. Compared with a steep decline
in the SWA population under the old rules, the reform-adjusted statutory working-age population
remains broadly stable through the early-to-mid 2030s. In S5, the SWA population rises by 5.5
million between 2024 and 2035, compared with a 42.8 million decline under the old-rules
counterfactual. Across the scenarios, which vary the female split, the 2024–2035 change ranges
from a decline of 7.9 million to an increase of 6.8 million. Compared with a future under the old
rules, S5 retains 28 million additional people below the statutory threshold by 2030, about 48
million by 2035, and roughly 67 million at peak in 2043. Across all scenarios, the 2035 effect
ranges from about 45 million to 55 million additional people (Table 4.1).

Chapter 5. Why the Female 50/55 Split Matters
The largest modeling uncertainty is the female split between the old 55-year and old 50-year
retirement tracks. This matters because the reform does not raise both female tracks by the same
amount. Women in the old-55 track move to 58, while women in the old-50 track move to 55
(Standing Committee of the National People’s Congress, 2024). Assigning too many women to
the old-55 track would understate the reform’s effect because it overestimates the share of
women who receive the smaller three-year increase rather than the larger five-year increase.
The urbanization-based specification used the national urbanization rate as a direct proxy for
the old-55 share, which assigned roughly 64 percent of women to the old-55 track in the 2020
census year, reflecting increases in urbanization over history and into the projected future. That
specification is retained as a legacy high-old-55-share comparison (S1). UN-published Chinese
census tabulations—UNdata occupation data for 2010 and 2020, coded under ISCO-08—provide
a more direct signal. In 2020, analysis of the occupation data suggests that only about 22 percent
of employed women worked in core white-collar occupations at the all-age level with only about
12 percent among women aged 50–54, a near-retirement group that is especially relevant for
evaluating the old-50 track. The specific classification of roles into blue- and white-collar
occupations is defined in Appendix D. The occupation-grounded scenarios therefore assign more
women to the old-50 track, which raises the estimated reform effect (United Nations Statistics
Division, 2026; author’s calculations).
This explains the main pattern in Table 4.1. At the 2035 benchmark, the occupation-
grounded scenarios produce a retained SWA population at least as large as—and in the static-
share cases substantially larger than—that of the legacy urbanization proxy. The same holds true
for the peak reform effect. The classification of service-and-sales workers affects the estimate
but does not drive the result: in the cohort-trended scenarios, varying their classification shifts
the 2035 effect by several million people, but the broader conclusion is unchanged—both female
tracks are raised under the reform, and the old-50 track receives the larger statutory increase. Full
details on the occupation mapping, the service-and-sales sensitivity parameter, and the cohort-
trending assumptions are provided in Appendix D and Appendix F.
The distributional implication is more important than the technical one. In the preferred
central scenario, women account for roughly 54 percent of the 2035 reform effect and men for 46
percent. Within the female component, the old-50 track—the empirical majority, receiving the
five-year increase—contributes roughly 70 percent, or about 38 percent of the total effect across
both genders. As successive cohorts enter the reform windows, male and old-55-track female
contributions grow through the 2040s (Figure 5.1).

Chapter 6. What the Model Does Not Show
The model is most useful as a policy-timing and headcount simulation by showing the size
and distribution of the statutory cushion created by the reform. However, the following features
are out of the model’s scope:
• Actual employment or participation. The model estimates statutory eligibility, not
whether people work.
• Informal work and effective retirement behavior. Many people work after formal
retirement; others exit earlier for health, caregiving, or employment reasons. Many
nominally retired women provide childcare and eldercare within families, and formal
support systems lean on that unpaid work; retaining these cohorts longer would reshuffle
care arrangements as well as labor supply. Age and gender preferences in hiring add a
further headwind, and these distributional effects sit outside the headcount.
• Pension contribution and benefit flows. The model implies fiscal timing effects but
does not quantify receipts, outlays, or contribution compliance.
• Employer demand for older workers. Statutory eligibility matters only if firms retain,
hire, or retrain older workers.
• AI’s realized labor impact. The model does not estimate whether AI complements,
substitutes for, or displaces the cohorts retained below the threshold.
• Internal policy intent. The overlap between the reform window and China’s 2035
modernization horizon is analytically important, but the model does not prove intent.

Chapter 7. What the Window Is Worth: The Reform, AI, and 2035
The retirement reform’s timing coincides with China’s 2035 modernization horizon, but how
the reform contributes to that goal will also depend on the economy’s productive capacity. The
reform’s effect operates through the labor channel — not capital or productivity — but labor is
no longer driving Chinese growth. Between 2016 and 2023, labor’s contribution to GDP growth
was essentially zero; capital and productivity carried almost all of it (Feenstra, Inklaar, and
Timmer, 2015; Figure 7.1). Keeping tens of millions of people in the statutory workforce slows
the demographic drag on that contribution, but the reform alone cannot make labor a new engine
of growth. Even on the generous assumption that every person the reform retains below the
statutory threshold works, the avoided decline in labor input would leave annual growth through
2035 only about 0.3 percentage points higher than under the old rules (Appendix J).

Chapter 9. Conclusion
China’s 2025 retirement reform is best understood as a temporary statutory buffer. It slows
the near-term decline in the population below retirement thresholds, keeping the SWA
population steady in the 2025–2035 window. The burden is concentrated on blue-collar women
and on younger birth cohorts. It does not reverse aging, and it does not guarantee realized labor
supply. It may delay some benefit claims and extend contribution periods, but the model does not
quantify pension cash-flow effects.
The policy significance lies in the overlap between that statutory window and China’s 2035
modernization horizon, a coincidence that this analysis can quantify but not explain. The reform
buys time, but the open question is what Beijing does with it. If AI and automation complement
older workers, raise productivity, and expand labor-intensive services, the reform could support
an effective demographic transition. If AI instead accelerates skill mismatch, labor-light output,
and displacement, it may only postpone a sharper reckoning. The model’s contribution is to
make the timing visible by showing that a window exists, how large it may be, and which groups
bear the adjustment. It does not forecast how China will use the window. That uncertainty is
precisely why participation, employer demand, pension behavior, service-sector absorption, and
AI’s complement-versus-substitute effects should be tracked closely through the mid-2030s.

Appendix D. The Pre-reform Structure and the Female-track Split
Under the rules in effect from 1978 through 2024, men retired at 60, old-55-track women at
55, and old-50-track women at 50, following the conventional cadre/worker (干部/工人)
distinction (State Council of the People’s Republic of China, 1978; Standing Committee of the
National People’s Congress, 2024). The simulation computes the male statutory population
directly and decomposes the female population into the two tracks. The female-track
proportional split is the central methodological choice.
The original analysis used each year’s NBS urbanization rate as a proxy for the female old-
55 share, with the rural share assigned to the old-50 track and projection years extrapolated from
a fitted logistic curve (Appendix E). This specification is retained as S1. Two limitations
motivated replacing it as the central case: the proxy is applied to the whole female population
rather than to employed women, and the legal cadre/worker distinction is occupational, not
residential.
UN-published census tabulations (2010, 2020) report occupation under ISCO-08 by sex and
five-year age group (United Nations Statistics Division, 2026). The nine ISCO major groups map
onto the cadre/worker distinction as follows, with one ambiguity:
• Old-55 (white-collar): managers, professionals, technicians and associate professionals,
and clerical support workers.
• Old-50 (blue-collar): skilled agricultural and forestry workers; craft and related trades;
plant and machine operators; elementary occupations.
• Ambiguous: service and sales workers—partly office-adjacent, partly frontline. The data
do not disambiguate within the major group, so the assignment is treated as a sensitivity
parameter (θ).
The 2020 cross-section shows the urbanization proxy substantially overstates the white-collar
share. Despite a roughly 64 percent urbanization rate, the actual share of employed women in
core white-collar occupations was about 22 percent at all ages. That white-collar female share
fell with age, to roughly 28 percent at 35–39, 17 percent at 45–49, and 12 percent at 50–54 (the
cohorts closest to the first female reform windows), and about 6 percent at 55–59. Comparing
2010 and 2020 at fixed cohorts shows women did move into white-collar work over the decade,
but well below the pace of urbanization growth, which motivates the sensitivity framework in
Appendix F.

Appendix E. The Original Proxy: the Urbanization Model
Why an Urbanization Proxy (S1) was Considered
The legal cadre/worker (干部/工人) distinction that sets the female retirement tracks is not
directly observed in the population data: the censuses and the UN single-age series report people
by age and sex, not by retirement track. The original analysis therefore needed an observable,
time-varying signal that plausibly correlates with the white-collar (old-55) versus blue-collar
(old-50) division and is available annually back to 1949 and projectable to 2050. Urbanization
was a natural first candidate. China’s urban–rural divide tracks the formal/informal and white-
collar/manual split more closely than almost any other series the state reports continuously:
urban employment skews toward the office, administrative, and professional work associated
with the cadre track, while rural employment skews toward agriculture and manual labor
associated with the worker track. The urban share is also one of the longest, most complete, and
most regularly updated series in Chinese official statistics, which suited a model needing
consistent coverage from 1949 through a 2050 projection horizon. The proxy was a defensible
first approximation given the data constraints, not an arbitrary choice, which is why it is
documented here rather than simply discarded.

linear model): the goal is a smooth S-shaped path for a single percentage series, with the fitted
value constrained between an estimated floor and ceiling, both bounded to the 0–100 percent
interval. The functional form is as follows:

extrapolated. A more rigorously projected urban share would still carry the same specification

replacing it is rooted in its large discrepancy with the ISCO data that no refinement of the fit

Appendix F. Sensitivity framework: Two parameters, Seven
scenarios
The simulations carry two main points of uncertainty. The first is how female service and
sales workers divide between the old-55 and old-50 tracks, which no Chinese dataset reports
directly (θ). The second is how much of the occupational upgrading observed within birth
cohorts between the 2010 and 2020 censuses continues after 2020 (β). This appendix defines the
seven scenarios and how they treat these uncertainties.
θ — classification of service and sales workers. θ is the fraction of female service and sales
workers assigned to the old-55 track, with the rest assigned to old-50. θ = 0 treats the group as
blue-collar; θ = 1 assigns it entirely to the old-55 track. 0.25 and 0.50 are intermediate values,
with 0.25 the preferred central value, reflecting the prior that most service and sales work among
Chinese women remains informal or frontline (blue-collar) rather than office-adjacent (white-
collar).
β — projection of cohort upgrading. β controls how much of the female occupational
upgrading (from blue-collar to white-collar) observed between the 2010 and 2020 censuses is
projected forward after 2020. The model measures occupational upgrading by tracking birth
cohorts across the two censuses. Because a woman aged 30–34 in 2010 is part of the same cohort
as a woman aged 40–44 in 2020, comparing that group’s θ-weighted old-55 share at the two
dates gives the cohort’s change over the decade. This is a within-cohort trend rather than a
comparison of different generations. The model computes this for four such cohort pairs (2010
ages 20–24 through 35–39, matched to 2020 ages 30–34 through 45–49), converts each into an
annual rate of change in logit space, and projects each birth cohort forward from its 2020 share at
β times its own rate, with the result clipped to a floor of 1 percent and a ceiling of 75 percent.
Cohorts born in 1988 or earlier, which would be older than the oldest matched cohort, carry no
trend and are held at their 2020 level. β = 0 freezes the 2020 structure (the static scenarios); β =
0.5 continues each trend at half its observed rate (applied to all cohort-trended scenarios, S4–S7);
β = 1.0 would fully extrapolate the observed trend but is defined for completeness only. Table
F.1 summarizes the seven specifications and their roles in the sensitivity design.
The role labels describe each scenario’s function in the design with no scenario as a
definitive upper or lower bound on the reform’s possible effect (Table F.1). The family spans
uncertainty in the female-track split, conditional on the model’s structure (population held fixed
across the two rule sets, no behavioral response, no participation modeled).

Appendix G. The Monthly Cohort Simulation
The simulation estimates the population below the statutory retirement threshold under two
sets of rules: the pre-reform system and the 2025 reform. The reform raises retirement ages on a
sliding birth-cohort schedule, so each calendar year contains multiple cohorts at different points
in the phase-in. Men and women on the old-55 track gain one retirement-age month for every
four birth months, capped at 36 months (three years). Women on the old-50 track gain one month
for every two birth months, capped at 60 months (five years). The full increase therefore binds
for men born in 1977 or later, old-55-track women born in 1982 or later, and old-50-track
women born in 1985 or later. The simulation uses single-year UN age data and, within each age
year, decomposes the population into twelve monthly sub-cohorts under a uniform-within-year
assumption. For each sub-cohort, it infers birth month, applies the cohort-specific delay, and
checks status at a mid-year (July) reference point. For each year, the female population is split
between blue-collar and white-collar tracks by the scenario share; men are modeled through a
single track, with the 60→63 phase-in applied to all.
The old-rules counterfactual is produced by running the same monthly birth-cohort model on
the same UN population data, changing only the retirement ages applied, which are the pre-
reform ages (60/55/50) instead of the phased-in new ones. Because the two series differ in
nothing but the retirement rule, the gap between them isolates the reform’s effect.

Appendix H. A Note on θ–β Extrapolation Consistency
The cohort-trended scenarios apply the β trend to the blended θ-weighted old-55 share
(Appendix F), rather than projecting the underlying core white-collar and service-and-sales
components separately and combining them via θ afterward. Trending the already-blended share
is a simplification: it lets θ and the projected trend interact in a way that component-level
projection would avoid. This blended approach is irrelevant in the static scenarios (S2, S3)
because the 2020 distribution is applied to all future years. Future work could project the two
components separately and combine them via θ, isolating the classification choice from the trend.

Appendix I. Reading the Scenario Results
The scenarios remain close in the near term because only a limited number of cohorts have
entered the phase-in. They diverge in the late 2030s and 2040s as more cohorts reach the affected
retirement ages and the alternative female-track assumptions accumulate. Three points are worth
flagging. First, the pre-reform baseline itself varies across scenarios. Because the old retirement
ages themselves differ by track (55 for the old-55 track, 50 for the old-50 track), the count of
women below the old threshold depends on how women are split between tracks—the same
assumption that varies across scenarios (θ and β in the occupation-grounded scenarios, the urban
share in S1). The occupation-grounded scenarios put most women on the old-50 track, whose
pre-reform age (50) is five years lower, which lowers their old-rules baseline accordingly. Each
scenario therefore has its own matched pair of old-rules and reform series, and the reform effect
is the gap between them within that scenario—not the gap between a scenario’s reform line and
some common baseline.
Second, S3 produces the largest reform effect in the family (about +78 million at 2040, peak
+79 million) largely because threshold-age cohorts carry the lowest old-55 shares under its
frozen age profile. Third, the scenarios do not converge to a single 2050 value: the reform still
holds roughly 64 to 71 million additional people below statutory thresholds in 2050, ranging
from 63.6 million in the legacy proxy (S1) to approximately 71.2–71.4 million in the static-share
scenarios (S2, S3), with the cohort-trended scenarios (S4–S7) between about 64.7 and 65.5
million. Within the cohort-trended specification, varying θ from 0 to 1 moves the 2035 effect
only from 50.5 to 45.6 million, because shifting women between tracks changes the size of each
delay (+3 versus +5 years), not whether they are delayed. Because men do not experience an
occupational split under the reform, the aggregate men’s +3-year delay results in the largest
single block within the three-way decomposition.

Appendix J. Growth Accounting and the 2035 Productivity
Benchmark
Chapter 7 places the reform against China’s 2035 income goal using a standard growth-
accounting decomposition. This part of the appendix sets out the data, parameters, identity, and
sensitivity logic behind the figures cited there.
Data and Parameters
The decomposition uses PWT version 11.0 (Feenstra, Inklaar, and Timmer, 2015). Output,
capital input, and labor input are taken from PWT’s real (inflation-adjusted) series; purchasing-
power-parity conversions are not used, since the exercise concerns growth within China rather
than cross-country level comparisons. Total factor productivity is not measured directly as it is
the residual in the growth-accounting identity, the part of output growth not explained by capital
and labor. PWT publishes its own TFP series (rtfpna). Moreover, computing the residual
independently from PWT’s output, capital, and labor figures reproduces a TFP that is essentially
equal to the published TFP series. This confirms internal consistency within the PWT data.
Period averages are taken over the following three intervals: 2000–2007, 2008–2015, and 2016–
2023. These period intervals are used to show the macro trajectory. The capital share used in the
recent-period accounting is α = 0.427 (the 2016–2023 average from PWT labor-share data), so
the labor share is 1 − α = 0.573.
Note: PWT’s TFP series is one estimate among several, and measures of China’s recent TFP
growth diverge widely across sources and methods (Lee, 2025). The labor-contribution bound in
this appendix is unaffected by that dispute: it is computed from the labor share and the difference
in labor-input growth, not from the TFP residual.

Over 2016–2023 the PWT averages are gY = 5.50, gK = 7.89, gL = −0.08, and gA = 2.19 percent a

The Narrowing of the Growth Model
TFP growth has slowed across the three periods: about 4.18 percent a year in 2000–2007,
2.43 percent in 2008–2015, and 2.19 percent in 2016–2023. Labor’s contribution to growth is
labor-input growth weighted by the labor share. Over the same intervals it fell from about 1.0
percentage point in the 2000s to roughly zero, turning slightly negative in the most recent period:
with g_L = −0.08 percent and a labor share of 0.573, the contribution is (1 − α)·g_L ≈ −0.05
percentage points. The statement in Chapter 7 that labor contributes ‘roughly nothing’ refers to
the contribution, not to labor-input growth itself. Capital’s contribution also fell from about 5.1
percentage points in 2008–2015 to about 3.4 points in 2016–2023 but remains the largest single
component.
The 2035 Benchmark
The productivity target in Chapter 7 is Beijing’s own. An official study volume
accompanying the 15th Five-Year Plan proposals, with contributions from People’s Bank of
China Governor Pan Gongsheng and Finance Minister Lan Foan, puts the average annual GDP
growth required to reach the 2035 income goal at 4.17 percent over the next decade. The figure
follows from a per capita GDP target above US$20,000 by 2035 and an expected population
decline of about 0.2 percent a year (“China Needs 4.17% Annual GDP Growth to Meet 2035
Goal, Publication Shows,” 2025). It is a benchmark the analysis tests against, not a finding of
this analysis.
Required-TFP Sensitivity
Applying the identity with the target gY = 4.17 percent, the capital share α = 0.427, and labor
input held at its 2016–2023 average (gL = −0.08 percent), the TFP growth required to meet the
target depends on assumed capital-input growth. The required values are 2.08 percent at 5
percent capital-input growth, 2.51 percent at 4 percent, and 2.94 percent at 3 percent. At the
higher capital-input rates of 6 and 7 percent, required TFP falls to 1.65 and 1.23 percent. Recent
TFP growth of 2.19 percent therefore just meets the target if capital-input growth holds near 5
percent and falls short as capital-input growth slows below it. For reference, capital-input growth
averaged 7.89 percent over 2016–2023, so every row of the sensitivity table assumes a slowdown
from the recent pace (Table 7.1). These are illustrative sensitivities, not forecasts; they hold labor
input and the capital share fixed and vary only capital-input growth.

The One-for-one Bound on the Reform’s Growth Contribution
Chapter 7 states that, even if every person the reform retains below the statutory threshold
were employed, the reform would add only about 0.3 percentage points a year to GDP growth
through 2035, by slowing the decline in labor input that would otherwise drag on growth. The
bound is constructed as follows. In the preferred central scenario (S5), the SWA base grows at
0.06 percent a year over 2024–2035 under the reform, against −0.51 percent a year under the old
rules, which is a difference of about 0.57 percentage points a year in the growth rate of the
statutory base. Treating that base one-for-one as labor input (the generous assumption that every
retained person works) and weighting by the labor share, the effect on GDP growth is
approximately 0.573 × 0.57 ≈ 0.33 percentage points a year. The “one-for-one” framing is
deliberate and generous: PWT labor input embeds hours and human capital, so mapping a
statutory headcount onto labor input overstates the true labor contribution. Therefore, the 0.3-
point figure is an upper bound, not a central estimate.

Appendix K. Technical Caveats
The policy-level limitations are set out in Chapter 6; the modeling-technical caveats are as
follows:
Scenario and Demographic Model (Appendices D-I)
• Mortality is inherited from UN WPP and not separately parameterized, which means the
model inherits WPP’s own uncertainty.
• Uniform-within-year monthly assumption: population is assumed evenly distributed
across the twelve birth months within each age year.
• Female-track classification is a model choice: even with census grounding, the
cadre/worker boundary is assumed as a two-group simplification, and the service-and-
sales group is handled as a sensitivity parameter precisely because the mapping onto
blue-collar and white-collar tracks has uncertainty.
• Cohort upgrading is observed over one decade only: the 2010–2020 shift is the sole
cohort observation; β surfaces but does not resolve this uncertainty. The static scenarios
act as a no-extrapolation benchmark.
• No behavioral response to the reform: the population is held unchanged across rule sets;
effective retirement behavior, take-up of voluntary-delay provisions, and disability flows
are not modeled.
• No quantified AI–labor offset: the analysis does not quantify what fraction of the
declining statutory population AI would substitute for or augment.
Growth Accounting (Chapter 7 and Appendix J)
• The one-for-one bound on the reform’s growth contribution is an upper bound, not an
estimate: PWT labor input embeds hours and human capital, so treating a statutory
headcount as labor input one-for-one overstates the true labor contribution. The figure of
about 0.3 percentage points a year is therefore generous by construction.
• The required-TFP sensitivity is one-dimensional: it holds labor-input growth and the
capital share fixed and varies only capital-input growth. It is an illustration of how the
productivity requirement moves with investment, not a joint projection. The capital share
and labor input could themselves change.
• Penn World Table measurement uncertainty is inherited: the TFP residual, capital stock,
and labor-share series carry PWT’s construction assumptions and periodic revisions,
which the analysis adopts without independent adjustment. This is analogous to the
inherited-WPP caveat above.
• The 4.17 percent benchmark is taken as given, not validated as it is Beijing’s own
arithmetic (per capita GDP above US$20,000 by 2035, population declining about 0.2
percent a year). This parameter is what the analysis tests against rather than an
independent derivation of it.

• TFP is a residual, not a measured quantity: it is what remains after capital and labor are
accounted for, so it absorbs measurement error and everything unmodeled, such as
allocative efficiency, capacity utilization, and technical change. “Productivity” is a
residual label, not a direct observation.
