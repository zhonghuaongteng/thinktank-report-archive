Innovation Policy Transformed? Unveiling a New Paradigm through
Natural Language Processing
Jan Einhoff and Caroline Paunov
Innovation policy is changing. No longer focused solely on growth and
competitiveness, today’s strategies increasingly address societal challenges
such as the green transition, digitalisation, and social inclusion. This paper
provides the quantitative evidence of this shift, analysing STI strategies from
24 OECD countries using natural language processing techniques. It finds
that goals linked to societal impact now make up over 40% of strateg ies’
topics — more than twice that of traditional objectives. New policy
approaches, including joint governance, foresight, and agility, are also
gaining ground. This change in innovation policy is mainly driven by the
prominence of green transition goals in strategies (18.6%), followed by social
and regional inclusion (9.9%) and digitalisation (9.5%). New STI policy
approaches are also prominently featured with references to joint
governance (5.4%), policy agility (5.2%) – such as foresight and evaluation
– and directional policy (2.3%).
Key words: innovation and technology policy, natural language processing, topic modelling,
JEL codes: O38, O31, O25, Q55, L52, C88

Executive summary
A paradigm shift for STI policy?
In recent years, STI policies have come under increasing pressure to address a growing set of complex
challenges, including the digital transformation, the push for environmental sustainability, and the response
to the COVID -19 pandemic. These challenges carry significant social dimensions —such as the risk of
labour displacement from advanced digital technologies and the rising frequency of extreme weather
events—that are prom oting a shift in the rationale for STI policy intervention beyond exclusively
competitiveness and productivity.
This evolving approach, where traditional objectives are intertwined with new priorities, is referred to in the
STI policy literature as ‘Innovation Policy 3.0’. According t o this framework, societal goals —such as
inclusiveness and environmental sustainability —are placed at the centre of STI efforts. Innovation is no
longer presumed to be inherently beneficial; instead, it is assessed based on its broader social and
environmental impacts.
However, there is still limited evidence on the extent to which countries have adopted this new strategic
outlook and implemented it in practice —insights that are essential for assessing the current state of STI
policy and guiding its future direction.
Leveraging topic modelling for exploring STI policy strategies
Capturing the global shift in STI policy is difficult with traditional qualitative methods due to the volume of
policy documents. Topic modelling offers a scalable solution by identi fying patterns and themes across
large text collections for expert analysis.
Using this approach, a study of 313 STI strategies —policy frameworks outlining national priorities, goals,
and actions in STI from 24 OECD countries, validated by national experts—examined whether the stated
policy goals reflect a shift toward Innovation Policy 3.0 and support the adoption of new implementation
approaches.
The approach identified 35 distinct “topics,” where each topic represents a group of words that frequen tly
appear together and capture a coherent theme within the documents. This allowed for analysing how often
and where these themes occur across the entire collection of policy texts.
Green and inclusion goals propel Innovation Policy 3.0 over digital
STI strategies from 24 OECD countries reveal a strong move toward Innovation Policy 3.0 goals, which
represent a 41.7% share of the 35 topics that were identified. While traditional objectives such as research,
business innovation, and productivity remain impo rtant (18.6%), they are now clearly secondary , or
increasingly pursued in conjunction with new imperatives for STI policy.

The green transition is the main driver of this shift (18.6% of topics) . Green transition -related content
significantly focuses on energy generation, CO₂ emissions, and efficiency, accounting for 7.9% of the total
mention of topics; sustainable industry accounts for 3.2%; and clean mobility accounts for 3%. However,
biodiversity, circular economy, and climate resilience are less promin ent, suggesting that these issues
have yet to gain traction in national STI policy documents relative to other green transition topics.
Social and regional inclusion also emerge as a priority (9.9% share of the identified 35 topics ), ahead of
other Innovation Policy 3.0 dimensions like health, knowledge, and technology (3.8% combined). Most
inclusion content focuses on employment and territorial disparities , while b roader societal aspects (e.g.,
citizen engagement, ethics, and public trust in technology) remain underrepresented.
Digital and technological transformation goals make up 9.5% of strategy content (roughly half the focus
given to green priorities). The relatively lower share may reflect the maturity of digital policy, where issues
such as co mpetition, ethics, and infrastructure are increasingly addressed outside STI strategies.
Meanwhile, green STI requires stronger policy intervention and is thus more prominent in current strategic
thinking.
New policy approaches are gaining ground
Strategies also reflect growing attention to how STI policy is implemented. New approaches to STI policy
– which are included as ‘topics’ for the purpose of the analysis – account for 12.8% of the total topics in
the corpus. This trend shows STI strategies not just redefining what STI is for, but also how it should be
governed and delivered.
These k ey emerging approaches include joint governance mechanisms (5.4%) , including vertical
coordination and stakeholder consultation ; policy agility (5.1%) , with focus on monitoring, foresight, and
flexible adaptation; and directional policy tools (2.3%), to steer innovation toward societal outcomes.

In recent years, there has been increasing societal pressure on science, technology, and innovation (STI)
to accelerate the digital and green transitions. The swift progress of digital technologies, such as AI -
enabled tools, automation, and social media, has ignited debates over their potential adverse socio -
economic effects. There is growing policy and academic focus on how governments can steer digital
technological developments in directions that are beneficial to society (Acemoglu and Johnson, 2023 [1]),
which follows from a growing recognition following the COVID-19 pandemic of how STI can support social
and economic resilience beyond the traditional domains of competitiveness (Paunov and Planes-Satorra,
2021[2]).
Addressing the complex transitional challenges that societies face implies a major shift of innovation policy
moving from the first generation, to the second and third. This evolution reflects changes in external drivers,
scope, directionality and objectives, progressing from a focus on basic research to the invention of “science
policy” and ultimately to the emergence of new policy imperatives (Grillitsch et al., 2019[3]). Systematic
empirical evidence to date on where OECD countries’ STI policy goals stand vis-à-vis the new STI policy
paradigm is limited.
Despite widespread enthusiasm about STI’s role in addressing complex societal transitions, it remains
unclear whether the content of national policy frameworks that set national priorities, goals, and actions in
STI has substantively changed in response. This paper uses topic modelling to analyse 313 STI strategies
across 24 OECD countries. Validated by national experts and active in 2021, these strategies are
examined to assess whether the goals the y articulate reflect a shift toward new objectives and in the role
of STI and whether new approaches to policy design and implementation emerge.
The contribution of this paper to the literature is twofold. It is the first to provide systematic evidence on
whether and how STI policy strategies reflect a new societal paradigm . This validates a large body of
conceptual work on new STI policies (Diercks, Larsen and Steward, 2019 [4]; Asheim, Moodysson and
Tödtling, 2011[5]; Mazzucato, 2016 [6]). Second, it demonstrates the usefulness of topic modelling for the
toolbox of STI policy analysis and how to address core methodological challenge these as other tools also
have. In this way, the paper anticipated the work being done in the context of the European Commission-
OECD STIP Compass initiative, which has since begun integrating LLM-driven tools for researchers to use
on the large corpus of policy documents the STIP Compass contains.
Critical to the approach is methodological validation to control for sample selection. The challenge
intensifies for international innovation policy documents due to variations in how countries address
innovation. The number and length of STI strategy documents vary significantly across countries. Not all
countries have standalone innovation strategies; some incorporate innovation wi thin broader economic
and industrial strategies, necessitating the inclusion of different documents in the analysis. Sample biases
are addressed by (i) obtaining validation by country policy experts to ensure that different country practices
are reflected in the sample , and (ii) conducting various sensitivity tests on the sample to assess the
robustness of the overall evidence.

Subjectivity biases may also affect the analysis through the selection of labels for identified themes and
the determination of th e number of themes . These biases are addressed by employing ChatGPT for
systematic labelling. As to the choice of topics, exclusivity and semantics tests are applied to identify the
optimal and cohesive number of topics, minimising the risks of excluding relevant themes. In addition, the
number of topics is also varied to test the robustness of results and identify on that basis dominant themes.
Seven core findings of the analysis are the following: First, evidence from a sample of 24 OECD countries
confirms that the majority has embraced the new policy paradigm, with Innovation Policy 3.0 goals
representing 41.7% of the topics identified in the corpus of documents. There remains a solid focus on
traditional core issues of research and business matters connected to building productivity and growth
(18.6%), but at less than half the importance of Innovation Policy 3.0 goals. Moreover, given strategies’
traditional focus on formulating policy objectives rather than their implementation, that 12.8% of the topics
in the corpus deal with new approaches to innovation policy is remarkably high . It testifies to growing
awareness on the implementation challenges with reaching Innovation Policy 3.0 goals.
Second, the main driver of Innovation Poli cy 3.0’s prominence in strategies is the green transition, which
represents 18.6% of the 35 topics. Energy generation sources, CO2 emissions, and efficiency are a
predominant theme, accounting for 7.9% of the topics. Sustainable industries ( 3.2%) and clean mobility
(3.0%) are also targets. Absent from key themes are biodiversity, circular economy and climate resilience.
The themes reflect lower prioritisation of certain themes in overall green transition-related policy debates.
There are also differences across countries in the emphasis set. For instance, Japan’s strategic documents
emphasise alternative power generation ( 7.6% vs. 2.3% for the average) , a response to the Fukushima
nuclear accident of 2011.
Third, digital and technological transformation goals represent about half the share of green transition goals
(9.5%). The strong emphasis on the green transition compared to digitalisation likely results from the
needed push in green STI. Due to swift progress on d igitalisation, by contrast, policy actions beyond STI
on such matters as competition, ethics and infrastructures have been in high demand.
Fourth, social and regional inclusion is about as prominent as digital and technological transformation goals
(9.9%), ahead of health, knowledge, and technology ( 3.8%), the last and final theme within Innovation
Policy 3.0. Within the inclusion theme , questions relate mostly to employment and regional disparities.
Other topics related to citizens, questions about ethical impacts of technology and innovation, and wider
issues of citizens’ interactions with STI are not yet among the core priorities of strategies. The health topic
is directly a reflection of the COVID -19 pandemic that led to a focus on the question within STI strategies
aside from strategies on health.
Fifth, new approaches to STI policy reflected in strategies include joint governance ( 5.4%) - comprising
vertical governance and consultations-, policy agility (5.1%) -with emphasis on monitoring and evaluation
and foresight-, as well as directional policy ( 2.3%). This is noteworthy and widespread across countries,
indicating awareness that new goals set need different approaches to implementing STI policy. A closer
look at specific examples illustrates a rich diversity of approaches implemented by STI policy makers.
Sixth, a robustness analysis of label accuracy across topic variations highlights the consistency and
variability in topic representation within the strategies. Innovation Policy 3.0 is the most represented
category, yet the analysis reveals that the 35-label model ( the baseline) slightly underrepresents its
prevalence compared to alternative models. Traditional innovation policies and new innovation policy
approaches vary significantly based on the R&D spending level of the countries, with lower R&D countries
focusing more on traditional approaches. The inclusion of miscellaneous topics, despite their procedural
nature, shows significant representation across the corpus, particularly when high-impact countries like the
United States are excluded.

Seventh, methodologically the paper shows that topic modelling can provide valuable comparative insights
into the policy goals countries discuss in their STI strategies. The strength of this analysis is that it enables
the identification of dominant discussions and themes across a large volume of documents, reducing
biases associated with selective reading or purely qualitative approach es. However, topic modelling also
necessitates extensive document preprocessing, such as the removal of stop words, and requires
programming skills. Moreover, it relies on human decisions that may introduce subjectivity, particularly in
selecting the number of topics and interpreting or labelling the results. Additionally, there is a risk of
overlooking important details and nuances in policies and programmes that may not appear in the final
topic model.
The work relates to two strands of literature. The first is the extensive literature on Innovation Policy 3.0,
including notably several empirical studies. This has been articulated in several contributions by Haddad
and Bergek (2023[7]), Edler and Boon, (2018[8]), Schlaile et al., (2017[9]), Weber and Rohracher (2012[10])
and Grillitsch et al. (2019[3]). These STI policy evaluation s rely on qualitative methods such as interviews
and qualitative document analysis , complemented by using available quantitative indicators . The second
strand connects to the quickly expanding literature that applies NLP, which is widely used in adjacent fields
such as in economics (Gentzkow, Kelly and Taddy, 2019[11]) or political sciences (Benoit, 2020[12]), but has
so far had limited application in innovation policy. In innovation-related works, NLP has also been applied
widely to patent and publications data analysis (e.g., Arts et al. (2021[13]).

According to Schot and Steinmueller (2016[14]), the development of innovation policy can be understood
through the lens of three successive "framings" (Grillitsch et al., 2019[3]). Each framing relates to theoretical
models that differ in the STI policy goals to be achieved, their underlying rationale and consequently the
tools required.
"Innovation Policy 1.0" emerged in the 1960s and was strongly influenced by neo -classical welfare
economics and growth theories. The model of innovation in Innovation Policy 1.0 is focused on the
discovery stage because the market may underinvest in such growth-enhancing research (Laranja, Uyarra
and Flanagan, 2008[15]). Policy interventions in the innovation process are then considered justifiable where
markets would not invest in basic research due to their inability to privately appropriate the wider societal
gains from this endeavour (Howlett, Ramesh and Perl, 2020[16]). The main tool applied here is the provision
of research funding conducted by research institutions and firms.
During the 1980s, the second framing of innovation policy, "Innovation Policy 2.0," increasingly focused on
systems of innovation, a “horizontal policy approach that mobilises technology, market mechanisms,
regulations and social innovations” (OECD, 2015, p. 7[17]). The rationale for policy interventions shifted to
system failures that emerge from missing or malfunctioning linkages between research institutions and
firms. New instruments consequently focus on boosting industry-science linkages and knowledge transfer.
The latest framing of STI policies – Innovation Policy 3.0 – sets societal goals at the heart of STI policy
(Diercks, Larsen and Steward, 2019 [4]; Asheim, Moodysson and Tödtling, 2011 [5]; Mazzucato, 2016 [6]),
beyond specific achievements within the STI field as was the case of Innovation Policy 1.0 (boosting basic
research) and Innovation Policy 2.0 (boosting industry-science linkages). As Schot and Steinmueller (2016,
p. 17[14]) put it, “innovation policy should focus much less on products, processes, firms, and R&D, but on
the achievement of system s wide transformations.” The emphasis on “failures” inherent to the first two
framings is replaced by more pro-active, purposive, and developmental approaches to innovation policies
(Asheim, Moodysson and Tödtling, 2011[5]; Mazzucato, 2016[6]). Here, the model of innovation emphasises
socio-technical systems and how processes of innovation and technological change are embedded in
society. Innovations are not seen as be neficial per se but are judged by their positive or negative
externalities for society.
In this Innovation Policy 3.0 period, new policy imperatives for policy become essential (Arnold et al.,
2023[18]), which are: (i) joint governance, referring to both effective coordination across different levels of
government but also stakeholder engagement. Joint governance is needed as reaching ambitious societal
transformation goals needs coordinated government e fforts from local to national levels and working with
diverse actors in industry, research, government, and society. Other imperatives are (ii) building more agile
and experimental policies , and (iii) setting policy targets to reach societal goals (directe d policy) .
Directionality is important as coordinated action to advance STI in specific areas, particularly in green
innovation, is otherwise not necessarily forthcoming. Furthermore, (iv) policy agility and experimentation
are also needed as t ransitions a re complex and uncertain processes that require adaptive and flexible
approaches. Policy experimentation allows for testing and learning from new ideas and interventions,
essential in a new context with impacts on policies’ success. This generation of poli cies owes its

To assess to what extent the new frame of “Innovation Policy 3.0” has been adopted, the paper examines
national STI strategies of 24 OECD countries.
Why strategies?
The analysis focuses on official STI policy strategies issued by national government entities to evaluate
whether innovation policies shifted to the Innovation Policy 3.0 paradigm. It follows Wauters (2019[19]) in
defining strategies as: “coherent action, guided by a longer -term sense of direction, to transform value -
generating processes, undertaken by (a network of) actors, backed by an argument.” In this definition: (1)
strategy is inextricably related to action; (2) action must be coherent with respect to a long-term objective;
(3) strategy is related to organising the foundational questions of what government should do (referred to
as “value-generating processes”) and not to managing the minutiae of regular activity, which Ruegg-Sturm
(2005[20]) distinguishes as mere “tactics”. Strategy is backed by an argument in favour of how and why it
occurs. The argument for “why” suggests that a strategy requires an articulation of why the issues it targets
are important. The argument for “how” relate to what Weiser et al. (2020[21]) call a “theory of change”, which
is an understanding of how the actions in a strategy cause the desired outcomes.
Consequently, STI strategy documents were selected for the assessment of Innovation Policy 3.0 as they
capture governments’ value-embedded, forward-looking, and high-level goals and plans. Moreover, to the
extent that po licy making is responsive to citizens, STI strategies should also partly reflect societal
endorsement of the directions of STI policy.
Database of STI strategy documents
A total of 313 documents were gathered, representing 24 OECD countries’ STI strategie s up until 2021,
thus covering the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic. Strategies were identified based on their contents
presenting forward-looking goals for future policy actions and priorities. To understand common trends,
the country selection represen ts the variety of OECD countries across Europe and beyond. In Europe, it
covers Northern Europe (Finland, Ireland, Sweden, United Kingdom), Western Europe (Austria, Belgium,
Germany, Luxembourg, Netherlands, Switzerland), Eastern Europe (Hungary, Slovakia) and Southern
Europe (Greece, Italy, Spain). It also covers North and South America (Canada, Chile, Costa Rica, United
States) as well as Asia-Pacific (Australia, Korea, Japan, New Zealand). Within those regions, the selection
was also based on including diverse countries, specifically regarding their sizes.
Following an initial ide ntification of documents based on the EC -OECD STIP Compass database as well
as other sources, policy makers from the respective governments validated the selection, identified
documents that were erroneously included, and provided additional relevant docum ents. The approved
document list was then used in the analysis.

Topic modelling and extraction of relevant text
For the analysis of the pre-proceed and text files, topic modelling is employed - a natural language
processing technique that uncovers “topics” within large amounts of textual data (Blei, 2012[22]; Hannigan
et al., 2019[23]). Topic modelling is an unsupervised machine learning tool, which means that it does not
require prior data inputs such as hand -labelled text passages but extracts the different topics solely from
the given textual data. This makes it useful for the analysis of our large and heterogenous corpus that
cannot be easily and consistently analysed through qualitative reading of each individual documen t.
Specifically, Structural Topic Modelling (STM) is employed, which is an extension of the often-used Latent
Dirichlet Allocation (LDA) algorithm. The basic idea behind LDA is to treat documents as a mixture of latent
topics and words as a distribution of these topics, enabling it to represent each document with a probability
distribution over topics and each topic with a probability distribution over words (Blei, 2012[22]). By iteratively
adjusting these probabilities to optimally capture the different topics in a text, LDA identifies the best-fitting
topics and their corresponding word distributions, generating an overview of documents’ semantic content.
STM extends LDA to incorporate non -textual information for each document to better capture semantic
nuances in the textual data and enhance the i nterpretability of topics. STM is particularly useful when
analysing data with inherent grouping, such as by country and year of publication of the different
documents in our database. Technically, the STM models the relationship between topic proportions and
covariates (country and year of publication) through a hierarchical Bayesian framework , and considers
how document-level covariates influence the se proportions in the process of identifying topics (Roberts,
Stewart and Tingley, 2019[24]).
The optimal number of topics for the corpus was determined based on two statistical measures of
exclusivity and semantic coherence ( Figure 1), and an assessment of the clarity of the topics obtained.
Exclusivity measures the extent to which the most frequent words in one topic are infrequent in other topics
as developed by Roberts et al. (2014[25]). As shown in Figure 1, exclusivity decreases with the number of
topics selected in the corpus. Semantic coherence assesses the extent to whic h the most frequent words
in a topic co-occur with one another, as proposed by Mimno et al. (2011[26]). The measure evolves reversely
with the number of topics , since frequent words co -occurrence decreases (increases) wi th more (fewer)
topics, though not fully linearly. A total of 35 topics were selected, since semantic coherence increases
substantially over those initial topics but less substantially afterwards while exclusivity does not decrease
substantially. The number was also suitable in providing clarity of distinct topics.

Robustness analysis of label accuracy across topic variations
In addition to the statistical analysis of exclusivity and semantic coherence for identifying the optimal
number of topics, a further robustness test was conducted to assess the accuracy of the results and the
selected baseline of 35 topics (see Figure 3). This test examined how the percentages varied across
different labels, considering both lower numbers of topics (25 and 30) and higher numbers (40 an d 45).
To assess the impact of specific countries, the core five identified countries —Canada, Germany, Japan,
Korea, and the United States—were each excluded separately from the sample during these checks. This
approach allowed for an analysis of how the i nclusion or exclusion of each country affected the overall
percentages allocated to the remaining 23 OECD countries.
Strategic variations in STI policy were also explored under three different scenarios: the top 10 countries
with the lowest R&D spending in the sample, the top 10 countries with the highest R&D spending, and
topics based solely on specific STI strategies.
These variations involved reviewing the percentage allocations for each topic across different topic
numbers and comparing them with the 35 -topic baseline. The goal was to identify any discrepancies or
variations, such as the underrepresentation of certain topics, and to assess how these variations impacted
the model.
The method for generating the labels remained consistent with the approach used to create the baseline
model of 35 topics, ensuring comparability across all tests and scenarios.
Methodological considerations
The main advantage of topic modelling lies in generating high-level summaries of documents’ contents by
identifying clusters of co-occurring words that represent key themes in the text. This saves time and avoids
the biases of human reading and structuring of textual information.
However, topic modelling also has its limitations. This includes:
• The need for extensive document preprocessing, such as the removal of stop words, and
programming skills.
• Reliance on human decisions that may introduce subjectivity, particularly in selecting the number
of topics and interpreting or labelling the results.
• Risk of overlooking important details and nuances in policies and programmes that may not appear
in the final topic model.
• Risk of an incomplete or unbalanced document corpus, despite validation efforts, where key texts
might be missing or irrelevant ones included, potentially limiting the representativeness and
significance of the policy debate captured.
Additionally, while topic modelling can process vast amounts of data and avoid the subjectivity of qualitative
reading, it lacks the interpretative capacities and k nowledge required to assess the rigorousness and
meanings of as well as the relations between information extracted from documents. Validation of all
analytical outputs by domain experts is thus necessary to ensure the accuracy and proper contextualisation
of outputs generated through computational tools.

This paper provides quantitative evidence of t he prominence that Innovation Policy 3.0 goals have on
governments’ STI policy agenda across 24 OECD member countries. Green transition goals dominate the
shifted STI policy agenda . Social and regional inclusion and digitali sation are also discussed as par t of
these priorities . Traditional innovation policy goals, which focus on firms, industry and growth and the
research sector and its linkages with industry, are also dealt with in strategies but at less than half the
prevalence. The strategies reviewed also incorporate elements related to new policy approaches required
for STI policy to reach those goals, including joint governance, agility, and directionality.
From a methodological standpoint, this paper demonstrates the feasibility of conducting extensive
assessments of qualitative policy data using the topic modelling approach , albeit with certain limitations.
The role of conceptual thinking and expert analysis remains paramount, as the nuanced nature of language
and policy discussions demand interpretation, setting it apart from the objectivity of quantitative data
analysis. AI-based techniques, including the use of generative AI technologies, open even richer avenues
going forward without negating, however, the importance of conceptual thinking and expert analysis.

Annex A. Pre-processing and cleaning steps for
the topic modelling
To prepare our database for the text analysis, we applied several pre-processing steps. The documents
were trimmed to remove page margins containing page numbers, repeated references to titles, chapters,
or the like. The files were then converted from PDF to TXT format, which removes all graphics and
formatting from the document, leaving only plain text. Where documents were not provided in English, they
were translated using Google Translate.
Taking the TXT file for each document, we conducted four basic text cleaning steps:
Lemmatise words. We reduce words to their “lemma” – their base form without any conjugation or
pluralisation and expand acronyms like “AI” (artificial intelligence) to their full written form.
Remove stop words. We remove prepositions, articles, and conjunctions that serve a grammatical function
but do not contain any semantic information. Common stop words include “the”, “and”, and “of”. Numbers,
misspelled words, and non-English words are all removed from the corpus.
• Remove punctuation. Punctuation marks are removed at the end of the process. Line breaks are
retained, as these prevent concepts from different paragraphs from being mixed incorrectly.
• Identify compounds. The lemmatised text is analysed for compound expressions. “Climate
change”, for example, is a compound word composed of “climate” and “change”, which takes on a
different meaning than the two words individually.
